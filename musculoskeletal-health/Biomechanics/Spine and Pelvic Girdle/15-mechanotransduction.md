# Mechanotransduction: How Tissue Hears Load

Mechanotransduction is the process by which cells convert physical forces into biochemical signals. A tendon cell cannot read the number printed on a dumbbell. A disc cell does not know whether the load came from a squat, a backpack, or prolonged sitting. A bone cell does not recognize an exercise by name. Cells respond to the local mechanical environment reaching them: deformation, compression, tension, shear, fluid flow, pressure, vibration, matrix stiffness, and the speed and duration with which those conditions change. ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19244270/), [2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9023345/), [19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41823544/), [3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20813920/), [4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21654799/))

Once the signal reaches a cell, mechanically sensitive structures can alter ion flow, enzyme activity, cytoskeletal tension, gene expression, protein synthesis, inflammatory signaling, matrix production, matrix breakdown, cell survival, and cell differentiation. Over repeated exposures, those cellular responses can contribute to visible tissue-level changes such as increased bone mass, altered tendon stiffness, skeletal-muscle hypertrophy, or, in experimental models, remodeling of an intervertebral disc’s extracellular matrix. ([2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9023345/), [3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20813920/), [4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21654799/), [6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32154945/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/), [19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41823544/))

A useful first rule is:

> **Tissue does not hear exercise names. It hears local mechanical signals.**

That distinction explains why the same external exercise can produce different tissue stimuli in two people. Anatomy, technique, range of motion, moment arms, speed, fatigue, muscle recruitment, previous adaptation, and tissue geometry all change how an external load becomes local stress and strain. It also explains why simply adding more weight does not guarantee a proportionally greater adaptive signal in every tissue. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32154945/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/), [30](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1758295/), [31](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17207676/), [32](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9796671/), [38](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33161870/))

## The practical translation chain

The simplest mechanotransduction map is:

**External task → internal tissue force → cellular deformation or fluid signal → mechanosensor activation → intracellular signaling → altered cell behavior → matrix or tissue remodeling.** ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19244270/), [2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9023345/), [3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20813920/), [4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21654799/), [6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/))

Every arrow matters.

A barbell is an **external load**. The forces experienced inside a lumbar disc, vertebral body, tendon, muscle, ligament, or facet joint are **internal loads**. Internal loading depends on more than bar weight. It also depends on posture, leverage, acceleration, muscle force, tissue cross-sectional area, spinal geometry, and how the load is distributed across neighboring structures. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41823544/), [30](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1758295/), [31](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17207676/))

The internal force then creates a local mechanical event. Collagen fibers may lengthen. A disc matrix may compress and lose fluid. Fluid may flow through bone’s microscopic canalicular network. A cell membrane may deform. The extracellular matrix beneath a cell may become stiffer or softer. Those mechanical events are closer to what the cell can actually detect. ([2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9023345/), [6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [11](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38529481/), [4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21654799/))

Detection then starts signaling rather than instant adaptation. Calcium may enter through an ion channel. A focal-adhesion protein may change shape. Cytoskeletal tension may rise. A transcriptional regulator may move into the nucleus. The cell may increase or decrease production of collagen, proteoglycans, enzymes, cytokines, or regulatory proteins. ([3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20813920/), [4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21654799/), [6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [26](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19179532/), [28](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29107331/)) Meaningful structural adaptation generally develops over time, often through repeated signaling, and depends on sufficient resources and recovery. ([12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32154945/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/), [18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39527327/)) [2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9023345/)

The chain is not guaranteed to end in positive adaptation. The same broad signaling machinery can support maintenance, repair, remodeling, inflammation, matrix breakdown, senescence, or cell death depending on tissue condition and loading dose. Mechanotransduction means that tissue responds to force; it does not mean every response is beneficial. ([7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42528914/), [8](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35606793/), [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36583692/))

## What tissue actually senses

### Load

**Load** is the broadest term. It can mean an external weight, an applied force, or the total mechanical demand placed on a structure. It is useful in programming but too vague to describe the cellular signal by itself. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41823544/))

A 100-kg bar does not create one universal biological dose. The internal force reaching a tendon or spinal segment depends on the exercise, joint angle, acceleration, moment arm, muscular strategy, and individual anatomy. External load is therefore an input to the mechanical problem, not a complete description of what the tissue experiences. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [30](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1758295/), [38](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33161870/))

### Stress

**Stress** means force distributed over an area. Two tissues can experience the same total force but different stress because one has a smaller load-bearing cross-sectional area. ([16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/))

This distinction matters clinically and athletically. A larger tendon, vertebral endplate, or bone cross-section may distribute force differently from a smaller one. Local material and structural properties also vary: cadaveric studies have found regional differences in vertebral-endplate stiffness and failure load, and relationships between vertebral compressive strength, bone density, and endplate area. ([16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/), [29](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11317111/), [37](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23905925/))

### Strain

**Strain** means relative deformation: how much a tissue changes length or shape compared with its starting dimensions. Tendon training research often estimates longitudinal strain, while disc research may examine compressive, tensile, shear, or regional matrix deformation. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/))

Strain is not automatically damage. Living tissues deform whenever they carry force. A tendon lengthens, a disc compresses, a bone bends by a small amount, and a muscle fiber changes shape. Damage becomes more likely when the magnitude, rate, number of repetitions, or accumulated exposure exceeds what the tissue can currently tolerate - not merely because deformation occurred. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/), [37](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23905925/))

### Strain rate

**Strain rate** describes how quickly deformation develops. The same peak deformation applied slowly and rapidly may produce different cellular signals. ([14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7573420/))

Classic rat-bone experiments held peak strain relatively constant while changing the rate at which it was applied. Higher strain rates produced more bone formation, supporting the idea that deformation speed and associated fluid flow can matter independently of peak strain. Because this was an animal bone model, its exact loading rates should not be converted directly into a human jump, squat, or rehabilitation prescription. ([14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7573420/))

### Compression, tension, and shear

**Compression** pushes tissue together.
**Tension** pulls it apart.
**Shear** acts parallel to a surface and encourages adjacent regions to slide relative to one another. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/))

Most tissues experience combinations rather than one pure mode. The annulus fibrosus can experience tension, compression, shear, and torsion during the same movement. A tendon carries longitudinal tension but also develops transverse compression and internal shear around curved paths or insertion sites. Bone bends through simultaneous compression on one side and tension on the other. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41823544/))

### Fluid flow and hydrostatic pressure

Some cells are influenced less by large visible tissue motion than by the microscopic fluid movement produced when the tissue deforms. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [11](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38529481/))

Bone loading can drive fluid through the lacunar-canalicular network surrounding osteocytes. Disc compression changes hydrostatic and osmotic pressure and redistributes fluid through the nucleus, annulus, and endplate regions. Cartilage compression moves interstitial fluid through a proteoglycan-rich matrix. These fluid-related signals can deform cell membranes, alter ion gradients, and activate mechanosensitive structures. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [11](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38529481/), [14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7573420/))

### Osmotic pressure

Fluid movement also changes the concentration of dissolved ions and molecules around cells. This is especially important in proteoglycan-rich tissues such as the nucleus pulposus and articular cartilage. Disc cells may respond not only to mechanical compression but also to the osmotic changes created as water leaves or returns to the matrix. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/))

### Matrix stiffness

Cells can respond to the stiffness of the material to which they are attached. A stiffer extracellular matrix changes how force is transferred through adhesion sites and the cytoskeleton. Foundational work identified YAP and TAZ as nuclear relays whose activity and location can change in response to extracellular-matrix stiffness, cell geometry, and cytoskeletal tension. ([4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21654799/))

This creates an important feedback loop. Loading changes the matrix. The changed matrix alters what future loading feels like to the cell. The cell then modifies the matrix again. In disc degeneration, for example, changes in hydration, proteoglycan content, collagen structure, acidity, and stiffness can alter the mechanical cues received by surviving cells, potentially reinforcing further pathological signaling. ([7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42528914/), [21](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25827971/))

## The extracellular matrix: microphone and message

The extracellular matrix, or **ECM**, is the collagen-, proteoglycan-, glycoprotein-, and water-rich environment surrounding cells. It is not passive packing material. It determines how forces reach cells, how much deformation occurs locally, and how molecules move through a tissue. ([2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9023345/), [19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41823544/), [4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21654799/), [6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/))

The ECM acts like a mechanical filter. Aligned tendon collagen transmits force differently from the hydrated proteoglycan-rich nucleus pulposus. Mineralized bone distributes and amplifies signals differently from cartilage. Scarred, degenerated, aged, or previously adapted tissue may transmit the same external force differently from younger or less altered tissue. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42528914/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/), [11](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38529481/), [24](https://doi.org/10.1002/jcp.24102))

The matrix is also an output. Cells respond to load by changing production and breakdown of collagen, proteoglycans, cross-links, enzymes, and regulatory proteins. Those alterations change tissue stiffness and geometry, which changes the next mechanical signal. The matrix therefore both delivers the message and records part of the tissue’s adaptation history. ([7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42528914/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/))

This is why mechanotransduction cannot be understood by studying cells on rigid plastic alone. Cells placed in culture may receive a matrix environment very different from the living disc, tendon, bone, or ligament they came from. Modern organ-culture and three-dimensional models attempt to preserve more realistic cell-matrix relationships, but they still cannot reproduce the full vascular, neural, immune, and whole-body environment of a living athlete. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36583692/))

## How cells detect force

There is no single master mechanosensor. Cells use overlapping systems, many of which interact. ([7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42528914/), [19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41823544/))

### Integrins and focal adhesions

**Integrins** are transmembrane proteins that connect extracellular-matrix molecules outside the cell to cytoskeletal structures inside it. Focal adhesions are larger protein complexes assembled around these connections, involving proteins such as talin, vinculin, kindlin, and focal-adhesion kinase. Force can alter the behavior of this network; in single-molecule experiments, stretching talin increased vinculin binding. ([2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9023345/), [7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42528914/), [26](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19179532/))

This creates a mechanical route from matrix to cytoskeleton. Force applied outside the cell can alter internal tension, enzyme activity, and gene regulation without requiring tissue injury. The network can extend through cytoskeletal filaments toward the nuclear envelope, allowing mechanical information to influence the nucleus both through biochemical cascades and direct physical coupling. ([2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9023345/), [4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21654799/), [27](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23062334/), [28](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29107331/))

Integrin signaling is especially relevant to tissues whose cells sit within structured matrices. Disc, tendon, cartilage, bone, and muscle cells all interact with their surroundings through matrix-adhesion systems, although the specific integrins, matrix ligands, and downstream responses differ by tissue and cellular state. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42528914/), [15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38795012/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/), [19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41823544/))

### Mechanically activated ion channels

Mechanosensitive ion channels can change their open or closed state directly or indirectly in response to deformation of the membrane or surrounding structures. Opening a channel can allow calcium or other ions to enter the cell, creating a rapid signal that then affects enzymes, gene expression, metabolism, and matrix turnover. ([3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20813920/), [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36583692/))

In 2010, Coste and colleagues identified Piezo1 and Piezo2 as essential components of mechanically activated cation channels. The finding did not establish that Piezo channels explain all mechanotransduction, but it supplied a molecular mechanism through which membrane deformation can produce immediate electrical and biochemical change. ([3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20813920/))

Piezo1 has since been investigated in bone, tendon, cartilage, muscle, and intervertebral-disc cells. Its effect is not simply “good when activated.” In tendon models, appropriate Piezo1 signaling has been linked with anabolic transcriptional programs involving the tendon regulators MKX and SCX. In high-intensity compression models of nucleus-pulposus cells, greater Piezo1 expression has been associated with inflammatory, senescence-related, and apoptotic changes and reduced matrix synthesis. Tissue, dose, duration, and cellular context change the meaning of the signal. ([8](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35606793/), [15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38795012/))

Transient receptor potential channels provide another example. In a mouse intervertebral-disc organ-culture model, TRPV4 activation influenced calcium signaling, inflammatory mediators, matrix accumulation, hydration, and tissue stiffness. Inhibiting TRPV4 during sustained compression reduced some inflammatory and degenerative changes but did not prevent all annular collagen disorganization. That distinction illustrates why blocking one signaling pathway cannot necessarily prevent all consequences of sustained loading. ([9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36583692/))

### Primary cilia

A **primary cilium** is a small membrane-bound projection extending from many cells into the surrounding environment. It can concentrate receptors and signaling machinery and may deform or alter its behavior in response to fluid flow and matrix mechanics. ([5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40738397/))

A 2025 review described primary cilia as mechanosensory structures involved across bone, muscle, cartilage, tendon, and intervertebral-disc homeostasis. Their assembly, disassembly, and downstream signaling can change with mechanical conditions, although the precise pathways remain incompletely characterized and may differ substantially among tissues. ([5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40738397/))

Primary cilia should not be presented as tiny levers that work identically in every cell. Even the exact way cilia detect force, including whether force directly elicits ciliary calcium signaling, remains debated. Their importance is better understood as part of a wider sensory network that combines mechanical, chemical, and developmental signaling. ([5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40738397/), [36](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27007841/))

### The cytoskeleton

The cytoskeleton includes actin filaments, intermediate filaments, microtubules, and associated proteins. It gives the cell shape, transmits internal tension, organizes organelles, and links membrane adhesions with the nucleus. ([2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9023345/), [27](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23062334/))

When the extracellular matrix stretches or a membrane channel opens, cytoskeletal tension can change. That can alter cell shape, focal-adhesion assembly, transport of signaling molecules, and nuclear deformation. Cytoskeletal organization also changes how sensitive the cell is to later mechanical input. ([2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9023345/), [4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21654799/))

### The nucleus and YAP/TAZ

Mechanical signaling does not stop at the cell membrane. Cytoskeletal structures connect to the nuclear envelope through protein complexes, providing a physical route from the matrix to the nucleus. In experimental cell systems, mechanical conditions can alter nuclear shape, chromatin organization, and the movement of transcriptional regulators through nuclear pores. ([2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9023345/), [27](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23062334/), [28](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29107331/))

YAP and TAZ are among the best-known mechanosensitive transcriptional regulators. Dupont and colleagues found that their nuclear localization and activity changed with matrix stiffness and cell geometry and that they helped mediate stiffness-dependent differentiation in mesenchymal stem cells. ([4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21654799/))

YAP/TAZ activity is not inherently anabolic or pathological. Its effect depends on cell type, signal duration, interaction with other pathways, and tissue state. Current disc research, for example, implicates YAP/TAZ in responses to altered matrix stiffness and abnormal mechanosensing, but most of this evidence comes from cell, animal, or engineered-tissue models rather than human exercise trials. ([7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42528914/))

## There is no master mechanosensor

The clean educational diagram - force activates Piezo1, which activates one pathway, which builds tissue - is rarely how living systems behave.

A single cell may use ion channels, integrins, focal adhesions, primary cilia, the cytoskeleton, cell-cell junctions, G-protein-coupled receptors, and the nucleus simultaneously. These systems interact with growth factors, inflammatory cytokines, oxygen availability, acidity, hormones, energy status, age, and genetic background. ([2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9023345/), [7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42528914/), [19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41823544/), [3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20813920/), [5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40738397/), [26](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19179532/), [28](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29107331/))

This overlap provides a plausible basis for biological redundancy. If tissue survival depended on one molecular switch, damage to that switch could eliminate all loading responses. Overlapping sensors may allow cells to respond to several aspects of their mechanical environment and preserve some responsiveness when one pathway is impaired; the extent of that compensation depends on the system. ([7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42528914/), [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36583692/), [19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41823544/))

It also makes clinical translation difficult. A drug or exercise that alters one signaling molecule may leave other pathways active. A biomarker increasing after exercise does not prove that it was necessary for the eventual adaptation. Blocking one pathway in a mouse, cell culture, or organ explant may reveal mechanism without telling us whether manipulating that pathway is safe or useful in a person. ([7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42528914/), [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36583692/))

## The dose makes the signal

Mechanical loading is not biologically classified as simply present or absent. The response depends on the loading dose. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32154945/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/))

Important variables include:

- Magnitude of stress or strain.

- Rate at which it develops.

- Direction of loading.

- Number of repetitions or cycles.

- Duration of each loading event.

- Time under sustained load.

- Frequency of sessions.

- Rest between cycles, sets, or sessions.

- Movement range and tissue position.

- Previous training and tissue condition.

- Age, health, nutrition, sleep, and recovery. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32154945/), [13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12162508/), [14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7573420/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/), [18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39527327/))

Human tendon and bone studies support the idea that magnitude, strain, strain rate, and repeated exposure matter, but they do not produce one formula that applies across tissues. A tendon-loading program that raises Achilles stiffness is not automatically the optimal dose for a lumbar disc, spinal ligament, vertebral endplate, or articular cartilage. ([12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32154945/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/))

### Magnitude is important, but not sufficient

A stimulus must usually be large enough to differ meaningfully from the tissue’s accustomed mechanical environment. Yet greater magnitude does not guarantee a proportionally better response. At some point, increasing stress or strain may create diminishing returns, excessive fatigue, matrix disruption, inflammation, or failure. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [8](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35606793/), [12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32154945/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/), [37](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23905925/))

In a 12-month randomized mechanistic trial of healthy adult women, estimated loading dose - combining strain magnitude, rate, and number of loading bouts - was related to bone changes at the distal radius. Those loading variables explained only a small portion of the variability in adaptation, showing that the measured dose variables did not fully predict individual responses. ([12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32154945/))

### Rate matters differently across tissues

Rapid deformation can generate greater fluid-flow signals in bone. Tendons, however, are viscoelastic: loading rate changes how much they deform and how much time cells experience strain. Disc cells experience another combination of pressure, fluid movement, and matrix deformation. No universal rule such as “faster is always more anabolic” survives across all these tissues. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7573420/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/))

### Static and dynamic loading are not simply bad and good

Dynamic loading often creates changing strain, pressure, and fluid flow and can provide a strong mechanotransductive signal. Sustained static loading can lead to creep, fluid loss, and a different cellular environment. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36583692/))

That does not mean dynamic loading is always beneficial or static loading always harmful. A high-magnitude dynamic impact can exceed tissue tolerance. A controlled isometric contraction can provide an effective tendon stimulus. Low-to-moderate static compression can produce anabolic responses in some nucleus-pulposus cell models, while sustained or high-magnitude compression can become catabolic. The effect depends on tissue, magnitude, duration, and starting condition. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36583692/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/))

### Rest is part of the mechanical dose

Cells can become temporarily less responsive to repeated identical stimulation. In a rat-bone model, separating loading into shorter bouts with recovery periods produced greater long-term structural and strength adaptation than delivering the same number of cycles in one uninterrupted session. ([13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12162508/))

That animal result supports the concept of mechanosensory recovery, but it does not prove that humans need a precise three-hour gap, a fixed number of jumps, or multiple daily sessions. Human bone adaptation is influenced by compliance, age, endocrine status, anatomy, baseline activity, and the actual strain reaching the target site. ([12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32154945/), [13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12162508/))

## The intervertebral disc: several tissues hearing different signals

The intervertebral disc is a useful demonstration of why “load” cannot be treated as one homogeneous input.

The nucleus pulposus is a hydrated, proteoglycan-rich central tissue. The annulus fibrosus is a collagen-rich lamellar structure. The cartilaginous endplates sit at the vertebral interfaces. Cells in these regions live in different matrices and experience different combinations of compression, tension, shear, hydrostatic pressure, osmotic change, and fluid flow. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42528914/))

A 2018 review concluded that disc-cell responses depend heavily on loading type, magnitude, duration, and anatomical origin. In the reviewed experimental literature, low-to-moderate static compression, osmotic pressure, or hydrostatic pressure could support anabolic nucleus-pulposus responses, while higher magnitudes tended to increase protease activity and other catabolic behavior. Annulus cells showed their own frequency- and magnitude-dependent responses because they experience a different mechanical environment. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/))

This is not a clinical threshold. Laboratory studies use isolated cells, animal tissues, organ culture, and loading devices that do not reproduce every feature of a living human spine. “Low,” “moderate,” and “high” are specific to the experimental model and cannot be translated directly into kilograms on a barbell or hours in a chair. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42528914/))

### Piezo1 and excessive compression

A 2022 study combined human cervical-disc observations with high-intensity compression of cultured nucleus-pulposus cells. Greater Piezo1 expression was associated with degenerated tissue, while high-intensity compression in the laboratory increased inflammatory and senescence-related signaling, reduced matrix synthesis, and altered autophagy and apoptosis. Treatment with GsMTx4, a blocker of mechanically activated channels that includes Piezo1 among its targets, partly reduced those effects. Because GsMTx4 is not selective for Piezo1, this result does not by itself establish a Piezo1-specific mechanism. ([8](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35606793/), [22](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature02743))

That study supports a plausible pathway through which excessive mechanical conditions may contribute to cellular dysfunction. It does not show that ordinary lifting activates the same response, that Piezo1 causes a person’s back pain, or that pharmacologically inhibiting Piezo1 is a proven treatment for disc degeneration. ([8](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35606793/), [22](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature02743))

### TRPV4 and mixed biological effects

TRPV4 research shows why mechanotransduction pathways resist simple good-versus-bad labels. In mouse disc organ culture, activating TRPV4 increased calcium signaling and later inflammatory cytokine production, but it also increased glycosaminoglycan accumulation, hydration, and tissue stiffness. During sustained compression, blocking the channel reduced some inflammatory changes while failing to prevent all annular collagen disorganization. ([9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36583692/))

The same pathway can therefore participate in matrix synthesis, inflammation, and adaptation depending on timing and context. A cellular signal is not automatically equivalent to a clinical benefit or harm. ([9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36583692/))

### The current spine-specific evidence gap

A corrected 2026 review identified Piezo1, transient-receptor-potential channels, integrin-focal-adhesion complexes, acid-sensing channels, primary cilia, and cytoskeletal-nuclear pathways as major candidates in disc mechanotransduction. It also emphasized that the evidence remains concentrated in nucleus-pulposus models, with fewer matched studies of the annulus, cartilage endplate, and vertebral interface. ([7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42528914/))

The translational gap is larger. Molecular disc studies show what may happen in cells or experimental tissues. Human exercise studies usually measure MRI signal, disc height, or degeneration grades and rarely identify the molecular pathway responsible. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42528914/), [10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41840183/))

The 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis of physical loading and disc health included 45 reports from 39 studies and 4,152 participants. Upright bipedal loading, mostly running, was associated with more favorable continuous disc-health measures than control groups not participating in the same loading, but the certainty was rated very low and the evidence was mainly observational. No other loading subgroup showed a clear association with better or worse continuous disc-health measures. However, pooled physical loading was associated with greater odds of degeneration defined by reduced MRI signal intensity, a categorical outcome (odds ratio 2.80, 95% confidence interval 1.53 to 5.11; five cross-sectional studies; low-certainty evidence). These observational findings do not establish causation. ([10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41840183/))

MRI-defined disc degeneration is also common in people without symptoms. A systematic review of 3,110 asymptomatic individuals estimated its prevalence at 37% at age 20 and 96% at age 80. These age-specific estimates show why imaging findings require clinical context: a structural difference does not by itself establish pain, disability, or clinical benefit, and the presence of asymptomatic findings does not make every imaging change irrelevant. ([25](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25430861/))

The evidence therefore supports neither “loading wears discs out” nor “all loading rebuilds discs.” Disc adaptation depends on load type, matrix health, dose, age, genetics, recovery, and the mechanical-biological feedback already operating in the tissue. ([7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42528914/), [10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41840183/), [21](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25827971/))

## Bone: osteocytes as load-distribution sentinels

Bone is mineralized and stiff, but it is not biologically inert. Osteocytes are embedded throughout the mineralized matrix and connected through a microscopic lacunar-canalicular network. Mechanical deformation and associated fluid flow can be translated into signaling that regulates osteoblasts, osteoclasts, and bone-lining cells. ([11](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38529481/), [14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7573420/))

Loading can reduce local expression of sclerostin, permitting greater Wnt-related osteoblast differentiation and matrix deposition. It can also influence RANKL-related regulation of osteoclast development and resorption. The result can be spatially targeted bone modeling and remodeling: bone can be added or removed according to the local mechanical and biological environment rather than changing uniformly throughout the skeleton. ([11](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38529481/))

This helps explain why bone adaptation is site-specific. Weight-bearing activity does not deliver the same local stimulus as an upper-limb compression task. A squat, a jump, and forearm loading differ in their mechanical demands. General physical activity therefore should not be assumed to provide an equivalent osteogenic signal at every skeletal site. ([11](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38529481/), [12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32154945/), [30](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1758295/), [38](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33161870/))

The 12-month human distal-radius trial provides evidence linking estimated local loading to adaptation. Model-estimated strain magnitude, strain rate, and number of completed bouts were associated with bone adaptation in healthy adult women, but together explained only a small amount of the variation in outcomes. Compliance and the loading actually received mattered, and individual responses remained diverse. ([12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32154945/))

Animal studies suggest that bone cells lose responsiveness rapidly during repeated identical cycles and can regain sensitivity after rest. This supports the idea that a small number of meaningful loading events may be more valuable than endlessly repeating a weak signal. But exact cycle counts and recovery intervals from rat studies should not be presented as established human exercise prescriptions. ([13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12162508/), [14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7573420/))

The bone lesson is not simply “lift heavier.” It is that bone responds to local strain, strain rate, direction, novelty, repetition, and recovery. The mechanical stimulus must also be appropriate for the person’s age, health, fracture risk, experience, and target skeletal region. ([11](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38529481/), [12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32154945/), [13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12162508/), [14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7573420/))

## Tendon: force transmission changes the transmitter

Tendons transmit muscle force to bone. Their cells, or tenocytes, are embedded among aligned collagen fibrils and experience longitudinal strain, matrix shear, fluid movement, and regional compression when the tendon is loaded. ([15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38795012/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/))

Current mechanistic research implicates integrins, cytoskeletal structures, primary cilia, ion channels, and transcriptional regulators. A 2024 review highlighted a proposed PIEZO1-MKX/SCX pathway through which appropriate mechanical stress may support anabolic tendon-cell behavior. The authors also emphasized that tendon mechanotransduction is only partially understood and that the connection between molecular changes, tissue properties, and performance remains incomplete. ([15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38795012/))

Human intervention evidence is stronger for tissue-level adaptation than for the precise molecular pathway. A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis included 61 studies and 763 participants, mainly examining the Achilles and patellar tendons. Pooled within-group pre/post analyses showed moderate increases in tendon stiffness, large increases in modulus, and small increases in cross-sectional area. Control-group data were not extracted for these pooled estimates. Changes in material properties were the main statistical moderator of increased stiffness. ([16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/))

Protocols estimated to produce higher local tendon strain generated larger changes in stiffness and modulus than lower-strain protocols. Yet only a small number of studies directly measured strain, most samples were small, and most participants were untrained or recreationally active. Only three studies involved well-trained athletes, limiting transfer to experienced strength and power populations. ([16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/))

The review also found no clear superiority of eccentric, concentric-eccentric, or isometric contraction when studies were directly matched, although this does not establish equivalence. Contraction mode may matter mainly as a practical way to deliver sufficient force, strain, duration, and tolerability; these findings do not establish a unique tendon-building pathway for any muscle action. ([16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/))

### Tendon findings do not automatically apply to ligaments

Ligaments also contain collagen-rich extracellular matrix and mechanosensitive fibroblasts, so shared mechanisms are plausible. The Achilles- and patellar-tendon intervention evidence summarized here does not establish an optimal human loading dose for spinal or pelvic ligaments. ([16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/), [19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41823544/))

It is therefore not defensible to assume that a tendon protocol - such as a particular estimated strain percentage, contraction duration, or set structure - is automatically optimal for an anterior cruciate ligament, sacroiliac ligament, spinal ligament, or healing annular tissue. The broad mechanobiology may overlap while geometry, vascularity, habitual loading, cellular population, and clinical constraints differ substantially. ([19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41823544/))

The gap should remain visible: **human ligament mechanotransduction dosing cannot be established by transferring tendon protocols unchanged.** ([16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/), [19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41823544/))

## Skeletal muscle: tension starts a network, not one switch

Mechanical overload can lead to skeletal-muscle hypertrophy, but the pathway from force to growth is not fully resolved. ([17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/))

Current models include mechanically influenced activation of mTORC1-related signaling, altered protein synthesis, ribosome biogenesis, cytoskeletal and costameric signaling, satellite-cell activity, changes in gene transcription, and remodeling of the extracellular matrix. Different mechanisms may dominate at different stages, and several proposed pathways remain contested. ([17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/))

The muscle cell does not need to be torn before it can sense tension. Mechanotransduction begins when mechanical forces alter membrane, cytoskeletal, adhesion, or sarcomeric structures. Muscle damage may occur during unfamiliar or excessive exercise, particularly with substantial active lengthening, but damage and soreness are not required definitions of an effective hypertrophic stimulus. ([17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/), [34](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27219125/))

This corrects the familiar “exercise tears the muscle, then it heals bigger” story. Remodeling can involve repair processes, but adaptation is not simply scar formation after repeated injury. The training signal includes force production, fiber recruitment, metabolic environment, protein turnover, and cumulative exposure. ([17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/), [34](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27219125/))

Muscle also adapts as part of a muscle-tendon-bone system. Increasing muscle force changes tendon and bone loading, but these tissues do not necessarily remodel by the same amount or on the same schedule. A program can be an adequate muscle stimulus while providing too little, too much, or simply different loading for the associated tendon, bone, cartilage, or disc. ([16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/), [18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39527327/))

## Cartilage and fascia: real mechanosensitivity, thinner clinical translation

Articular cartilage cells respond to compression, hydrostatic pressure, fluid flow, osmotic change, matrix stiffness, and shear. Depending on the dose and tissue state, loading can support matrix homeostasis or contribute to inflammatory and catabolic behavior. Integrins, ion channels, primary cilia, cytoskeletal structures, and nuclear regulators are all under investigation. ([5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40738397/), [19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41823544/))

Fascial fibroblasts are also capable of sensing matrix tension and stiffness. ([24](https://doi.org/10.1002/jcp.24102)) But this cellular evidence does not establish that a particular stretch, manual technique, or movement remodels fascia through a named molecular pathway in humans. Cellular mechanosensitivity does not validate every fascia-specific treatment claim. ([24](https://doi.org/10.1002/jcp.24102))

For both cartilage and fascia, the existence of mechanotransduction is supported by experimental evidence. That evidence alone does not establish the optimal human treatment dose, the clinical importance of individual pathways, or the superiority of one intervention over another. ([5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40738397/), [19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41823544/), [24](https://doi.org/10.1002/jcp.24102))

## Underloading is also a mechanical signal

Mechanotransduction is not activated only by exercise. A reduction in normal loading changes cell behavior too. ([11](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38529481/), [15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38795012/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/))

Bone cells alter sclerostin- and RANKL-related signaling when mechanical stimulation decreases, shifting the balance toward less formation and potentially more resorption. Skeletal muscle reduces protein synthesis and force capacity during disuse. Tendons can lose mechanical quality when habitual tension falls. Disc and cartilage cells also experience altered transport, pressure, and matrix signaling when the normal loading-unloading cycle changes. ([11](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38529481/), [15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38795012/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/), [6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41823544/))

This makes complete rest a biological intervention rather than the absence of one. Temporary unloading may be part of an injury-specific or postoperative plan. Prolonged unloading can also reduce some tissue capacities, so its duration needs to be considered alongside the demands of return to activity. ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19244270/), [11](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38529481/), [15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38795012/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/), [18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39527327/))

The practical choice is rarely between load and no load forever. It is between different magnitudes, directions, frequencies, and progressions of load.

## Adaptation and damage are not opposite switches

Mechanical loading can produce several processes at once:

- Reversible deformation.

- Temporary fluid redistribution.

- Cellular signaling.

- Protein synthesis and degradation.

- Matrix remodeling.

- Fatigue.

- Microscopic damage.

- Inflammation.

- Repair.

- Structural adaptation. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/), [33](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12952631/), [37](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23905925/))

The presence of one does not prove the presence of every other. A disc can lose height during the day without being injured. A tendon can change its elongation response across repeated contractions. A muscle can signal for growth without becoming markedly sore. A bone can deform microscopically without fracturing. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7573420/), [33](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12952631/), [34](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27219125/))

Likewise, an adaptive signal does not guarantee that the full tissue response will be positive. A loading session may stimulate protein synthesis while also producing fatigue or matrix disruption. The final result depends on the balance among stimulus, damage, resources, and recovery across repeated exposures. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/))

The phrase **productive loading** is therefore more useful than “good stress.” It means the total loading process produces enough stimulus to build or retain capacity without repeatedly overwhelming the tissue’s current ability to recover.

## An acute signal is not a chronic adaptation

Mechanotransduction can begin within seconds. Ion channels open rapidly. Calcium levels change. Kinases become active. Transcriptional regulators move. Gene expression may change within hours. ([3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20813920/), [27](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23062334/))

Structural adaptation takes longer. New proteins have to be synthesized, incorporated, organized, cross-linked, and maintained. Cells may need to proliferate or differentiate. Old matrix may be removed. A rise in one signaling molecule after one set does not establish lasting changes in bone geometry, tendon modulus, muscle size, or disc composition. ([12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32154945/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/))

This creates a common research trap. An exercise may increase a molecular marker associated with collagen synthesis or hypertrophy. That does not establish that the intervention will improve long-term tissue strength, pain, function, or injury risk. ([12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32154945/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/), [34](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27219125/))

The reverse is also true. Failure to detect one expected acute marker does not prove that no adaptation will occur. Cells use overlapping pathways, measurement timing matters, and structural change may result from mechanisms that were not measured. ([7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42528914/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/))

A credible training claim therefore needs evidence at the level of the claimed outcome. Molecular signaling can support a mechanism claim. Ultrasound or MRI can support claims about the tissue features actually measured. Mechanical testing supports claims about the specific properties measured, such as stiffness or failure strength. ([10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41840183/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/), [29](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11317111/)) Treatment-effect claims require human outcome evidence capable of supporting causal inference, ideally well-conducted randomized trials when feasible. Trial methods matter: empirical research has linked inadequate allocation concealment and lack of blinding with larger estimated treatment effects. ([23](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7823387/))

## Mechanotherapy: using load as treatment

Khan and Scott used the term **mechanotherapy** for the therapeutic use of mechanical loading to stimulate tissue repair and remodeling. The concept helped explain why exercise is more than generic movement: a prescribed load can serve as a biological input to tendon, muscle, cartilage, and bone. ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19244270/))

The concept is powerful, but it can be oversold. Mechanotherapy does not mean that clinicians can select one exercise, name one molecular pathway, and predict the exact tissue response.

A loading prescription still has to answer ordinary clinical questions:

- What tissue or capacity is being targeted?

- What force direction reaches it?

- What magnitude and range are currently tolerated?

- What is the intended adaptation?

- How will the response be monitored?

- What competing tissues or systems are also being stressed?

- What progression is appropriate?

- Are symptoms or red flags suggesting a different problem?

A 2025 narrative review emphasized that tissues and physiological systems recover on different time courses and that one session can load one structure appropriately while underloading or overloading another. The article is best used as a monitoring framework rather than proof of fixed recovery timelines because it was not a controlled dosing trial. One author operates a performance consultancy, which is a disclosure rather than a measure of the evidence’s validity. ([18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39527327/))

The strongest clinical translation remains modest:

> **Apply enough relevant load to stimulate the target capacity, then adjust according to the person’s response over time.**

## What this means for spinal training

The spine is not one tissue. A deadlift can simultaneously load:

- Vertebral bone.

- Intervertebral discs.

- Facet joints.

- Spinal ligaments.

- Thoracolumbar fascia.

- Paraspinal muscles.

- Abdominal muscles.

- Hip muscles and tendons.

- Neural and cardiovascular systems. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/), [18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39527327/), [30](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1758295/), [31](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17207676/))

Those tissues do not experience identical forces or require identical adaptive doses. A program designed primarily for erector-spinae strength may not provide the same stimulus to bone, disc, annulus, tendon, or ligament. Nor does increasing the muscular challenge prove that every passive spinal tissue received a better biological dose. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32154945/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/), [18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39527327/))

Range of motion changes where deformation occurs. Speed changes strain rate. During repeated lifting, muscle fatigue can occur alongside changes in posture and the distribution of estimated spinal compression and bending loads. Load position changes external moment arms. Bracing changes abdominal recruitment and can alter estimated spinal stability and compression. Exercise selection therefore changes the mechanical environment through several pathways at once. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [30](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1758295/), [31](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17207676/), [32](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9796671/), [38](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33161870/))

This is why “the bar weighed the same” does not mean “the tissue stimulus was the same.”

## The athlete translation

### Specificity begins below the exercise name

An athlete adapts to the mechanical signal generated by the task, not to the label attached to it. ([12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32154945/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/))

Two squats with the same bar weight may differ in:

- Depth.

- Torso angle.

- Acceleration.

- Stance.

- Bar position.

- Repetition duration.

- Bracing.

- Fatigue.

- Spinal and hip contribution.

Those differences can change joint moments and internal loading, which in turn influence local stress, strain, pressure, and fluid movement. They may alter which tissues receive the dominant stimulus even when the external training log looks identical; these local cellular signals are generally inferred rather than measured directly during the exercise. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [30](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1758295/), [31](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17207676/), [32](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9796671/), [38](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33161870/))

### Novelty amplifies some signals - but also raises uncertainty

A tissue accustomed to one loading pattern may respond less strongly to repeated identical exposure; reduced responsiveness across repeated cycles has been demonstrated in animal bone models. Changing direction, rate, range, or magnitude changes the mechanical exposure, but does not guarantee a more useful adaptive signal. ([12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32154945/), [13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12162508/), [14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7573420/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/))

Novelty also warrants a fresh assessment of tolerance. Capacity demonstrated in one habitual task is not a direct test of capacity in another. Introducing a new hinge depth, sprint volume, rotational task, or impact surface may change the mechanical exposure more than the athlete expects from the apparent workload. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39527327/), [30](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1758295/), [38](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33161870/))

The practical answer is not constant exercise variation. It is controlled novelty.

### Capacity is tissue-specific

An athlete’s muscle strength is not a direct measure of the repeated-load capacity of the associated tendon, bone, disc, or ligament. That distinction deserves attention after time away from sport, rapid strength gains, immobilization, or a return from injury. ([16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/), [18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39527327/))

That does not mean passive tissues are inherently slow or fragile. It means adaptation should be judged by the target tissue and task rather than by muscular performance alone. Human tendon intervention research, for example, shows measurable adaptation but also limited data in highly trained athletes and uncertainty beyond the typical study duration. ([16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/))

### More soreness does not mean more mechanotransduction

Soreness can accompany unfamiliar loading and local tissue responses, but it does not quantify the adaptive response. In a small longitudinal resistance-training study, soreness and other damage indicators were greatest early in training and declined with repeated exposure; soreness was not significantly correlated with the measured hypertrophic outcomes. It should not be treated as a measure of collagen synthesis, bone formation, tendon stiffness change, or disc adaptation. ([17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/), [34](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27219125/))

A session can be productive without substantial soreness. If soreness disrupts subsequent training, adjust the program according to its intended purpose rather than treating soreness as the goal. ([17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/), [34](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27219125/))

## The assessment implication

Posture, palpation, and a single movement screen do not directly measure the molecular events discussed here. ([2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9023345/), [3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20813920/), [4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21654799/), [28](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29107331/))

A clinician or coach cannot identify Piezo1 activation, YAP/TAZ nuclear localization, collagen synthesis, or osteocyte fluid shear simply by palpating the skin. They can observe the external task and measure some outputs - force, range, velocity, pain, swelling, stiffness, imaging, or functional capacity - but those observations do not identify the molecular process. The studies discussed here use specific cellular, imaging, or tissue measurements to investigate those events. ([3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20813920/), [4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21654799/), [10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41840183/), [11](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38529481/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/), [28](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29107331/))

A useful loading assessment asks:

**What external task was performed?**
Load, repetitions, duration, speed, range, and frequency matter.

**Which tissues were plausibly loaded?**
This requires anatomy and biomechanics rather than one generic “posterior chain” label.

**What was the person’s prior exposure?**
An ordinary dose for one athlete may be novel for another.

**What happened during and after the session?**
Symptoms, performance, swelling, range, confidence, and recovery provide practical feedback.

**Did capacity change over repeated exposures?**
Longitudinal function matters more than one acute sensation or molecular story.

The assessment should remain humble. Even a clear improvement after a loading program does not prove which mechanosensor or signaling pathway caused it. ([17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/), [23](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7823387/))

## The programming implication

### Define the intended adaptation

“Load the back” is not a sufficiently precise goal.

Possible targets include:

- Muscle force.

- Muscle endurance.

- Bone strength.

- Tendon stiffness.

- Tolerance of spinal flexion.

- Tolerance of compression.

- Rotational control.

- Work capacity.

- Confidence in a previously painful task.

Different targets require different loading conditions and outcome measures. ([12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32154945/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/), [18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39527327/))

### Manipulate more than weight

External load matters, but so do:

- Range of motion.

- Repetition speed.

- Time under tension.

- Number of cycles.

- Set and session spacing.

- Exercise direction.

- Impact rate.

- Isometric hold duration.

- Frequency across the week.

- Rest and recovery.

These variables help determine the local mechanical signal. Raising bar weight while shortening range or changing technique may increase one tissue’s demand and decrease another’s. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/), [30](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1758295/), [38](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33161870/))

### Progress one uncertainty at a time

When possible, avoid dramatically increasing load, range, speed, volume, and frequency simultaneously. Changing one or two variables makes it easier to interpret the person’s response and to track changes in mechanical demand.

This is a programming principle rather than a molecular law. Mechanotransduction research explains why several variables matter; it does not provide a universal progression formula.

### Repeat long enough to permit adaptation

A single session creates an acute signal. Substantial training adaptations generally develop across repeated exposures. ([12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32154945/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/))

As a programming principle, keep enough consistency to evaluate the response to a loading pattern, and consider progression when the current dose no longer serves the intended goal. Programming needs enough consistency to assess capacity and enough adjustment to keep the stimulus relevant. ([12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32154945/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/), [18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39527327/))

### Include recovery in the dose

Recovery is not the period when nothing happens. Protein synthesis, matrix turnover, fluid recovery, inflammation resolution, and structural organization continue after the external loading ends. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/), [18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39527327/))

The appropriate interval depends on tissue, session magnitude, training history, sleep, nutrition, age, health, and the next task. Current research does not justify one universal 24-, 48-, or 72-hour rule across tissues. ([18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39527327/))

### Use symptoms as feedback, not molecular proof

Track pain, stiffness, and soreness alongside function and performance when reviewing a loading program. These observations do not reveal the exact tissue pathway. ([18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39527327/), [20](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32694387/))

When symptoms increase, consider whether the dose, timing, unfamiliarity, or context changed. A symptom-free session may still provide substantial tissue loading. Interpret symptoms alongside function, performance, neurological status, recovery, and longer-term trend. ([18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39527327/), [20](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32694387/), [30](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1758295/))

## Pain is not a mechanotransduction meter

Mechanotransduction and nociception are not the same process. ([3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20813920/), [20](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32694387/))

A cell can detect and respond to mechanical load without producing pain. Bone remodels, tendons alter stiffness, discs change fluid content, and muscles synthesize proteins during ordinary painless activity. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32154945/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/))

Mechanical stimulation can also activate nociceptors or contribute to inflammatory signaling. But nociceptive input is not identical to the conscious experience of pain. The revised IASP definition recognizes pain as a sensory and emotional experience associated with, or resembling that associated with, actual or potential tissue damage, and emphasizes that pain and nociception are distinct. ([20](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32694387/))

Pain can be influenced by local tissue state, past pain experiences, sleep, stress, social expectations, fear, immune activity, context, and nervous-system sensitivity. A painful loading response may contain useful information, but it cannot identify whether Piezo1, YAP/TAZ, integrins, primary cilia, cytokines, matrix strain, or some other pathway is responsible. ([20](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32694387/), [35](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16297554/), [39](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34062143/))

The correct conclusion is:

> **Tissue hears load through mechanotransduction. The person experiences pain through a wider nervous-system process.**

The two can interact without being interchangeable.

## What mechanotransduction is not

Mechanotransduction is **not tissue damage**. Cells can detect ordinary, noninjurious deformation. ([2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9023345/), [3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20813920/), [4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21654799/))

It is **not muscle soreness**. Soreness does not quantify the adaptive response. ([17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/), [34](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27219125/))

It is **not progressive overload by itself**. Progressive overload is a training strategy; mechanotransduction is the biological process through which tissue may respond. ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19244270/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/))

It is **not one molecular pathway**. Integrins, channels, cilia, cytoskeleton, nuclei, growth factors, metabolism, and inflammatory pathways interact. ([3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20813920/), [5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40738397/), [7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42528914/), [19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41823544/))

It is **not automatically anabolic**. Mechanical signals can support maintenance, synthesis, breakdown, inflammation, senescence, or death depending on dose and context. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [8](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35606793/), [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36583692/))

It is **not proof that heavier is always better**. Local tissue stress and strain do not rise in a simple one-to-one relationship with external weight. ([12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32154945/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/), [30](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1758295/), [38](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33161870/))

It is **not proof that movement variability is always beneficial**. A new vector can provide a useful signal or exceed current capacity. ([12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32154945/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/), [18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39527327/))

It is **not evidence that one exercise “heals” a tissue through a named molecule**. A plausible pathway is not a clinical-outcome trial. ([7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42528914/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/), [23](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7823387/))

It is **not synonymous with structural adaptation**. Signaling can begin quickly; lasting structural adaptation generally takes time and often repeated exposure. ([12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32154945/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/))

It is **not a pain diagnosis**. Pain does not identify a cellular mechanosensor or quantify tissue adaptation. ([20](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32694387/))

## Working vocabulary for the rest of the curriculum

**Mechanotransduction** is the conversion of mechanical cues into biochemical and cellular responses. ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19244270/), [19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41823544/))

**Mechanobiology** is the broader study of how mechanical conditions and biological processes influence one another. ([19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41823544/))

**Mechanotherapy** is the therapeutic use of mechanical loading to promote remodeling, repair, or functional adaptation. ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19244270/))

**Load** is the external or internal mechanical demand applied to a structure. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [30](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1758295/))

**Stress** is force distributed over an area. ([16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/))

**Strain** is deformation relative to a tissue’s starting dimension. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/))

**Strain rate** is the speed at which strain develops. It can influence biological response independently of peak strain. ([14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7573420/))

**Compression** pushes a tissue together. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/))

**Tension** pulls a tissue apart. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38795012/))

**Shear** promotes sliding between adjacent regions. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/))

**Hydrostatic pressure** is compressive pressure acting equally in all directions. It is particularly relevant to hydrated tissues such as the nucleus pulposus and cartilage. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/))

**Osmotic pressure** reflects the tendency of water to move according to dissolved solutes and fixed charges within a matrix. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/))

The **extracellular matrix** is the structural and biochemical environment surrounding cells. It transmits mechanical signals and is remodeled in response to them. ([2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9023345/), [7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42528914/))

A **mechanosensor** is a cellular structure or molecular system capable of detecting a mechanical cue. ([3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20813920/), [19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41823544/))

An **integrin** is a transmembrane adhesion protein linking extracellular matrix to intracellular cytoskeletal systems. ([2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9023345/))

A **focal adhesion** is a multi-protein complex that connects matrix, integrins, cytoskeleton, and signaling molecules. ([2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9023345/), [7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42528914/), [26](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19179532/))

A **mechanically activated ion channel** changes ion flow in response to membrane or tissue deformation. ([3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20813920/))

**Piezo1** and **Piezo2** are mechanically activated cation-channel components identified in foundational ion-channel research. ([3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20813920/))

A **primary cilium** is a small membrane-bound cellular projection that can participate in mechanical and chemical sensing. ([5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40738397/))

The **cytoskeleton** is the internal filament network that helps determine cell shape, transmit tension, and connect membrane structures to the nucleus. ([2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9023345/), [27](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23062334/))

**YAP and TAZ** are transcriptional co-regulators whose activity can respond to matrix stiffness, cell geometry, and cytoskeletal tension. ([4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21654799/))

An **anabolic response** supports synthesis or accumulation of tissue components. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/))

A **catabolic response** supports breakdown or loss of tissue components. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/))

**Homeostasis** is the dynamic maintenance of tissue function through balanced synthesis, breakdown, repair, and adaptation. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41823544/))

**Accommodation** describes a reduced response to a repeatedly presented, unchanged stimulus. ([13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12162508/))

**Remodeling** is the longer-term reorganization of tissue structure and composition in response to biological and mechanical demands. ([11](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38529481/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/))

## The modern verdict

### Strongly supported

Cells convert mechanical cues into biochemical signals. ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19244270/), [3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20813920/), [4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21654799/))

The extracellular matrix, cell membrane, adhesion complexes, ion channels, primary cilia, cytoskeleton, and nucleus all participate in mechanical sensing. ([2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9023345/), [3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20813920/), [4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21654799/), [5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40738397/), [28](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29107331/))

Load magnitude, rate, duration, direction, cycles, and recovery can alter the response. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32154945/), [13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12162508/), [14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7573420/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/))

Bone, tendon, muscle, cartilage, ligament, and intervertebral-disc tissues are mechanosensitive. ([5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40738397/), [6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [11](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38529481/), [15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38795012/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/), [19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41823544/))

Repeated mechanical loading can produce measurable human adaptation in bone, tendon, and muscle. ([11](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38529481/), [12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32154945/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/))

### Mechanistically supported but clinically uncertain

Piezo1, TRPV4, integrin signaling, primary cilia, YAP/TAZ, and related pathways plausibly influence disc, tendon, bone, and cartilage responses. ([5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40738397/), [7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42528914/), [8](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35606793/), [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36583692/), [15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38795012/))

The same pathway can be anabolic in one context and catabolic in another. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [8](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35606793/), [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36583692/))

Most pathway-specific evidence comes from isolated cells, animals, engineered matrices, or organ culture. ([7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42528914/), [15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38795012/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/))

Pharmacologically manipulating a mechanosensor is not yet equivalent to a proven rehabilitation strategy. ([7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42528914/), [8](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35606793/), [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36583692/), [15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38795012/))

### Not established

The evidence reviewed here does not establish a universal optimal strain threshold across tissues. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32154945/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/))

The evidence reviewed here does not establish a single exercise that maximizes mechanotransduction everywhere. ([6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/), [12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32154945/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35657492/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/))

The studies reviewed here do not validate soreness, posture, or palpation as measures of cellular signaling. ([3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20813920/), [4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21654799/), [7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42528914/), [34](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27219125/))

Microdamage should not be assumed to be required for every adaptive response. ([3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20813920/), [4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21654799/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/), [34](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27219125/))

A pain flare does not by itself establish harmful mechanotransduction. ([20](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32694387/), [39](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34062143/))

The evidence reviewed here does not establish a molecular shortcut that replaces appropriate loading, functional assessment, and monitoring. ([7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42528914/), [15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38795012/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/), [18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39527327/))

The updated model is:

> **Mechanical load changes the cell’s physical environment. Cells interpret that change through overlapping sensors and signaling networks. Repeated responses can remodel tissue - but the result depends on the dose, the tissue, the person, and the recovery environment.**

## The takeaway

Mechanotransduction is how tissue **hears load**.

Cells do not hear “deadlift,” “stretch,” “good posture,” or “bad posture.” They hear local deformation, pressure, fluid movement, matrix stiffness, and the rate and duration of those signals. Integrins, focal adhesions, mechanically activated ion channels, primary cilia, the cytoskeleton, and nuclear regulators translate those physical events into biological activity. ([2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9023345/), [3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20813920/), [4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21654799/), [5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40738397/), [6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30569032/))

That activity can support protein synthesis, collagen remodeling, bone formation, matrix maintenance, inflammation, breakdown, or cell death. There is no universal beneficial signal and no single molecular switch controlling adaptation. ([7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42528914/), [8](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35606793/), [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36583692/), [11](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38529481/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37382939/))

For training and rehabilitation, the practical rule is:

> **Apply a relevant mechanical signal, at a tolerable dose, often enough to stimulate adaptation, with enough recovery to express it.**

The goal is not to maximize force at all times. It is to create the right local signal for the capacity being built - and to adjust that signal according to how the person responds.
