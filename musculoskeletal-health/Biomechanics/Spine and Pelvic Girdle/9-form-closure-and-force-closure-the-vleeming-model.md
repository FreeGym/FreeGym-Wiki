# Form Closure and Force Closure: The Vleeming Model

Form closure and force closure are among the most useful concepts in pelvic biomechanics, but they are also among the easiest to turn into folklore.

Within the Vleeming-Snijders biomechanical framework, the terms describe two interacting ways the sacroiliac joints resist unwanted shear and transfer load. **Form closure** describes stability arising from joint shape, surface contour, contact, and friction. **Force closure** describes additional compression and tension arising from muscles, fascia, ligaments, gravity, external loads, and sometimes external supports such as pelvic belts. Neither term means that the pelvis is literally “closed,” locked, perfectly aligned, or unable to move. ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31218826/), [2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3512279/))

A useful first rule is:

> **Form and force closure are load-transfer mechanisms, not diagnoses.** ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31218826/), [2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3512279/), [8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0268003324000652))

A person does not “have form closure” in the same way they have a fracture. The closure model does not provide a validated way to identify a force-closure deficit from pelvic posture or a palpated landmark, and palpatory SIJ mobility tests have not demonstrated concurrent validity against a credible reference standard. ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31218826/), [20](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33896601/)) A difficult active straight-leg raise does not by itself establish muscle weakness; the task can reflect pain, disability, perceived difficulty, and altered motor strategy. ([15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11805667/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25119510/), [18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28841825/)) A pelvic belt helping symptoms does not prove the SI joint was unstable. ([14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/)) The model offers a way to conceptualize how forces might be managed; it does not, by itself, establish why someone hurts. ([8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0268003324000652), [21](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7680716/)) Contemporary SIJ reviews continue to support the mechanical importance of the region while emphasizing that the relationship among structure, motion, pain, and treatment remains incompletely explained. ([8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0268003324000652))

## The model in one sentence

The simplest useful version is:

**Form closure is what the joint’s architecture contributes. Force closure is the additional compressive joint-reaction force arising from muscles, tensioned passive tissues, gravity, external loads, and support. Neuromotor control modulates the active muscular contribution according to the task.** ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31218826/), [5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/), [14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/))

These components are not independent switches. Joint geometry affects how muscle and ligament forces are transmitted. Muscle activity changes ligament tension and contact force. Joint position changes moment arms and the direction of compression. Pain can alter muscle recruitment. External support may alter the muscular effort needed for a task. The pelvis therefore behaves as an integrated system rather than as separate passive and active compartments. ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31218826/), [5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/), [14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/))

The model is easiest to understand as a continuum:

**Joint shape and passive tissues provide a starting level of restraint. Muscular, fascial, and external forces modify that restraint according to the task.** ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31218826/), [5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/), [14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/))

## Why the SI joint needed a closure model

The SI joints sit between the sacrum and the two ilia. They receive load from the spine and transmit it into the pelvic ring, acetabula, femurs, and lower limbs. Forces also travel in the opposite direction, from the ground through the legs and pelvis toward the trunk. The SIJ therefore has to permit enough relative movement for pelvic-ring accommodation while resisting excessive translation and rotation under changing loads. ([2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3512279/), [6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7041664/))

The joint does not solve that problem through a large range of motion. Systematic and contemporary biomechanics reviews generally describe SIJ motion as small, although estimates differ according to measurement method, posture, population, and whether researchers measure true sacrum-to-ilium movement or broader spinopelvic orientation. A 2024 review reported that measured SIJ translations are commonly below 1 mm and found no clear motion difference separating physiological from pathological joints in the literature it examined. ([7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33256545/), [8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0268003324000652))

The joint’s job is therefore not to generate visible movement. It is to remain sufficiently controlled while the lumbar spine, whole pelvis, hips, and lower limbs create much larger movements around it. The original closure model was an attempt to explain how a relatively flat, low-motion joint could transfer substantial load without relying on a perfectly interlocking bony fit. ([4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2326707/), [5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/))

## Closure does not mean closed

The word **closure** creates immediate confusion. In this model, closure does not mean that a joint gap physically disappears. It does not mean fusion, locking, immobility, repositioning, or the achievement of one ideal pelvic alignment. ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31218826/), [8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0268003324000652))

Closure means that the joint system can resist displacement under load. ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31218826/), [5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/))

A door can be closed and motionless; that is not the intended analogy. The SIJ remains a living joint complex with cartilage, contact surfaces, ligaments, fascia, fluid, innervation, and small relative motion. “More closure” means greater resistance to unwanted shear in a specified loading condition, not necessarily less movement everywhere and not automatically better clinical function. ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31218826/), [8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0268003324000652))

This distinction is central because the model should not be converted into the following positional story:

- Form closure becomes “the bones are aligned.”

- Force closure becomes “the core is switched on.”

- Pain becomes “the joint is out.”

- Treatment becomes “put it back and stabilize it.”


The underlying literature does not justify that chain of conclusions. The model describes mechanical contributions to stability. It does not provide a validated method for identifying minute pelvic positional faults or proving that a particular muscle has failed. ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31218826/), [8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0268003324000652), [15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11805667/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28582642/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25119510/), [18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28841825/)) Palpatory SIJ mobility tests have not demonstrated concurrent validity against a credible reference standard. ([20](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33896601/))

## Where the model came from

The modern form-and-force-closure vocabulary grew from anatomical and biomechanical work by Vleeming, Snijders, Stoeckart, and colleagues around the late 1980s and early 1990s. Two 1990 papers examined the relationship between SIJ form and function. The anatomical paper described joint-surface features, while the biomechanical paper tested how surface texture, ridges, and depressions affected resistance to movement. ([3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2326706/), [4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2326707/))

Their experiments supported the idea that the SIJ’s coarse, irregular surfaces increase friction and resistance compared with smoother surfaces. Ridges and depressions contributed more resistance than coarse texture alone in the experimental conditions. These were anatomical and mechanical findings, not evidence that more irregularity necessarily means less pain, or that a clinician can infer joint congruence through external palpation. ([4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2326707/), [2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3512279/))

Snijders, Vleeming, and Stoeckart then developed a self-bracing model in which compression across the SIJ increases frictional resistance to shear. Their model incorporated joint-surface configuration, ligament forces, muscle forces, and the geometry of the pelvic ring. It was a foundational biomechanical explanation for how the sacrum could transfer load into the ilia without depending on perfect articular interlocking. ([5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/))

The important methodological point is that these were anatomical experiments and mechanical models. They support the existence and plausibility of closure mechanisms. They do not demonstrate that a specific exercise corrects SIJ pain, that every painful pelvis lacks force closure, or that a stone-arch analogy accurately captures every aspect of living pelvic mechanics. ([3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2326706/), [4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2326707/), [5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/))

## Form closure: geometry before effort

Vleeming and Schuenke define form closure as a theoretical condition in which closely fitting joint surfaces provide stability without requiring additional lateral compressive force. If the sacrum fitted perfectly between the ilia, external compression would not be needed to maintain joint stability during loading and unloading. The word **theoretical** is essential: perfect form closure is an ideal mechanical limit, not a normal clinical state that can be measured by posture. ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31218826/))

Form closure includes several interacting features:

**Surface shape.** The sacral and iliac articular surfaces have complementary contours rather than functioning like two flat polished plates. ([2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3512279/), [3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2326706/))

**Ridges and grooves.** Irregularities can resist sliding and increase the mechanical engagement of the opposing surfaces. ([4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2326707/))

**Surface friction.** Rougher, more irregular contact can provide greater resistance to shear when compressive force is present. ([4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2326707/), [5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/))

**Pelvic-ring geometry.** The orientation of the sacrum, ilia, SI joints, pubic symphysis, and acetabula affects how vertical, horizontal, and rotational forces are distributed. ([5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/), [6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7041664/))

Form closure is therefore more than “bone shape.” It also depends on the direction of load and the surfaces being held in contact. A joint surface can have useful ridges and grooves, but those features cannot resist shear effectively if contact is lost or if the applied force pushes the surfaces apart. The mechanical contribution of form cannot be separated completely from the forces acting on the form. ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31218826/), [5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/))

## Why form closure is not “good alignment”

Form closure should not be interpreted as the pelvis being in one correct anatomical position. That is not how the model is defined. ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31218826/), [8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0268003324000652))

A pelvis can change orientation during walking, squatting, hinging, sitting, rolling in bed, breathing, and single-leg stance without losing all mechanical stability. The sacrum can undergo small relative nutation or counternutation while the system continues transferring load. The whole pelvis can tilt through a large visible range while actual SIJ movement remains small. ([7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33256545/), [8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0268003324000652))

Nor is there one uniform SIJ shape. A 2024 review concluded that SIJ articular morphology varies substantially among individuals and that the relationship between surface morphology and pain remains unclear. A 2025 finite-element study likewise found that mechanically modeled responses differed between one expert-selected representative female geometry and one representative male geometry, but those results were model-dependent and cannot establish population-level sex differences without broader in-vivo verification. ([8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0268003324000652), [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40526189/))

Form closure therefore does not supply a visual template against which every pelvis should be judged. It describes a mechanical contribution arising from anatomy that naturally varies. ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31218826/), [8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0268003324000652), [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40526189/))

## Force closure: adding compression to the system

Because the SIJ does not have perfect form closure, additional compression can increase resistance to shear. This is **force closure**. ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31218826/), [5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/))

In the original model, compression across the SIJ increases friction between the joint surfaces. That friction helps resist the tendency of the sacrum and ilia to translate relative to one another under trunk weight, ground-reaction forces, muscle action, and external loading. Force closure allows the system to adapt its stiffness to the task rather than relying entirely on fixed bony geometry. ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31218826/), [5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/))

Possible contributors include:

**Muscle forces**, including forces from gluteus maximus, erector spinae, biceps femoris, abdominal-wall muscles, pelvic-floor muscles, and other muscles whose actions influence the sacrum, ilia, lumbar spine, femurs, ligaments, or fascia. ([10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14986072/), [12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15234479/))

**Ligament tension**, including interosseous, dorsal sacroiliac, sacrotuberous, sacrospinous, and other pelvic ligaments. ([5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/), [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40526189/))

**Fascial tension**, particularly forces transmitted through the thoracolumbar fascia and abdominal aponeuroses. ([2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3512279/))

**External compression**, such as manual compression or a pelvic belt. ([14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/))

**Load direction and gravity**, which can compress, shear, rotate, or distract different parts of the pelvic ring depending on posture and task. ([5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/), [13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18204902/))

The phrase **additional compression** is more accurate than “muscular stability.” Force closure does not belong exclusively to muscles. Ligaments may already be tensioned before a voluntary contraction begins, and external load can alter joint reaction force without making force closure exclusively muscular. ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31218826/), [5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/), [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40526189/), [13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18204902/))

## The friction model: useful, but simplified

The core physics is straightforward. In a simplified friction model, the amount of shear that can be resisted increases as the compressive force pressing two surfaces together increases. In conceptual terms:

**Maximum frictional resistance ≈ effective coefficient of friction × compressive contact force.** ([5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/), [13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18204902/))

This is a simplified Coulomb-friction analogy, not a validated constitutive law for the living SIJ. It helps explain why active or external compression can make a relatively flat joint more resistant to sliding. It is the mechanical foundation of the self-bracing concept. ([5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/), [13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18204902/))

But the SIJ is not two dry blocks. It is a biological joint complex with cartilage, irregular surfaces, fluid, ligaments, deformable tissues, variable contact regions, neural control, and small three-dimensional motion. Friction is part of the explanation, not the complete explanation. The 2024 biomechanics review concluded that pelvic mechanical environments clearly matter but that no concrete, comprehensive mechanical explanation currently links SIJ structure, dysfunction, pain, and treatment. ([8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0268003324000652))

The practical interpretation is:

> **Compression can improve resistance to shear, but a living pelvis cannot be reduced to a clamp.** ([5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/), [8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0268003324000652), [13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18204902/))

## Form and force closure are not two separate teams

An introductory diagram may place bones under “form closure” and muscles under “force closure.” That is useful for introductory teaching but mechanically incomplete. ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31218826/), [2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3512279/), [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40526189/))

Ligaments are passive tissues, yet ligament pretension means that they already carry tensile load before further movement or loading occurs. Their resistance depends on length, orientation, stiffness, and the position of the sacrum and ilia. Joint shape determines which ligaments become tensioned under a given load. Muscle contraction changes joint position and ligament tension. The same structure can therefore participate in both the starting architecture and the force-dependent response. ([2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3512279/), [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40526189/))

The 2025 finite-element study by Heyland and colleagues is particularly useful here. It used two finite-element geometries: one expert-selected representative female joint and one representative male joint, chosen from a retrospective CT cohort of 818 people. Within those two models, ligament pretension substantially influenced predicted relative SIJ movement and stress. Differences between the models reflected their selected geometries and assumptions and cannot establish a population-level sex effect. The authors concluded that ligament pretension must be verified in situ before its clinical relevance can be established. ([9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40526189/))

Those model findings do not give clinicians a method for measuring ligament pretension. They show why the form-versus-force boundary is porous. Passive anatomy is not force-free, and active force does not act independently of anatomy. ([9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40526189/))

## What the in-vivo muscle study actually showed

A small in-vivo study of muscular force closure is van Wingerden and colleagues’ 2004 study. Six healthy women underwent SIJ stiffness measurement using colour Doppler imaging combined with induced oscillation of the ilium. Stiffness was assessed at rest and during attempted isometric activation of biceps femoris, gluteus maximus, erector spinae, and contralateral latissimus dorsi. ([10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14986072/))

SIJ stiffness increased during muscular activity, with the largest effects associated with erector spinae, biceps femoris, and gluteus maximus. Latissimus dorsi had a smaller effect, and co-contraction made perfect muscle isolation difficult. The study supports the plausibility that voluntary muscle activity can alter SIJ stiffness measured under this laboratory protocol. It did not directly quantify articular compression or establish improved clinical stability. ([10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14986072/))

Its limitations matter. ([10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14986072/))

- The sample contained six healthy women.

- The task involved attempted isolated isometric contraction, not squatting, running, or lifting.

- Stiffness was a mechanical outcome, not pain, disability, injury risk, or athletic performance.

- Co-contraction occurred.

- The study did not test an exercise program.


The evidence supports the plausibility of a **muscular contribution to force closure**. It does not establish one correct muscle-recruitment pattern or prove that strengthening those muscles treats SIJ-related pain. ([10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14986072/))

## The sacrotuberous ligament: a bridge between passive and active force

The sacrotuberous ligament is a useful example of why the active-passive split is incomplete. In an embalmed-pelvis study, Vleeming and colleagues applied load to the sacrotuberous ligament along directions intended to represent hamstring and gluteus-maximus forces. This loading significantly reduced ventral sacral rotation, or nutation, in the experimental setup. ([11](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/026800338990003X))

The mechanism is plausible: a muscle applies force, that force tensions a ligament, and the ligament then changes SIJ resistance. The muscle does not need to cross the SI joint in a simple tendon-over-joint arrangement to influence its mechanics. ([11](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/026800338990003X), [2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3512279/))

The limitation is equally important. This was an embalmed-specimen mechanism study. It does not show that hamstring tightness indicates SIJ instability, that the biceps femoris should be activated to “lock” the pelvis, or that manipulating the sacrotuberous ligament treats pain. ([11](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/026800338990003X))

The correct conclusion is smaller:

**Muscle-to-ligament connections can alter SIJ mechanics.** ([11](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/026800338990003X))

## Pelvic-floor contribution: mechanically plausible, clinically uncertain

Pelvic-floor muscles are often placed inside the force-closure system because their contraction can influence the inferior pelvic ring and oppose separation of the innominate bones. Pool-Goudzwaard and colleagues tested this mechanically in 18 embalmed specimens by applying springs intended to simulate pelvic-floor tension. ([12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15234479/))

Simulated pelvic-floor tension increased pelvic-ring stiffness by 8.5% in the female specimens but did not significantly increase stiffness in the male specimens. It also produced backward sacral rotation in both sexes. These findings demonstrate a possible mechanical effect under cadaveric testing conditions. ([12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15234479/))

They do not prove that pelvic-floor weakness causes pelvic pain or that repeated pelvic-floor contraction is the correct treatment for every person with an SIJ-region complaint. The specimens were embalmed, muscle force was simulated with springs, and no clinical outcome was measured. ([12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15234479/))

This is a recurring rule for the entire model:

> **A tissue’s ability to influence stiffness does not establish that insufficient activity of that tissue is causing a person’s symptoms.** ([12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15234479/))

## Transversus abdominis: contributor, not pelvic seat belt

A computational model by Pel and colleagues included 100 muscle elements, eight ligaments, and eight joints to examine how muscle and ligament forces might reduce vertical SIJ shear during standing. When the model was constrained to reduce shear by 20%, calculated SIJ compression increased by approximately 70%. Imposing another 20% shear reduction caused a much larger increase in compression, largely through modeled transversus-abdominis and pelvic-floor forces. ([13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18204902/))

The authors proposed that training these muscles might help SIJ-related pelvic pain. That treatment implication was an inference from a static optimization model, not a finding from a clinical trial. The study did not enroll patients, prescribe exercise, or measure pain. The model’s outputs depended on its anatomical, force-limit, and optimization assumptions. ([13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18204902/))

The study is valuable because it demonstrates a mechanical tradeoff within the model: reducing modeled shear required increasing modeled compression, and the relationship was not linear. It does not justify the claim that transversus abdominis is a pelvic seat belt that should always be contracted harder. ([13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18204902/))

## More force closure is not automatically better

Force closure should not be interpreted as though more compression always equals more stability and therefore better function. That conclusion does not follow from the cited evidence. ([13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18204902/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25119510/), [18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28841825/))

Compression can increase shear resistance and joint reaction force. In the Pel static model, progressively reducing modeled shear demanded disproportionately greater modeled compression and muscle force. Greater co-contraction may plausibly carry metabolic, fatigue, or movement costs in vivo, but those outcomes were not measured. ([13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18204902/))

Clinical motor behavior also does not consistently fit a “too little activation” story. Mens and Pool-Goudzwaard compared women with long-lasting pregnancy-related posterior pelvic-girdle pain with controls during an active straight-leg raise. The pain group demonstrated greater ultrasound-measured transversus-abdominis thickening, interpreted by the investigators as greater contraction, rather than reduced contraction. The authors concluded that their findings did not support routinely trying to increase transversus-abdominis activation in that population. ([18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28841825/))

Experimental pain produces a similar caution. In a randomized crossover experiment involving 34 healthy participants, injection-induced pelvic pain made the active straight-leg raise more difficult and increased bilateral trunk and thigh muscle activity. The broader stabilization strategy appeared after pain was induced; it was not evidence of a pre-existing closure deficit. ([17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25119510/))

Together, these studies support a better principle:

> **Useful force closure is appropriately scaled force closure.** ([13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18204902/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25119510/), [18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28841825/))

The evidence therefore does not support prescribing more compression for every person or task. Some people may show an immediate change in pain or task performance with external compression, whereas others may already use a broad, high-contraction strategy during painful tasks. The clinical goal is not maximal closure. It is adaptable load transfer. ([14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25119510/), [18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28841825/))

## Nutation and counternutation inside the model

**Nutation** and **counternutation** describe small relative motions of the sacrum and ilia. Nutation generally refers to the sacral base tipping anteriorly and inferiorly relative to the ilia; counternutation describes the reverse pattern. These are SIJ-relative terms, not synonyms for whole-pelvis anterior and posterior tilt. ([2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3512279/), [7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33256545/))

In closure models, nutation is often discussed as a position that tensions certain posterior ligaments and may increase self-bracing. That is a direction-dependent mechanical hypothesis, not a command to keep the sacrum permanently nutated. The sacrotuberous-ligament experiment, for example, showed that loading the ligament restricted further nutation. It did not demonstrate that nutation is universally stable, painless, or desirable. ([11](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/026800338990003X))

Real motion is small and three-dimensional. A 2024 review found that SIJ movement during whole-body tasks remains insufficiently understood and that current evidence does not provide a simple movement pattern distinguishing healthy from pathological joints. ([8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0268003324000652))

The practical rule is:

**Nutation and counternutation are mechanical descriptors, not good and bad positions.** ([7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33256545/), [8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0268003324000652), [11](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/026800338990003X))

## Neuromotor control: the part that makes closure task-specific

Joint shape does not change from repetition to repetition, but force requirements do. Walking, sprinting, standing on one leg, carrying a suitcase, rolling in bed, squatting, and deadlifting place different combinations of compression, shear, rotation, and muscular demand on the pelvic ring. ([5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/), [13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18204902/), [14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/))

Neuromotor control is the process by which the nervous system coordinates muscular force, timing, direction, co-contraction, and feedback in anticipation of and response to task demands. Discussions of external pelvic compression commonly treat form closure, force closure, and neuromotor control as synergistic contributors rather than isolated systems. ([14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/))

This makes the active motor contribution to force closure inherently task-dependent. By inference, a muscle’s contribution in a static isometric test should not be assumed to match its contribution during gait, and a high-bracing strategy used for a maximal lift should not be presumed necessary for walking. ([10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14986072/), [14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/)) Pain itself can increase perceived difficulty and produce a more extensive trunk-and-thigh stabilization strategy even without demonstrated pre-existing SIJ instability. ([17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25119510/), [18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28841825/))

Force closure is therefore not simply something a person either possesses or lacks. The active motor strategy contributing to force closure changes with the task. ([10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14986072/), [14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25119510/))

## External compression: what a pelvic belt can change

A pelvic belt applies external compression to the pelvic ring. Mechanically, it may supplement internally generated force closure, alter joint laxity or movement, and redistribute muscular demand. It may also change how the task feels, although the 2012 review did not establish a specific sensory mechanism. ([14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/))

A 2012 systematic review included 18 studies of external pelvic compression. Study quality ranged from 33% to 72% on the review’s modified Downs and Black assessment. The studies used heterogeneous immediate comparison conditions, including an uncompressed or baseline condition or another application condition, rather than one common comparator. Against those study-specific comparison conditions, the review found moderate evidence for immediate changes in SIJ laxity, lumbopelvic kinematics, muscle recruitment, and pain, but limited evidence for changes in sacral mobility and surrounding muscle strength. Considerable heterogeneity in belt type, position, compression force, population, and outcome prevented firm conclusions, and the findings concerned immediate rather than sustained effects. ([14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/))

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis included seven pregnancy studies. The included studies used varying comparators, including no-belt or usual-care conditions, exercise or information-based care, and other belt designs. Across those comparisons, the pooled estimate suggested a modest reduction in pain and minimal improvement in disability, with the evidence rated low or very low. These findings are population-specific and do not establish a diagnostic mechanism. ([22](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40716213/))

These findings do not justify saying that belts “restore form closure.” A belt supplies external compression; it does not change the underlying bony morphology. The 2012 review supports immediate changes in laxity, kinematics, muscle recruitment, and pain under some conditions. Reduced active muscular demand, altered confidence, sensory input, or expectation remain possible and non-exclusive explanations rather than mechanisms established by that review. A positive response can identify a support strategy worth testing further, not a specific anatomical fault. ([14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/), [22](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40716213/))

The 2022 clinical practice guideline for postpartum pelvic-girdle pain recommends that pelvic belts not be used in isolation. Belts may be used alongside exercise, education, gait assistance, functional training, or manual therapy while exercise tolerance improves. The same guideline recommends exercise based on identified muscle-performance impairments and advises modifying exercise when painful. These recommendations apply specifically to postpartum pelvic-girdle pain and should not be generalized automatically to all athletes or all SIJ-region symptoms. ([19](https://doi.org/10.1097/JWH.0000000000000236))

## A belt response is not a diagnostic test for instability

Suppose manual pelvic compression makes an active straight-leg raise easier. Several interpretations are possible:

- External compression changed pelvic-ring mechanics.

- Muscle recruitment changed.

- Pain decreased.

- The task felt safer.

- Sensory feedback improved.

- The person changed breathing or effort.

- The result reflected expectation or familiarity.


The first three interpretations are consistent with immediate outcomes reported in the compression literature; the remaining interpretations are plausible but were not directly established by the 2012 review. The response does not prove that the SIJ was excessively mobile, that one innominate was rotated, or that a particular force-closure muscle was deficient. The 2012 compression review itself found heterogeneity and uncertainty regarding application parameters and mechanisms. ([14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/))

A belt trial can still be used as a temporary management experiment when the relevant outcome is specified, such as immediate pain or task difficulty. The correction is epistemic: **use the response to guide management, not to invent a structural diagnosis.** ([14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/), [19](https://doi.org/10.1097/JWH.0000000000000236), [22](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40716213/))

## The active straight-leg raise: a load-transfer task, not a position test

The **active straight-leg raise**, or ASLR, is closely associated with the force-closure model. The person lies supine and lifts one extended leg. The task creates asymmetrical demands across the trunk, pelvis, hips, and lower limbs and can be rated according to difficulty or symptom response. ([15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11805667/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28582642/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25119510/))

In a study of 200 women with posterior pelvic pain after pregnancy, ASLR difficulty correlated with established measures of disability and symptom severity. The correlation with the Québec Back Pain Disability Scale was 0.70. The authors recommended the ASLR as a disease-severity scale for that population. ([15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11805667/))

That is not the same as validating the ASLR as a direct measure of SIJ motion, form closure, or force-closure failure. ([15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11805667/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28582642/))

Kibsgård and colleagues directly measured SIJ motion during the ASLR using radiostereometric analysis in 12 patients with severe, long-lasting pelvic-girdle pain scheduled for SIJ fusion. SIJ movements were small. The larger measured movement occurred on the rested-leg side, with mean backward rotation of 0.8° and inward tilt of 0.3°. The findings contradicted an earlier assumption that the lifted-leg innominate rotated forward during the test. ([16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28582642/))

Experimental pain can also impair the test and increase trunk and thigh muscle activity without any demonstrated preceding joint instability. ([17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25119510/))

The evidence-supported interpretation is:

> **The ASLR is a functional task that can reflect perceived difficulty, pain, disability, effort, and motor strategy. It is not a direct window into SIJ position.** ([15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11805667/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28582642/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25119510/))

## What the model explains well

The Vleeming model offers a useful account of several important mechanical ideas.

It offers a plausible mechanical account of why a low-motion joint with relatively flat surfaces can still transfer substantial load. Surface irregularity and contact provide part of the resistance, while added compression increases frictional resistance to shear. ([4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2326707/), [5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/))

It helps explain why pelvic stability cannot be assigned to one muscle. Muscles, ligaments, fascia, joint geometry, and external forces act together. ([2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3512279/), [10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14986072/))

It helps explain why muscular activity can alter measured SIJ stiffness even when joint motion is small. ([10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14986072/))

It helps explain why a belt may immediately change task performance or symptoms without changing anatomy. ([14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/))

It helps explain why the appropriate level of trunk and pelvic stiffness depends on the load rather than on one permanent bracing strategy. ([13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18204902/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25119510/))

These are durable insights. ([2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3512279/), [4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2326707/), [5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/), [10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14986072/), [13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18204902/), [14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25119510/))

## What the model does not explain by itself

The model does not identify a pain source. SIJ-region pain can have multiple local and referred contributors, including intra-articular and extra-articular tissues, hip or lumbar referral, inflammatory disease, and broader pain-related factors. ([6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7041664/), [8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0268003324000652), [21](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7680716/))

The model does not prove that a painful joint is unstable. Contemporary reviews have not found a clear movement distinction separating physiological and pathological SIJs, and the structure-pain relationship remains uncertain. ([8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0268003324000652))

The model does not provide a validated diagnosis based on palpatory SIJ mobility tests. No study in a 2021 systematic review established concurrent validity for those tests. This finding does not automatically generalize to pain-provocation tests, neurological assessment, imaging for inflammatory or traumatic disease, or image-guided diagnostic procedures. ([20](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33896601/))

The model does not identify one corrective muscle. Muscle-contraction and computational studies demonstrate possible contributions to stiffness, not a universal hierarchy. ([10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14986072/), [13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18204902/))

The model does not prove that more stiffness is better. Some people with pain may already use an extensive stabilization strategy, and reducing modeled shear carries a modeled compression cost. ([13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18204902/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25119510/), [18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28841825/))

The model does not prescribe a unique treatment. Current postpartum guidance supports impairment-based exercise, education, functional training, and adjunctive rather than isolated use of belts or manual therapy. ([19](https://doi.org/10.1097/JWH.0000000000000236))

## What “force-closure dysfunction” would have to mean

The phrase **force-closure dysfunction** sounds specific, but the cited biomechanical literature does not supply a validated routine clinical definition. ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31218826/), [5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/), [8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0268003324000652), [14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/))

As a descriptive hypothesis, it could refer to insufficient joint compression for a particular task, poor timing or direction of muscular force, excessive effort, reduced strength, pain-related inhibition or guarding, or an inefficient distribution of force among the trunk and hips. Those are different proposed problems and would require different measurements. ([14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25119510/), [18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28841825/), [19](https://doi.org/10.1097/JWH.0000000000000236))

The cited belt, ASLR, and palpatory-test literature does not provide a validated routine measure of SIJ contact compression, effective friction, ligament pretension, or an overall force-closure capacity. Muscle strength tests, ASLR performance, belt response, palpation, posture, and movement observation therefore provide indirect information about a hypothesized closure mechanism rather than direct quantification. ([14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28582642/), [20](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33896601/))

The more honest clinical wording is usually descriptive:

- Difficulty transferring load during single-leg tasks.

- Pain with asymmetrical loading.

- Excessive trunk or pelvic rigidity.

- Reduced hip or trunk force capacity.

- Low confidence under load.

- Improvement with external compression.

- Poor tolerance of a specific range, speed, or volume.


Those descriptions identify observable problems without claiming that an invisible closure mechanism has been directly measured. ([14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/), [15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11805667/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28582642/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25119510/), [18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28841825/), [19](https://doi.org/10.1097/JWH.0000000000000236), [20](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33896601/))

## The athlete translation

This section is a clinical and coaching extrapolation from the model rather than a directly validated SIJ-specific intervention framework.

For a strength athlete, form closure is the relatively fixed anatomical background against which training occurs. Force closure is the task-dependent compression and stiffness generated as the athlete braces, produces hip force, receives ground reaction, and transfers load through the trunk and pelvic ring. This is a biomechanical extrapolation from the model rather than a directly tested athlete-specific description. ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31218826/), [5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/), [10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14986072/), [13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18204902/))

A bilateral squat, conventional deadlift, split squat, unilateral carry, sprint, throw, and change of direction do not require identical force-closure strategies. Stance width, hip position, load asymmetry, bar position, speed, fatigue, breathing, and external support all change the direction and magnitude of force through the pelvis. The model provides a rationale for expecting task-specific variation; it does not establish one ideal recruitment pattern. ([5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/), [13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18204902/))

Direct athlete-specific evidence is scarce. The key closure studies consist largely of cadaveric experiments, static computational models, small healthy-volunteer studies, and pregnancy-related pelvic-girdle-pain populations. Those findings support mechanistic plausibility but do not demonstrate that a named “force-closure program” prevents pain or improves performance in powerlifters, weightlifters, runners, or field athletes. ([10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14986072/), [12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15234479/), [13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18204902/), [14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/))

The following is a defensible coaching heuristic, not a tested SIJ-specific treatment principle:

> **Train the ability to create, direct, and release stiffness according to the task, not a mythical permanent pelvic lock.** ([5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/), [10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14986072/), [13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18204902/), [14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/))

## The assessment implication

A closure-informed assessment should begin with the task that is limited, not with a presumed bone position. This is a clinical reasoning approach, not a validated diagnostic test for closure. ([19](https://doi.org/10.1097/JWH.0000000000000236), [20](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33896601/))

Ask where symptoms occur, what loads provoke them, whether the problem is bilateral or asymmetrical, how symptoms respond to stance and range, whether hip or lumbar movement changes the task, whether speed or fatigue matters, and whether external compression changes pain or effort. ([14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/), [19](https://doi.org/10.1097/JWH.0000000000000236))

A pelvic belt or manual-compression trial can be used as a temporary modification. An ASLR can be used to observe difficulty and symptoms. Hip, trunk, lower-limb, neurological, and pain-provocation testing can help build a differential. ([14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/), [15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11805667/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28582642/), [19](https://doi.org/10.1097/JWH.0000000000000236)) None of those findings independently proves defective form or force closure. ([14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/), [15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11805667/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28582642/), [20](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33896601/)) A separate 2021 meta-analysis found that a positive cluster of SIJ pain-provocation tests had limited rule-in value, whereas a negative cluster was more useful for reducing the probability of SIJ pain. The certainty of evidence was very low. ([23](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34210160/))

The postpartum pelvic-girdle-pain guideline similarly recommends comprehensive abdominal, back, hip, neurological, functional, and symptom assessment rather than relying on one alignment or mobility test. ([19](https://doi.org/10.1097/JWH.0000000000000236))

Palpation may be used to document tenderness or symptom reproduction as part of a broader examination, but the 2021 review addressed SIJ mobility tests and does not validate palpation as a precise measure of sacral or innominate position. The review found variable reliability and no verified concurrent validity for the included SIJ mobility tests. ([19](https://doi.org/10.1097/JWH.0000000000000236), [20](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33896601/))

A good assessment asks:

**What load-transfer task is difficult, what strategy is being used, and what modification improves it?**

That is a more defensible application of the model than claiming to feel a closure failure. ([19](https://doi.org/10.1097/JWH.0000000000000236), [20](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33896601/))

## The programming implication

Programming should build the person’s ability to tolerate and coordinate force across the lumbopelvic-hip region. Depending on the assessment, that may include trunk strengthening, hip strengthening, pelvic-floor rehabilitation, carries, split-stance work, hinging, squatting, gait exposure, graded rotation, breathing practice, or temporary use of external support. This is a broad programming extrapolation; the postpartum guideline directly supports impairment-based exercise, functional training, and adjunctive external support in its population. ([19](https://doi.org/10.1097/JWH.0000000000000236))

The cited closure literature does not establish a universal local-to-global sequence. A low-load pelvic-floor or abdominal exercise may be appropriate for one person. Another may need heavier hip and trunk strength work. Another may need reduced bracing and more relaxed movement. Another may need graded exposure to a feared single-leg task. These examples are assessment-based programming options rather than a tested SIJ-specific sequence. Observational evidence of excessive transversus-abdominis activation in some people with pelvic-girdle pain directly cautions against assuming that everyone needs more deep-abdominal contraction. ([18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28841825/), [19](https://doi.org/10.1097/JWH.0000000000000236))

The 2022 postpartum guideline recommends exercise directed at identified muscle-performance impairments and modification when exercise is painful. It permits belts and manual therapy as cointerventions, not stand-alone treatments. That is a clinically stronger recommendation than choosing exercises solely because they are alleged to activate a force-closure muscle. ([19](https://doi.org/10.1097/JWH.0000000000000236))

The following progression is a clinically reasoned extrapolation, not a sequence established by the closure studies. A useful progression is not “restore closure, then move.” It is:

**Find a tolerable strategy, build capacity, reduce unnecessary support as appropriate, and return progressively to the target task.** ([19](https://doi.org/10.1097/JWH.0000000000000236))

## Pain is not a closure meter

Pain near the SIJ does not reveal the amount of joint compression, motion, shear, or ligament tension. Pain can change muscle activity and perceived effort, which means the observed movement strategy may be a response to pain rather than its original cause. The experimental-pain ASLR study showed that experimentally induced pain increased muscle activity and impaired task performance. ([17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25119510/))

The revised IASP definition describes pain as a sensory and emotional experience associated with, or resembling that associated with, actual or potential tissue damage. It also emphasizes that pain and nociception are different phenomena and that pain is influenced by biological, psychological, and social factors. ([21](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7680716/))

That does not make biomechanics irrelevant. It means the biomechanics must be placed inside a wider pain model. ([21](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7680716/))

A person can have: ([8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0268003324000652), [14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25119510/), [21](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7680716/))

- A local SIJ-region tissue problem plus protective muscular compression.

- Reduced capacity without measurable joint instability.

- High pain with small movement.

- Low pain despite anatomical variation.

- Improvement with a belt through several possible mechanisms.

- Persistent pain after the original tissue threat has changed.


The closure model describes one biological layer. It is not the whole pain explanation. ([8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0268003324000652), [21](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7680716/))

## Working vocabulary for Tier 2

**Form closure** is the theoretical contribution of joint shape, surface contour, contact, and friction to SIJ stability without additional lateral compressive force. ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31218826/))

**Force closure** is the additional compression and tension applied across the pelvic ring by muscles, fascia, ligaments, gravity, external load, or external support. ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31218826/), [5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/))

**Self-bracing** is the mechanical concept that SIJ compression and pelvic-ring geometry can increase resistance to shear under load. ([5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/))

**Joint compression** is force acting to press opposing joint surfaces together. It can increase frictional resistance while also increasing compressive joint-reaction loading. ([5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/), [13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18204902/))

**Shear** is force acting parallel to a surface, creating a tendency for adjacent structures to slide relative to one another. The closure model is primarily concerned with resistance to SIJ shear. ([5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/))

**Frictional resistance** is resistance to sliding generated when contacting surfaces are pressed together. In the model, it depends on both surface properties and compression. ([4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2326707/), [5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/))

**Ligament pretension** is tension already present in a ligament before additional movement or loading. Computational evidence suggests that assumed pretension strongly affects modeled SIJ motion and stress. ([9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40526189/))

**Neuromotor control** is the task-dependent coordination of muscle activation, timing, force direction, co-contraction, and feedback needed to manage pelvic loading. ([14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/))

**External pelvic compression** is compression applied through a belt, support, or manual contact. It may change mechanics, muscle activity, pain, or task difficulty without establishing a specific diagnosis. ([14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/))

The **active straight-leg raise** is a functional loading task that can quantify difficulty and symptom severity in selected pelvic-girdle-pain populations. It is not a direct measure of SIJ position. ([15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11805667/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28582642/))

**Nutation** and **counternutation** describe small relative sacral-iliac motion patterns, not whole-pelvis alignment diagnoses. ([2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3512279/), [7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33256545/))

## The modern verdict

The Vleeming model remains useful because it captures a real mechanical problem: the SI joints must transfer load while resisting shear, and neither bone shape nor muscle force solves that problem alone. ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31218826/), [2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3512279/), [5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/))

The original contribution survives well:

- Joint architecture contributes passive resistance. ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31218826/), [2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3512279/), [4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2326707/))

- Compression increases resistance to shear. ([5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/), [13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18204902/))

- Muscles, fascia, and ligaments can alter stiffness. ([2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3512279/), [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40526189/), [10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14986072/), [12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15234479/))

- Stability is task-dependent. ([5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/), [14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/))

- The pelvis is a load-transfer system rather than a rigid block. ([2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3512279/), [5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/), [6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7041664/))


What has aged poorly is the certainty built around the model. Current evidence does not support identifying a specific closure deficit through palpatory SIJ mobility tests, and neither ASLR difficulty nor an immediate belt response directly measures closure. ([14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/), [15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11805667/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28582642/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25119510/), [20](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33896601/)) It does not support assuming that pain means instability or routinely increasing deep-abdominal activation. ([8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0268003324000652), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25119510/), [18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28841825/), [21](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7680716/)) Newer modeling indicates that ligament pretension and selected morphology can materially influence predicted mechanics under model assumptions, while contemporary reviews state that the structure-motion-pain relationship remains unclear. ([8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0268003324000652), [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40526189/))

The updated version is:

> **Form closure supplies anatomical opportunity. Force closure supplies adjustable compression. Neuromotor control scales the active muscular contribution. Pain and function cannot be inferred from any one of them.** ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31218826/), [5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/), [8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0268003324000652), [14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25119510/), [21](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7680716/))

## The takeaway

Form closure and force closure are **complementary load-transfer mechanisms**, not opposing systems and not clinical diagnoses. ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31218826/), [2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3512279/), [5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/), [8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0268003324000652))

Form closure describes how SIJ shape, surface contour, contact, and friction contribute to resistance against shear. Force closure describes the additional compression and tension supplied by muscles, fascia, ligaments, gravity, external load, and supports. Neuromotor control modulates the active muscular contribution for each task. ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31218826/), [2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3512279/), [5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23916048/), [14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/))

The model does not mean that the pelvis locks, that the SI joint should not move, or that one muscle holds the sacrum in place. ([1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31218826/), [2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3512279/), [7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33256545/), [8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0268003324000652)) It does not prove that a painful pelvis is unstable. ([8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0268003324000652), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25119510/), [21](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7680716/)) It does not validate named positional or muscle-failure diagnoses such as “upslip,” “rotated ilium,” or “core not firing.” ([8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0268003324000652), [18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28841825/), [20](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33896601/)) A belt response and an active straight-leg raise can provide useful functional information, but neither directly measures closure. ([14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/), [15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11805667/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28582642/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25119510/))

The central principle is:

**Effective pelvic stability is not maximum stiffness. It is enough appropriately directed stiffness for the current load, with the ability to move and relax when the demand changes.** ([13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18204902/), [14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22386280/), [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25119510/), [18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28841825/))
