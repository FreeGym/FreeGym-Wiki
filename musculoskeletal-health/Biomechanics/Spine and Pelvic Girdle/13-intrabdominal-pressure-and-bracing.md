# Intra-Abdominal Pressure and Bracing: What Actually Happens

Intra-abdominal pressure is not a muscle, a breath, or a special substance generated by the “deep core.” It is a pressure state arising inside the abdominal cavity as the diaphragm, abdominal wall, pelvic floor, posterior abdominal wall, abdominal contents, rib cage, spine, and glottis respond to gravity, respiration, acceleration, external load, and muscular force. During physical activity, the value recorded by a pressure sensor may also include effects from organ weight, impact, sensor movement, and the orientation of the body relative to gravity. ([1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00192-026-06635-2))

**Bracing** is a muscular strategy. In its usual training meaning, it involves increasing activity across multiple abdominal and spinal muscles to make the trunk more resistant to displacement. A brace may raise intra-abdominal pressure, but the two terms are not interchangeable. A person can increase pressure through forceful breathing or a Valsalva maneuver without reproducing one exact bracing pattern, and a person can increase trunk-muscle co-contraction while continuing to breathe. ([3](https://doi.org/10.1152/jappl.2000.89.3.967), [10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17207676/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37715283/))

The most useful first rule is:

> **Pressure is an output. Bracing is a strategy. Stiffness, spinal load, and pain are different variables.**

A brace can increase stiffness while increasing compression. Higher pressure can contribute to an extensor moment while requiring greater muscular effort. Less spinal movement does not automatically mean less tissue load. More pressure does not automatically mean greater strength. And pain does not tell you how much pressure, stiffness, or compression is present.

## The practical orientation map

The pressure system is often drawn as a cylinder, with the diaphragm above, pelvic floor below, abdominal wall around the sides, and lumbar spine behind. That is a useful spatial map, but the biological trunk is not a sealed steel canister.

The **diaphragm** forms the muscular boundary between the thoracic and abdominal cavities. During inspiration, its contraction changes thoracic and abdominal geometry. During postural tasks, diaphragm activity can also contribute to pressure generation before an expected disturbance. ([3](https://doi.org/10.1152/jappl.2000.89.3.967))

The **abdominal wall** includes transversus abdominis, internal oblique, external oblique, rectus abdominis, and their aponeurotic and fascial relationships. These muscles can compress the abdominal contents, generate or resist trunk moments, alter rib-pelvis geometry, and increase multidirectional trunk stiffness. ([6](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0021-9290%2898%2900129-8), [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21571410/), [10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17207676/))

The **pelvic floor** forms the lower muscular and connective-tissue boundary. It participates in continence, pelvic support, and pressure regulation, but a pressure sensor in the vagina or bladder does not directly measure the force or strain experienced by a specific pelvic-floor tissue. The 2026 measurement review emphasizes that pressure values during movement are influenced by sensor placement, gravity, acceleration, and organ loading. ([1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00192-026-06635-2))

The **posterior boundary** includes the lumbar spine, posterior abdominal wall, paraspinal musculature, thoracolumbar fascia, and adjacent connective tissues. These structures are not passive walls. They deform, transmit force, and change stiffness as muscles contract and spinal position changes.

The **glottis** is not part of the abdominal cavity, but it matters because restricting airflow changes thoracic pressure and alters how the diaphragm, abdominal wall, and pelvic floor manage force. A forceful expiratory effort against a closed or restricted glottis is central to the Valsalva maneuver. ([23](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23222073/))

The **abdominal contents** transmit pressure through a deformable, fluid-rich environment. This is not perfectly uniform hydraulic pressure. Body position, breathing phase, local muscle tension, organ weight, and acceleration all influence what a sensor records. ([1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00192-026-06635-2))

## What intra-abdominal pressure actually is

Pressure means force distributed over an area. Intra-abdominal pressure, or **IAP**, refers to the pressure within the abdominal cavity, but exercise researchers cannot normally place an unrestricted sensor directly in the free abdominal cavity during loaded movement. Instead, IAP has been estimated through sensors in the stomach, rectum, vagina, and bladder. Esophageal pressure generally estimates intrathoracic pressure; measurements from catheters with both gastric and esophageal sensors must be distinguished by sensor location. ([1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00192-026-06635-2), [3](https://doi.org/10.1152/jappl.2000.89.3.967))

That creates an interpretation problem. A gastric sensor during a deadlift, an intrarectal balloon during a squat, and an intravaginal transducer during running do not necessarily measure identical physical conditions. Sensor damping, tissue contact, local organ pressure, orientation, acceleration, and movement artifact can all influence the output. Among the studies included in the 2026 review, the measurement properties of sensors were rarely described, and none had been fully validated for dynamic physical activity. ([1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00192-026-06635-2))

The review, with searches through January 30, 2025, identified 52 eligible studies, but 28 had fewer than 20 participants. Twenty-five included only women and 16 only men. Reported pressures varied substantially across methods and activities, limiting the ability to define one normal exercise value or compare every study directly. ([1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00192-026-06635-2))

This does not make IAP research useless. It changes what the numbers can support.

A pressure recording can tell us that one condition produced a larger sensor output than another under the same measurement method. It can help compare breathing instructions, loads, exercises, or belt conditions within a study, provided changes in posture, sensor position, and movement artifacts are considered. It is much less defensible to take a pressure reported in one body cavity, convert it into a universal “safe pressure,” and apply it to every athlete, lift, spinal tissue, or pelvic floor.

The clean distinction is:

**Measured cavity pressure is not the same thing as tissue force.**

A sensor may record pressure near the pelvic floor, but it does not directly reveal the tensile strain in a ligament, the force on a specific pelvic-floor muscle, disc compression, facet-joint load, or the shear force at a lumbar segment. Those outcomes require additional measurements or biomechanical assumptions.

## What bracing actually is

An abdominal brace is usually described as increasing tension around the trunk without deliberately drawing the abdominal wall inward. In practice, it involves some combination of abdominal co-contraction, spinal-muscle activity, altered breathing, rib-cage control, pelvic-floor response, and increased pressure.

Bracing is not one standardized contraction. The cue, external load, and whether the person expects the disturbance influence the pattern of activity. Body position, training experience, breathing pattern, fatigue, and pain are additional considerations when applying these findings, rather than factors whose individual effects were all established by these comparisons. Studies that compare bracing conditions do not necessarily reproduce the exact strategy used by a powerlifter during a maximal squat. ([10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17207676/), [12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32736012/))

A brace can be:

- Light enough to permit comfortable breathing.
- Moderate and sustained during a carry or repeated set.
- High enough to restrict airflow during a heavy effort.
- Anticipatory, beginning before the load moves.
- Reactive, increasing after an unexpected disturbance.

These strategies share the goal of controlling force, but they do not create identical pressures, stiffness, compression, or respiratory effects.

## Bracing, hollowing, breath holding, and Valsalva are different

These terms are often used as though they mean the same thing.

**Abdominal hollowing** is a drawing-in maneuver in which the lower abdominal wall is pulled inward, usually at relatively low force. It is often used to develop awareness of transversus-abdominis-related activity or low-load motor control.

**Abdominal bracing** is broader co-contraction of the abdominal and trunk musculature, generally without intentionally drawing the abdomen inward. Its mechanical goal is greater whole-trunk stiffness.

**Breath holding** means airflow has stopped. A person can briefly hold their breath without producing a maximal muscular brace.

A **Valsalva maneuver** involves a forceful expiratory effort against a closed or restricted glottis. It raises intrathoracic and intra-abdominal pressure and changes cardiovascular as well as trunk mechanics. ([23](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23222073/), [24](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-026-06167-z))

A **weightlifting belt** is an external surface against which the abdominal wall can generate force. Wearing one does not automatically reproduce a deliberate brace or establish how much IAP the athlete is producing. Its effects can vary with stiffness, athlete technique, and the task. Fit and placement are practical considerations, but their independent effects were not established by these studies. ([19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40698971/), [22](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000005523))

These strategies can overlap. A lifter might inhale, brace, close the glottis, and push outward against a belt. Another person might brace while slowly exhaling. A rehabilitation patient might hollow while breathing quietly. The resulting mechanics are not interchangeable.

## What happens when you brace

### Before the external load moves

If the load or perturbation is expected, the nervous system can increase trunk-muscle activity before visible movement begins. That anticipatory activity raises baseline stiffness so the trunk deforms less when force arrives.

This does not mean one deep muscle always contracts first. Multiple abdominal and spinal muscles can be recruited according to the direction and magnitude of the expected disturbance. The activity pattern is task-specific rather than a fixed core sequence. ([9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21571410/), [11](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16996278/), [12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32736012/), [30](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18448877/))

### The abdominal wall develops tension

The abdominal muscles generate force around the abdominal contents. That force may produce flexion, rotation, lateral-bending, or anti-movement moments depending on which muscles are active and how the body is positioned. When opposing muscles contract together, they can raise stiffness without producing a large visible trunk movement.

### The diaphragm and glottis influence pressure

The diaphragm may descend or maintain a lower position while the abdominal wall resists outward deformation. If the glottis is restricted, thoracic pressure can rise and the pressure system becomes more resistant to rapid volume change. Pressure generally rises more during some breath-holding strategies than during unrestricted breathing, but the response varies across individuals and tasks. ([3](https://doi.org/10.1152/jappl.2000.89.3.967), [14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15094544/), [23](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23222073/))

### The pelvic floor responds to the pressure environment

The pelvic floor is exposed to changes in pressure and muscle activity, but it is not simply pushed downward as a passive sheet. Its response may include active contraction, deformation, or task-specific modulation. Current exercise-pressure measurements cannot convert a single IAP value directly into pelvic-floor tissue strain. ([1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00192-026-06635-2))

### Pressure pushes outward against the trunk boundaries

As pressure rises, it acts against the abdominal wall, diaphragm, pelvic floor, and posterior boundary. The abdominal muscles and any external belt provide resistance to that expansion. This can help create a more rigid trunk-pressure system, but the effect depends on body geometry and muscular force.

### Trunk stiffness rises

Muscular co-contraction increases resistance to flexion, extension, lateral bending, and rotation. Pressure itself can also contribute to lumbar stiffness. In a very small experiment using phrenic-nerve stimulation to elevate IAP while limiting normal voluntary trunk-muscle activation, lumbar stiffness increased with pressure. The unusual method and sample of three participants make this mechanism evidence, not a training prescription. ([4](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbiomech.2004.08.016))

### Motion may decrease

In a 2020 sudden-loading experiment involving 16 healthy participants, abdominal bracing increased baseline abdominal and back-muscle activity and reduced estimated flexion motion across most measured lumbar segments. Segmental angles were estimated using skin-surface markers. The absolute differences were small - generally less than one degree - and the reduction at L5-S1 was not statistically significant. ([12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32736012/))

A smaller motion response does not mean that no force reached the spine. It means the system became more resistant to displacement.

### Muscular effort rises, and compression can also rise

Muscles create force by pulling on their attachments. A brace that increases co-contraction can therefore raise spinal compression even while it improves resistance to unwanted movement. In Grenier and McGill’s study of eight healthy men, an idealized simulation based on one participant’s data produced higher modeled stability and compression during bracing than during simulated hollowing. The authors reported differences of approximately 32% and 15%, respectively, calculated relative to the bracing values. These were model-specific comparisons; the participant-derived analysis did not find a statistically significant compression difference between strategies. ([10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17207676/))

This is the central trade-off:

> **A brace can reduce motion by applying more force to the system.**

## Stiffness, stability, and loading are not synonyms

**Stiffness** is resistance to deformation. If the same external force causes less displacement, the system is stiffer.

**Stability** refers more broadly to the system maintaining controlled behavior rather than collapsing, buckling, or moving beyond a manageable range.

**Loading** includes compression, shear, torsion, bending moments, pressure, and muscle-generated forces acting on tissues.

A brace can increase stiffness and improve stability while increasing compressive loading. It can reduce one type of movement while increasing force elsewhere. ([6](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0021-9290%2898%2900129-8), [12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32736012/)) [7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20655636/) It can make the trunk easier to control while changing breathing mechanics. When the brace includes a Valsalva maneuver, cardiovascular responses also require consideration. ([16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37715283/), [23](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23222073/), [24](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-026-06167-z))

These distinctions prevent several common errors.

“More stable” does not automatically mean “less loaded.”

“Less movement” does not automatically mean “safer.”

“Higher pressure” does not automatically mean “less disc compression.”

And “neutral-looking” does not tell you how much muscle force is being generated.

## How pressure can contribute mechanically

### Pressure can create an extensor moment

When abdominal pressure acts on the diaphragm and abdominal cavity, it can contribute a moment that opposes external trunk flexion. In theory, that can reduce the amount of force the spinal extensors need to produce for a given external demand.

The magnitude of this contribution depends on the effective area, moment arm, pressure distribution, posture, abdominal-wall behavior, and model assumptions. Pressure does not produce a fixed amount of spinal unloading under every condition. ([6](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0021-9290%2898%2900129-8), [8](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16333683/)) [7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20655636/)

### Pressure can increase stiffness

Pressure creates an internally supported trunk structure that resists deformation. Experimental and modeling studies support a stiffness contribution from IAP, although estimates vary with methodology. ([4](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbiomech.2004.08.016), [6](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0021-9290%2898%2900129-8)) [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21571410/)

### Pressure interacts with abdominal-wall tension

Pressure requires containment. If the abdominal wall did not resist expansion, the cavity would simply change shape. The pressure effect is therefore inseparable in practice from muscular and fascial tension.

### Pressure can change load distribution

The lumbar spine, rib cage, pelvis, abdominal wall, and diaphragm share the mechanical consequences of pressure. This may reduce demand on one structure while increasing demand on another. “Load sharing” is more accurate than “unloading” when the exact tissue outcome is unknown.

## Does IAP unload the spine?

The evidence does not support one universal answer.

A 2010 analytical model predicted that increasing IAP from 5 to 10 kPa reduced modeled spinal compression by approximately 18% during flexion effort, 21% during extension, 29% during lateral bending, and 31% during axial rotation, at an external moment of 60 Nm in each tested direction and under the study’s assumptions. These were model outputs using optimized muscular recruitment, not direct measurements inside living lumbar discs. ([7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20655636/))

Arjmand and Shirazi-Adl used a different nonlinear model informed by measured postures and external loads. IAP produced unloading and stabilization when abdominal coactivation was absent, but the unloading effect became smaller or disappeared as modeled abdominal co-contraction increased. The result depended on posture, load and the assumed muscular strategy. ([8](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16333683/))

The two findings are not necessarily contradictory. They answer the same mechanical question using different assumptions about muscle recruitment and pressure generation.

During voluntary bracing, IAP is generated and modulated through muscular and respiratory activity, alongside passive mechanical influences. The extensor moment created by pressure may reduce some erector-spinae demand, while the muscles used to produce and contain that pressure add their own compression. Whether the final result is net unloading, unchanged compression, or greater compression depends on the task.

The defensible conclusion is:

> **IAP can contribute to spinal stiffness and load sharing. It does not guarantee spinal unloading.**

Any claim that bracing “reduces spinal load by X percent” should specify the model, posture, pressure, co-contraction assumptions, and tissue outcome being discussed.

## More pressure is not automatically better

Pressure is useful when it helps the athlete create enough stiffness for the task. Beyond that point, additional pressure may increase effort without producing meaningful performance or control benefits.

In a maximal isometric-lifting study, changing breath control altered IAP but did not significantly change maximal force. The association between pressure and force was only low to moderate. Higher measured IAP was therefore not a direct strength meter under the tested conditions. ([15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16757073/))

Abdominal bracing may have a respiratory cost. In a study of 31 healthy adults holding a load of approximately 20% of body mass, breath volume decreased during braced loaded breathing compared with the preceding unloaded breaths, despite increased diaphragm movement. The reported analyses did not directly compare the braced and unbraced loaded conditions. The study did not directly measure IAP or establish that minute ventilation was impaired. ([16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37715283/))

For a one-repetition maximum, that cost may be acceptable for a few seconds. For running, repeated Olympic lifts, high-repetition squats, or long carries, a strategy that restricts ventilation too aggressively may impair the task. This is a practical inference, not a performance finding from the lifting study. ([16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37715283/))

The better question is not “how much pressure can I produce?”

It is:

**How much pressure and stiffness does this effort require, and can I release them afterward?**

## Bracing versus hollowing

The mechanical evidence generally favors bracing when the goal is broad resistance to a substantial external disturbance.

Grenier and McGill compared hollowing and bracing under several loading conditions. In their small study and accompanying model, the brace produced a larger stability increase than the hollow. Selective transversus-abdominis activation contributed little additional stability in this model, which did not account for its interaction with IAP. ([10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17207676/))

Vera-Garcia and colleagues tested bracing and hollowing during sudden trunk perturbations. Bracing provided better control of trunk displacement, but the increased co-contraction also increased modeled spinal compression. The findings supported using enough coactivation for the expected disturbance rather than maximizing contraction without regard to the task. ([11](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16996278/))

That does not make hollowing useless. Hollowing can be used as a low-load motor-learning exercise to practice identifying abdominal-wall activity and coordinating a gentle contraction. During a highly irritable period, its tolerability should be assessed rather than assumed. Its value need not come from maximizing mechanical stiffness. ([11](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16996278/), [13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39449487/))

The clinical evidence does not establish one universally superior method. A 2024 scoping review included 22 studies but found relatively few direct comparisons between hollowing and bracing and substantial variation in participants, exercises, comparators, and outcomes. Selected studies compared programs incorporating these techniques with conventional rehabilitation or strengthening; improvements in pain, function, or muscle measures depended on the study and outcome and did not consistently demonstrate superiority between techniques. The review could not establish a clear general superiority. ([13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39449487/))

The practical division is:

**Use hollowing when selective low-load awareness solves a real problem. Use bracing when broad stiffness is required. Do not mistake either for a universal treatment.**

## Anticipated versus unexpected loading

Preparation changes the cost of stability.

If the athlete knows when and where the force is coming from, muscular activity can be organized before the load arrives. That may allow enough stiffness with less reactive displacement.

If the disturbance is unexpected, the trunk initially depends on baseline stiffness and then on reflexive muscular responses. A moderate pre-brace can reduce movement, but keeping maximal co-contraction continuously would be energetically expensive and could restrict useful motion.

Perturbation studies show that bracing raises baseline trunk-muscle activity and restricts lumbar movement. They do not demonstrate that everyone should walk, sit, or perform every repetition with a maximal brace. ([11](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16996278/), [12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32736012/))

A capable trunk can:

1. Predict force when prediction is possible.
2. Generate appropriate stiffness rapidly.
3. Tolerate small movement when movement is harmless or necessary.
4. Increase force if the disturbance grows.
5. Reduce tension once the demand ends.

That is adaptability - not permanent rigidity.

## Bracing and breathing can coexist

A brace does not require complete breath holding.

At low or moderate load, the abdominal wall can remain active while the diaphragm continues moving and air continues flowing. Pressure and lung volume may change, but respiratory and postural functions can occur together. ([3](https://doi.org/10.1152/jappl.2000.89.3.967), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37715283/))

As demand rises, the athlete may progressively restrict airflow. The strategy exists on a spectrum:

**Continuous breathing:** lowest interruption to ventilation, usually lower peak pressure.

**Breathing behind the brace:** trunk muscles remain active while smaller breaths occur.

**Inspiratory hold:** an inhalation is held during the effort, often producing higher IAP than unrestricted breathing under comparable laboratory tasks.

**Expiratory brace:** abdominal tension is increased during exhalation; a forceful exhale can generate substantial pressure.

**Valsalva maneuver:** a forceful expiratory effort occurs against a closed or highly restricted glottis, generating high thoracic and abdominal pressures. ([14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15094544/), [15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16757073/), [23](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23222073/))

In Hagins and colleagues’ lifting study, an inhalation-hold produced higher peak IAP than natural breathing, an exhalation-hold, or an inhale-exhale condition. Load also affected pressure magnitude. The sample included 11 asymptomatic participants, so the exact hierarchy should not be assumed to apply identically to every athlete and lift. ([14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15094544/))

The correct strategy depends on whether the immediate priority is maximal stiffness, repeated force production, ventilation, symptom tolerance, or cardiovascular restraint.

## The Valsalva maneuver: useful and physiologically expensive

A Valsalva maneuver can increase trunk rigidity during a demanding effort by raising intrathoracic and intra-abdominal pressure. It is common during heavy resistance exercise, particularly when the load approaches maximal capacity. ([2](https://doi.org/10.5114/biolsport.2019.88759), [23](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23222073/))

It also changes venous return, arterial pressure, cerebral hemodynamics, and the pressure surrounding blood vessels. That makes its cardiovascular interpretation more complicated than reading a single intra-arterial blood-pressure number. A 2026 commentary emphasizes that **transmural pressure** - the pressure inside a vessel relative to the pressure outside it - matters when interpreting the vascular stress produced during resistance exercise. ([25](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42258580/))

In a 2026 crossover experiment involving 15 healthy untrained adults, carotid-femoral pulse-wave velocity increased significantly from baseline after resistance exercise with repeated Valsalva maneuvers and after repeated Valsalva alone, but did not significantly change after exercise with paced breathing. Usable pulse-wave-velocity data were available for 14 participants. Measurements were taken after five minutes of supine rest following each condition. The study examined an acute response; it did not establish how long the changes persisted or assess cardiovascular events or long-term harm. ([24](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-026-06167-z))

The correct conclusion is neither that Valsalva is universally dangerous nor that it is irrelevant to health.

For a healthy trained athlete performing a brief maximal effort, a short Valsalva may be a purposeful performance strategy. As a precaution, someone with relevant cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, retinal, neurological, postoperative, or pelvic-floor concerns should seek individualized medical or rehabilitation guidance before choosing a high-pressure strategy. ([23](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23222073/), [24](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-026-06167-z))

A heavy single and a twenty-repetition set should not automatically use the same breathing strategy.

## IAP during squats and deadlifts

Resistance exercises involving large loads and substantial trunk extensor demand often produce high pressure readings. A 2019 systematic review reported the largest values in squat studies, with deadlift, rowing, and leg-press tasks also producing substantial pressures. Exercise type, external load, posture, measurement location, and breathing technique all influenced the results. ([2](https://doi.org/10.5114/biolsport.2019.88759))

Those absolute values should now be interpreted through the 2026 measurement review. Different studies used gastric, rectal and other sensors, often in small sex-specific samples. Dynamic readings also include effects from acceleration, gravity, organ weight, and sensor properties. ([1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00192-026-06635-2))

The robust conclusion is comparative rather than absolute:

- Heavier efforts generally require greater trunk-muscle activity and tend to produce higher recorded pressures.
- Exercise selection changes the pressure demand.
- Breath control changes the pressure response.
- Individual technique creates substantial variability.
- A pressure measured in one study is not a universal safety threshold.

There is no established target such as “produce exactly this many millimetres of mercury to protect L5-S1.”

## There is no established universal optimal IAP number

An ideal pressure would have to balance several outcomes simultaneously:

- Enough stiffness to control the external load.
- Enough movement for the task.
- Acceptable spinal and pelvic loading.
- Acceptable respiratory restriction.
- Acceptable cardiovascular demand.
- Low enough effort to avoid unnecessary fatigue.
- A strategy that can be repeated across the intended set.

The studies reviewed here have not established one pressure value that optimizes all of those outcomes across people, lifts, intensities, and tissues.

Even measuring the value reliably during dynamic exercise remains difficult. ([1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00192-026-06635-2))

Pressure should therefore be treated as one part of the strategy, not as a performance score. An athlete can brace effectively without knowing the numerical value. Conversely, a high recorded pressure does not prove that the spine was well positioned, that the force was distributed efficiently, or that the lift was safe.

## What a weightlifting belt actually changes

A belt provides a relatively stiff external boundary around part of the trunk. When the abdominal wall expands or contracts against it, the belt can provide resistance, tactile feedback, and a larger surface against which force can be directed.

A belt provides passive mechanical support and can contribute to trunk stiffness in addition to effects associated with increased IAP. Its effects interact with the athlete’s muscular and respiratory strategy. This helps explain why belt effects vary across athletes and instructions. ([29](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10552322/))

Classic lifting studies reported that belts could raise measured IAP during squat and lifting tasks, but these studies were small and used older measurement methods. The 2026 measurement review makes clear that absolute pressure comparisons across sensor types should be interpreted cautiously. ([1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00192-026-06635-2), [17](https://doi.org/10.1080/00140139008927106), [18](https://doi.org/10.1249/00005768-198904000-00012))

### A belt does not automatically reduce muscle activity

In a 2025 study of 28 healthy men squatting at 75% of predicted one-repetition maximum, deliberate abdominal bracing increased abdominal-muscle activity. Wearing a stabilization belt by itself did not produce the same change in measured activation, and combining the belt with bracing remained dependent on the athlete’s voluntary strategy. ([19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40698971/))

That result argues against two opposite myths:

- A belt does not automatically “switch on” the core.
- A belt does not necessarily make the trunk muscles stop working.

The athlete’s behavior inside the belt matters.

### A belt can alter kinematics and muscular distribution

A 2025 study of 17 weightlifters compared no belt, a flexible belt, and a stiff belt during deadlifts and back squats. Belt conditions changed lumbar angle and the measured activity of several back muscles. The authors inferred that the changes might reduce spinal load, but spinal compression and tissue load were not measured directly. ([20](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12283-025-00494-8))

The conflict-of-interest disclosure is important: **both authors were employees of Decathlon, which commercialized one of the two belts tested.** The findings should therefore be interpreted as a small biomechanics study with a direct commercial relationship, not as independent proof that a belt protects the lumbar spine. ([20](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12283-025-00494-8))

### Belts may improve squat performance

A 2026 crossover study involving 49 resistance-trained adults of both sexes, with differing prior experience of belt use, found that participants completed more squat repetitions and had a shorter concentric phase when wearing a belt. Participants also reported feeling more stable, but force-plate measures of postural stability did not improve. ([21](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41860443/))

Another 2026 study involving 25 resistance-trained athletes found that wearing a belt increased measured back-squat one-repetition maximum and bar velocity at matched absolute loads. The relative load-velocity relationship was not substantially affected below 85% of one-repetition maximum, although velocities were slightly lower with a belt at relative loads of 85-100%. Belted and unbelted velocity comparisons therefore depend on whether loads are matched in kilograms or as percentages of the one-repetition maximum measured in each condition. ([22](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000005523))

These studies support a modest ergogenic effect. They do not prove the mechanism. Improved performance could reflect greater pressure, altered technique, tactile feedback, confidence, familiarity, or several factors together.

### Belts have not been shown to make heavy lifting injury-proof

The available studies largely measure pressure, electromyography, kinematics, repetitions, velocity, perceived stability, or short-term performance. They do not establish that belt use reduces long-term back-injury incidence in trained lifters.

That absence matters. “A belt lets me lift more” and “a belt prevents injury” are different claims.

A belt is best understood as a performance and force-management tool - not personal protective equipment that overrides fatigue, poor load selection, inadequate recovery, or tissue tolerance.

## Belts and unbelted strength are not opposites

Training with a belt does not automatically make the trunk weak. Wearing a belt still involves abdominal and spinal-muscle activity, and deliberate bracing can increase that activity. These acute measurements do not establish how habitual belt use affects long-term trunk strength or training adaptations. ([19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40698971/), [20](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12283-025-00494-8))

Unbelted training can still be valuable because it changes the sensory and mechanical environment. An athlete may use unbelted work to practice force control without the external boundary, while using a belt for heavier or more competition-specific work.

The evidence reviewed here does not establish a rule that every athlete must earn a particular unbelted load before using a belt. Nor does it establish that every set should be belted. Belt use should follow the demands of the sport, the athlete’s preferences, and the intended training stimulus.

For powerlifting, belted and unbelted squats may reasonably be treated as related but distinct skills.

## Pressure and the pelvic floor

Intra-abdominal pressure is frequently discussed as though it directly pushes on the pelvic floor with a known force. That is too simple.

A biomechanical assessment of pelvic-floor tissue loading would need to consider anatomy, muscle activity, organ support, pressure distribution, body position, acceleration, continence strategy, fatigue, and the direction and duration of force. This is a framework for interpretation, not a list of effects all established by cavity-pressure measurements. A pressure recorded by an intravaginal or bladder sensor does not directly quantify tissue strain, levator-ani force, ligament loading, or prolapse progression. ([1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00192-026-06635-2))

The 2026 scoping review was written partly because physical-activity pressure values have often been interpreted more confidently than the measurement science justifies. Its conclusion was not that pressure is irrelevant; it was that the output needs more precise terminology and better validation. ([1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00192-026-06635-2))

This means neither of the following is evidence-based:

- “Heavy lifting always damages the pelvic floor because IAP is high.”
- “A strong pelvic floor makes every pressure level harmless.”

Pelvic-floor symptoms such as leakage, heaviness, bulging, pelvic pain, or difficulty relaxing deserve a specific assessment. For women with pelvic-floor symptoms, NICE recommends an individual history and assessment guided by the symptoms described. Generic advice to brace harder, breathe out on every repetition, or avoid all heavy lifting does not establish what the individual pelvic floor needs. ([32](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK579556/))

## Bracing can spread force beyond the lumbar spine

The following implications for other joints and sporting tasks are biomechanical hypotheses, rather than outcomes demonstrated by the lumbar studies. ([11](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16996278/), [12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32736012/))

A deliberate brace changes more than the abdominal wall. Increased trunk rigidity could alter hip, knee, shoulder, and whole-body mechanics because adjacent regions now move against a stiffer trunk.

That can be useful when the goal is efficient force transfer. It may be less useful when the task depends on fluid trunk motion, shock absorption, or rapid breathing.

The optimal brace for a squat is not necessarily the optimal brace for sprinting, landing, swimming, throwing, or a high-repetition conditioning circuit. The external force direction and the movement objective change.

The cue “brace as hard as possible” is therefore incomplete. It lacks a task.

## Bracing and spinal position

A brace does not guarantee a neutral spine.

Muscular stiffness makes the current configuration more resistant to change. If the athlete braces in flexion, extension, rotation, or lateral bending, the brace may help maintain that configuration. Whether that position is appropriate depends on the lift, range, load, symptoms, and training goal.

Nor does visible spinal motion prove that the brace failed. No biological spine is perfectly motionless under load. Segmental movements can be small and distributed even when the trunk looks fixed externally.

The useful question is:

**Did the athlete control the intended position and tolerate the resulting load?**

That is more informative than asking whether the spine remained geometrically perfect.

## Bracing and pain

A stronger brace can be compared with a lighter brace during the same task to assess symptom response. If symptoms improve, limiting a provocative motion, changing load distribution, increasing confidence, or making the task feel more predictable are possible explanations. The symptom change would not establish which, if any, of these mechanisms was responsible. ([10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17207676/), [11](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16996278/), [28](https://doi.org/10.1097/j.pain.0000000000001939))

Maximal co-contraction may increase effort, compression, or respiratory restriction. Whether it changes fear, sensitivity, or symptoms should be assessed rather than inferred from those mechanical effects. ([10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17207676/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37715283/), [28](https://doi.org/10.1097/j.pain.0000000000001939))

Pain cannot reveal which mechanism occurred. The modern IASP definition separates pain from nociception and recognizes that pain is shaped by biological, psychological, and social influences rather than serving as a direct meter of tissue stress. ([28](https://doi.org/10.1097/j.pain.0000000000001939))

A painful lift does not prove insufficient IAP. A painless lift does not prove that pressure was optimal. For persistent pain, assessment should consider loading history, neurological findings, movement tolerance, sleep, stress, confidence, and other relevant factors. ([28](https://doi.org/10.1097/j.pain.0000000000001939), [31](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK569960/))

## Bracing is not a uniquely superior back-pain treatment

Motor-control and stabilization exercises can help people with chronic nonspecific low-back pain, particularly compared with minimal intervention. But they generally have not shown large, consistent superiority over other appropriately delivered exercise approaches.

A Cochrane review of 29 trials and 2,431 participants found that motor-control exercise was more effective than minimal intervention, while differences from other exercise or manual therapy were generally small or not clinically important. ([26](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD012004))

A broader Cochrane review of 249 trials and 24,486 participants found moderate-certainty evidence that exercise probably improves pain in chronic nonspecific low-back pain compared with no treatment, usual care, or placebo. The average improvement in functional limitations was smaller and did not meet the review’s prespecified threshold for clinical importance. ([27](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD009790.pub2))

These reviews did not test “bracing” as one uniform intervention, but they set the clinical context. Improving someone’s ability to brace can be a task-specific goal. It does not prove that insufficient pressure or spinal instability was the cause of their pain.

The eventual goal should usually be greater activity and task capacity - not permanent abdominal contraction.

## The assessment implication

An evidence-informed bracing assessment should not begin by trying to estimate a hidden pressure number.

It should examine what the person can do. The following assessment and programming suggestions are practical principles, not a validated protocol.

### Can the person produce trunk tension deliberately?

The athlete should be able to increase abdominal and spinal-muscle tension without necessarily sucking the abdomen inward. Palpation and observation can provide qualitative feedback about the attempted contraction, but they cannot quantify IAP or spinal compression.

### Can the person scale the effort?

A useful brace should have more than an on and off setting. The person should be able to produce low, moderate, and high tension according to the task.

### Can the person breathe under submaximal load?

During carries, rows, split squats, or endurance exercises, the athlete should often be able to maintain enough stiffness while continuing to ventilate. If breathing disrupts control, a practical next step is to trial a less forceful brace or a lower load.

### Can the person create a brief high-pressure strategy?

For sports involving heavy lifting, the athlete may need to generate a strong brace and tolerate temporary airflow restriction. That is a specific skill rather than a resting posture.

### Can the person release the brace?

Once the force demand ends, practice releasing unnecessary tension and returning to comfortable breathing. Relaxation is part of motor control.

### Does the brace change the relevant task?

Retest the squat, hinge, carry, press, or painful movement. A cue is useful when it improves performance, control, confidence, or tolerance. That response does not prove exactly which internal mechanism changed.

### Are symptoms present outside ordinary training discomfort?

Dizziness, fainting, chest pain, severe headache, visual symptoms, unusual breathlessness, neurological changes, or pelvic-floor symptoms belong outside a simple core-cue discussion and warrant appropriate clinical assessment. ([23](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23222073/), [32](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK579556/)) In particular, dizziness, excessive breathlessness, chest pain or pressure, or palpitations during resistance training warrant stopping exercise and immediate medical evaluation, with medical clearance before resuming. ([33](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38059362/))

## The programming implication

### Begin with the demand, not the cue

A maximal deadlift, moderate hypertrophy set, running interval, suitcase carry, and rehabilitation hinge require different combinations of pressure, stiffness, movement, and ventilation.

Define the task first. Then select the brace.

### Build a range of strategies

A useful progression includes:

**Low-tension control:** quiet breathing with enough trunk activity to organize position.

**Submaximal loaded breathing:** maintaining ventilation during carries, rows, hinges, or squats.

**Moderate bracing:** stronger co-contraction while allowing small breaths between or during repetitions.

**High-pressure efforts:** brief breath holding or Valsalva when the load and health context justify it.

**Recovery:** deliberate release of pressure and muscular tension after the repetition or set.

The percentages are not standardized. The goal is qualitative control and progressive task exposure.

### Practice bracing before maximal loading

The first attempt at a high-pressure brace should not occur during a near-maximal lift. Athletes can learn how the abdomen expands, how the rib cage and pelvis organize, and how to release the brace with moderate loads.

### Treat the belt as feedback

A belt can give the athlete a surface to expand into. The cue does not have to be “push the stomach forward.” Depending on the athlete, useful expansion may occur anteriorly, laterally, posteriorly, or circumferentially.

“Expand into the belt” is a sensory instruction, not proof that pressure is equal in all directions.

### Use unbelted and belted work deliberately

Unbelted work can be used to practice force control without external support. Belted work can be used to practice sport-specific skill and may permit higher force output. The choice should reflect the training phase and target lift. ([21](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41860443/), [22](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000005523))

### Do not brace maximally through every exercise

A maximal brace during low-load accessory work may create unnecessary fatigue and restrict breathing. A useful aim is to learn how much tension the task actually requires. ([16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37715283/))

Use the least costly strategy that reliably controls the intended load.

## What IAP and bracing are not

IAP is **not air trapped inside the abdomen**. It is a pressure state involving muscles, tissues, contents, posture, respiration, and external forces.

IAP is **not a direct measure of disc pressure, spinal compression, or pelvic-floor strain**. Different tissues experience different loads, and dynamic cavity sensors have important validation limitations. ([1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00192-026-06635-2))

Bracing is **not the same as hollowing**. Hollowing emphasizes drawing inward; bracing emphasizes broader co-contraction.

Bracing is **not automatically a Valsalva maneuver**. It can occur with continuous breathing.

A Valsalva maneuver is **not required for every repetition**. Its usefulness rises with force demand, while its respiratory and cardiovascular costs also matter.

A belt is **not a replacement for active muscular control**. It changes the external boundary against which the athlete braces.

A belt is **not proven injury insurance**. Existing studies demonstrate mechanical and performance effects, not guaranteed injury prevention.

More pressure is **not always better**. Pressure is contained by active muscle forces, passive tissue tension, and any external support; strategies that increase pressure can also restrict ventilation.

More stiffness is **not always better**. Movement, breathing, shock absorption, and rotation are necessary in many tasks.

Less spinal motion is **not the same as less spinal loading**.

Pain is **not proof that the person failed to brace**. ([28](https://doi.org/10.1097/j.pain.0000000000001939))

## Working vocabulary for Tier 2

**Intra-abdominal pressure** is pressure recorded within or used to represent the abdominal cavity. During dynamic activity, measured values are influenced by sensor type and location, body orientation, acceleration, gravity, organ weight, respiration, and muscular activity. ([1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00192-026-06635-2))

**Abdominal bracing** is broad co-contraction of abdominal and trunk muscles intended to increase trunk stiffness. It does not inherently require drawing the abdomen inward or holding the breath.

**Abdominal hollowing** is a lower-load drawing-in maneuver often used to emphasize selective abdominal-wall control. Its mechanical effect is not equivalent to whole-trunk bracing.

**Co-contraction** means simultaneous activity in muscles producing different or opposing moments. It can increase multidirectional stiffness while also increasing compression and energy cost.

**Spinal stiffness** is resistance to spinal displacement under load.

**Spinal stability** is controlled mechanical behavior under load or perturbation. It is not synonymous with immobility.

**Spinal loading** includes compression, shear, torsion, bending moments, and muscle-generated forces acting on spinal tissues.

An **extensor moment** is a turning effect that opposes trunk flexion. IAP can contribute to an extensor moment, but the magnitude depends on geometry and muscular strategy.

A **breath hold** is cessation of airflow. It does not specify how much pressure or abdominal contraction is present.

A **Valsalva maneuver** is a forceful expiratory effort against a closed or restricted glottis, raising thoracic and abdominal pressures. ([23](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23222073/))

**Intrathoracic pressure** is pressure within the thoracic cavity. It changes substantially during breath holding and Valsalva and affects cardiovascular interpretation.

**Transmural pressure** is the pressure inside a vessel relative to the pressure surrounding it. It is more directly related to vessel-wall stress than intra-arterial pressure alone. ([25](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42258580/))

A **weightlifting belt** is an external trunk boundary that may alter pressure, sensory feedback, muscle activity, kinematics, and performance. Its passive mechanical support interacts with muscular and respiratory force.

**Pressure management** is a coaching term for the ability to generate, contain, direct, tolerate, and release trunk pressure according to the task.

## The modern verdict

The evidence supports several strong conclusions.

Intra-abdominal pressure is a real contributor to trunk mechanics. Experimental and modeling studies show that it can increase lumbar stiffness and contribute to an extensor moment. ([4](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbiomech.2004.08.016), [8](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16333683/)) [7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20655636/)

In the laboratory loading and perturbation tasks studied, abdominal bracing produced greater modeled stability or better control of trunk displacement than selective hollowing. ([10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17207676/), [11](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16996278/))

That stiffness is not free. Bracing also increases muscular effort and can increase spinal compression and change breathing mechanics. ([10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17207676/), [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37715283/))

IAP may unload some spinal structures under some modeling assumptions, but its net effect depends on abdominal co-contraction, posture, load direction and the muscles used to generate the pressure. ([7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20655636/), [8](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16333683/))

Exercise pressure values are much less clean than popular explanations suggest. The 2026 review found major variation in sensor placement, validation, sample size, body position and interpretation. ([1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00192-026-06635-2))

Belts can alter pressure, kinematics, muscular activity and performance. Recent studies suggest modest squat-performance benefits, but mechanisms remain uncertain, and injury prevention has not been established. ([17](https://doi.org/10.1080/00140139008927106), [18](https://doi.org/10.1249/00005768-198904000-00012), [19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40698971/), [22](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000005523))

What the evidence does **not** establish is equally important.

It does not establish one universal optimal IAP number.

It does not establish one perfect bracing cue.

It does not show that maximum pressure produces maximum force.

It does not show that less spinal movement always means less tissue load.

It does not show that everyone with back pain has insufficient core stiffness.

It does not show that a belt makes the trunk inactive.

It does not show that a belt prevents back injury.

It does not show that every high-pressure effort damages the pelvic floor.

The updated model is:

> **The nervous system uses muscular force, respiration, pressure, posture, and sometimes external support to create enough trunk stiffness for the task. The useful strategy is the one that controls the load without imposing unnecessary mechanical, respiratory, or physiological cost.**

## The takeaway

Intra-abdominal pressure and bracing are related, but they are not the same thing.

**IAP is an output of the trunk-pressure system. Bracing is a muscular control strategy.** A brace can raise pressure, increase stiffness and reduce unwanted movement. It can also increase compression, muscular effort and respiratory restriction. When combined with a Valsalva maneuver, it also changes cardiovascular responses.

Pressure can contribute to spinal load sharing, but it does not automatically unload the spine. A belt can improve performance and contribute mechanical support, but it does not replace active control or guarantee protection. Hollowing can teach low-load control, while bracing has generally been more effective for modeled stability or control of displacement in the loading and perturbation tasks studied. Neither is universally superior for every clinical presentation or movement.

For training and rehabilitation, a reasonable practical rule is:

> **Create enough brace for the load, continue breathing when the task permits it, use higher-pressure strategies when the demand justifies them, and release the tension when the effort ends.**

The goal is not maximal pressure.

The goal is **adaptable force control**.
