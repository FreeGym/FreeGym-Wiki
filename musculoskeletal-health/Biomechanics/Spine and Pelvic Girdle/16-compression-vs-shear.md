# Compression vs Shear: Why the Same Load Isn’t the Same Stress

The spine does not experience one generic quantity called “load.” Every lumbar motion segment is exposed to a combination of forces and moments: compression, anterior-posterior shear, mediolateral shear, flexion-extension moments, lateral-bending moments, and torsion. The combination changes from one spinal level to another and from one instant of a lift to the next. [26](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14706323/) [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36606738/)

That is why two people can lift the same barbell and experience different estimated spinal forces. It is also why two techniques using the same weight can produce similar compression but different shear, or lower one shear component while raising another. In one asymmetric-lifting study, increasing asymmetry raised compression and lateral shear while anterior-posterior shear decreased. The load had not become simply “higher” or “lower”; its internal direction had changed. [10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9629066/)

A useful first rule is:

> **The weight in the hand is not the force inside the spine, and the force inside the spine is not the stress inside one tissue.**

External mass is only the beginning of the calculation. Internal loading also depends on moment arms, body-segment weight, acceleration, muscular force, co-contraction, spinal orientation, disc geometry, facet contact, abdominal pressure, and the way the nervous system distributes force across the trunk and hips. Musculoskeletal models consistently show that changes in muscle leverage or recruitment assumptions can produce large differences in estimated compression and shear. [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36606738/)

## The practical force map

Several terms need to remain separate when interpreting musculoskeletal models, finite-element analyses, and segment testing. [8](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32147242/) [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36606738/) [26](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14706323/)

**External load** is force or moment applied from outside the body. In a lift, external forces include gravity and contact forces from the barbell or object and the ground. Acceleration affects the forces and moments required to produce the movement.

**Internal force** is the force transmitted through muscles, joints, discs, facets, ligaments, bones, and other tissues while the body manages the external demand.

**Compression** acts mainly toward the opposing surfaces of a motion segment, approximately perpendicular to the local disc or endplate plane.

**Shear** acts approximately parallel to that local plane, creating a tendency for one vertebra to translate relative to the next.

**Moment**, or torque, is the turning effect of a force around an axis. A load can create a large flexion moment without being especially heavy if it is held far from the spine.

**Stress** is internal force per unit area on a specified plane. Force divided by area gives an average stress; local stresses may vary across the tissue. A force of 2,000 N acting across a large area does not create the same average stress as 2,000 N concentrated across a smaller area.

**Strain** describes deformation relative to a reference configuration, including relative length change and angular distortion.

These quantities answer different questions. A model may estimate a 5-kN compressive joint-reaction force, but that value does not tell us how much stress appears in one part of the endplate, how much annular fiber strain occurs posterolaterally, or how much force is being carried by the facets. Detailed posture modeling has shown that global compression, intradiscal pressure, facet force, passive moment, and annular fiber strain can change by very different percentages during the same movement comparison. [8](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32147242/)

## Compression: force toward the segment

Compression tends to press adjacent vertebral bodies and the disc-endplate complex together. It is a normal part of weight-bearing activities such as standing, walking, running, and lifting, and muscle force contributes to it. [4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10222525/) [15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19493597/)

Compression is not produced only by the object being lifted. Trunk muscles usually generate substantial force to oppose the external moment. Because many spinal muscles have relatively short moment arms, they may need to generate forces much greater than the external weight to control the trunk. Their force then contributes to spinal compression. This is one reason a 20-kg object can be associated with internal spinal forces far greater than the object’s weight. [4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10222525/) [15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19493597/)

Compression is also direction-sensitive. In an ex-vivo experiment, a lumbar spine supported a much larger compressive load when the load followed the curvature of the spine and remained close to the centers of rotation (the “follower-load” concept) than when compression was applied along a simple vertical path that encouraged buckling. The experiment shows that the same magnitude of compression can produce different mechanical behavior depending on its line of action. It does not prove that the living spine literally carries one perfect follower load during exercise. [14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10332793/)

Compression can contribute to local segment stiffness and resistance to translation. Whether it also improves whole-spine stability depends on the load path and boundary conditions. That does not make compression universally protective. The same force can increase disc pressure, endplate stress, vertebral stress, and facet contact depending on posture and load sharing. [6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12472249/) [26](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14706323/) [14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10332793/) Repeated lifting can also produce muscle fatigue and changes in movement. [30](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12923468/)

The correct conclusion is neither “compression is bad” nor “compression stabilizes everything.” It is:

> **Compression is a normal load component whose effect depends on magnitude, direction, distribution, duration, tissue capacity, and the other forces acting at the same time.**

## Shear: force along the segment

Shear acts parallel to the local disc or endplate plane. In the lumbar spine, it is usually divided into:

**Anterior-posterior shear**, which acts forward or backward relative to the vertebra.

**Mediolateral shear**, which acts side to side.

The direction matters. An anterior shear force is not mechanically interchangeable with posterior shear, and sagittal-plane shear is not interchangeable with lateral shear.

Shear also depends on the coordinate system. Imagine a vertically directed resultant force passing through a tilted L5-S1 segment. Relative to the room, the force is vertical. Relative to the inclined disc plane, part of that force acts as compression and part acts as shear. In a simplified two-dimensional example:

\[ \text{Compression} \approx F\cos(\theta) \]\[ \text{Shear} \approx F\sin(\theta) \]

where \(F\) is the resultant force and \(\theta\) is its angle relative to the local compression axis. The real spine is more complicated because muscles, facets, ligaments, translations, and three-dimensional geometry affect the resultant forces, while bending and torsion also contribute moments.

This local-coordinate issue is why changing lumbar or sacral orientation can alter estimated shear even if the global load looks similar. Modeling of static lifting found that posture-dependent changes in disc inclination and extensor-muscle lines of action changed shear across lumbar levels. [25](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16319750/)

Shear is not the same thing as one vertebra visibly “slipping out.” Relative vertebral translation is one component of measured lumbar motion; shear force alone does not specify how far one vertebra moves. The disc, facets, ligaments, and segment geometry contribute to the mechanical resistance to translation. [5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24345591/) [7](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0021929013003199) [26](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14706323/)

## Compression and shear travel through different tissue pathways

Cadaveric testing of thoracolumbar motion segments illustrates why compression and shear should not be treated as interchangeable force components. Under compression, investigators measured relatively high disc pressure and endplate deformation. Under shear, disc pressure and central endplate deformation were lower, while vertebral-rim strains were generally greater. The authors interpreted compression as being transmitted strongly through disc pressure and endplate loading, while shear appeared to rely more heavily on the annulus fibrosus and peripheral structures. [6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12472249/)

That experiment used isolated cadaveric T12-L1 and L1-L2 segments loaded to 500 N. It did not include living muscle activity, abdominal pressure, adaptation, or pain. It establishes a basic mechanical difference, not the injury risk of a particular barbell lift.

Finite-element work at L4-L5 similarly suggests that shear is shared among the disc, facets, and other posterior structures rather than being carried by one tissue. The estimated proportions changed with shear magnitude, compressive preload, posture, facet spacing, and the time allowed for viscoelastic response. The facets carried a substantial proportion of the modeled anterior shear, while the relative disc contribution increased under conditions such as flexion, lower compressive preload, and larger facet gaps. [7](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0021929013003199)

Compression can therefore alter how shear is resisted. A compressive preload may increase joint stiffness, bring facet surfaces into greater contact, or change annular and ligamentous tension. This is one reason it is misleading to evaluate shear as though the spine were unloaded in every other direction.

## Force is not stress

A joint-reaction force is usually reported in newtons or kilonewtons. Tissue stress is commonly reported in pascals or megapascals; force divided by area gives an average stress over the specified area.

That distinction matters because the same estimated compressive force can create different stresses in different people. Vertebral size, endplate area, disc shape, bone density, degeneration, curvature, and regional load distribution can influence the local result. [19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10593417/) [8](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32147242/)

Human vertebral testing demonstrates substantial variability in compressive strength. In 101 L3 vertebrae from adults aged 18 to 96 years, bone-density measures explained approximately 75% to 86% of the variance in maximum compressive load or stress, depending on the measurement method. The same nominal spinal force therefore does not represent the same proportion of failure tolerance for every person. [19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10593417/)

Human shear testing shows a similar problem. In anterior-shear testing of L2-L3 cadaveric motion segments held in neutral posture under a 500-N compressive preload, peak shear force correlated positively with trabecular bone mineral density in specimens tested without prior axial creep. The study detected no significant age-group difference within its sample. [18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22578739/)

Even average stress does not describe the entire tissue state. Endplates, annular fibers, vertebral rims, facets, and ligaments experience nonuniform local stresses. A single whole-segment number can hide a much higher concentration in one region.

The practical correction is:

> **Kilonewtons describe force. Megapascals are units of stress or pressure. Neither number alone describes tissue damage or pain.**

## Intradiscal pressure is not total compression

Intradiscal pressure is often used as shorthand for spinal loading, but it measures one part of the system.

The classic Wilke experiment placed a pressure transducer inside the L4-L5 nucleus pulposus of one 45-year-old man and recorded pressure during daily activities. Relaxed standing produced approximately 0.5 MPa, forward-flexed standing about 1.1 MPa, and lifting a 20-kg object with a rounded flexed back about 2.3 MPa. The same object produced approximately 1.7 MPa when lifted with flexed knees and an upright trunk. Holding it close to the chest produced about 1.1 MPa, compared with about 1.8 MPa when held 60 cm from the chest. [4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10222525/)

Those observations are valuable because they directly measured a living human disc. They also have strict limitations:

- There was one participant.
- One nondegenerate disc was measured.
- Pressure was recorded at one location in the nucleus.
- The experiment did not directly measure total segmental compression, shear, annular stress, facet loading, or injury risk.

Pressure in megapascals cannot be converted directly into total compressive force without assumptions about the effective load-bearing area and pressure distribution. Nor does a modest change in pressure prove that every other structure changed by the same amount.

Posture modeling in static lifting tasks makes this divergence clear. Compared with a kyphotic lifting posture, a lordotic posture raised estimated segmental compression by up to 46.1% and shear by up to 47.5%, yet increased intradiscal pressure at L5-S1 by only about 1.8% to 3.4%. Estimated maximum disc-fiber strain at L5-S1 decreased by about 0.4% to 4.7%, while facet force and muscle activity changed more substantially. [8](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32147242/)

The sentence “this position produces more disc pressure” is therefore not equivalent to “this position produces proportionally more total spinal load.”

## External moment: why distance can matter as much as weight

The basic moment relationship is:

\[ M = F \times d \]

where \(M\) is moment, \(F\) is force, and \(d\) is the perpendicular distance from the force’s line of action to the joint or axis. [25](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16319750/)

A 20-kg object held close to the body produces a smaller external flexion moment than the same object held at arm’s length. The back and hip extensors therefore require less counter-moment, generally reducing the muscular forces contributing to spinal compression. Wilke’s direct pressure measurements illustrate the practical magnitude that changing object distance can have, although the findings came from one person. [4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10222525/)

Object bulk matters for the same reason. A light but awkward object can create a substantial external moment if its center of mass must be held far forward. A heavier compact object close to the body may create a similar or even smaller moment.

This is why programming and workplace discussions should not treat kilograms alone as the mechanical dose. The relevant combination includes:

- Weight.
- Horizontal reach.
- Trunk and pelvic orientation.
- Vertical starting height.
- Movement speed.
- Acceleration.
- Asymmetry.
- Repetition count and duration.

In a study of 39 adults performing lifting tasks, object mass, bulk, distance from the feet, off-axis placement, and movement speed all influenced bending or compressive demands. Stoop lifting produced a lower estimated extensor moment than squat lifting in that experiment but a much higher passive bending torque. No technique minimized every quantity simultaneously. [22](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7962011/)

## Compression and shear do not have to peak together

During a lifting movement, compression and shear can reach their maximum values at different positions.

In a study using subject-specific MRI models and dual-fluoroscopic lumbar kinematics, three participants performed a weight-lifting extension while holding 15 lb in total, with 8 lb in the right hand and 7 lb in the left. Estimated L3-L4 disc shear was highest in the flexed position, approximately 230 N, while maximum disc compression, approximately 1,500 N, occurred in the upright position with the arms extended horizontally. [5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24345591/)

The study was small and used finite-element calculations rather than implanted force sensors. Its value is conceptual: the phase of a movement producing peak shear need not be the phase producing peak compression.

In repetitive lifting, the timing of these demands depends on the movement. As a mechanical illustration, a lifter may experience the greatest forward-bending moment near the floor, the greatest muscle force during acceleration through the middle, and a different combination of compression and posterior-element loading near lockout. “The spinal load of the exercise” is therefore a time-varying curve, not one fixed value.

## The same external weight can create different muscle force

External mechanics do not uniquely determine internal muscular mechanics. The body has many muscles capable of contributing to the same net joint moment.

Suppose two athletes produce the same lumbar extensor moment. One may use a strategy with larger muscle moment arms and lower force. Another may use muscles with shorter effective moment arms and greater force. Both balance the external moment, but the second strategy may generate greater compression.

A model sensitivity analysis illustrates this dependence. In a single-subject heavy-deadlift model, increasing the assumed muscle moment arms by 40% reduced estimated peak L5-S1 compression by 32% and shear by 36%. Changing how the back muscles wrapped around the trunk decreased estimated compression by 12% while increasing shear by 19%. [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36606738/)

The absolute estimates in that paper were high: 17.2 kN compression and 4.2 kN shear during a modeled 68-kg low-handle hex-bar deadlift. Those values came from one 23-year-old male and depended strongly on model structure. The model omitted intra-abdominal pressure and did not cap muscle forces at assumed physiological maxima. They are not universal deadlift numbers or prospective injury thresholds. The authors’ subsequent injury interpretation went beyond what a single-subject computational model can establish. [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36606738/)

A comparison of two lumbar models provides another warning. The models received similar external-moment inputs and produced roughly comparable compression estimates for most tasks, yet they recruited global and local muscles in markedly different proportions and produced substantially different shear estimates. [15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19493597/)

The nervous system’s force-sharing solution matters, and it is difficult to measure directly.

## Why posture can increase one load while decreasing another

Posture changes several things simultaneously:

- The angle of each motion segment.
- External moment arms.
- Muscle lengths.
- Muscle moment arms.
- Passive ligament and disc tension.
- Facet contact.
- The direction of muscle force relative to the disc.
- The relative contribution of the hips and lumbar spine.

That is why simple statements such as “more lordosis means less disc load” or “more flexion means more compression” are unreliable.

In the combined in-vivo and finite-element study comparing lordotic, freely selected, and kyphotic lifting postures, greater lordosis increased estimated global back-muscle activity, segmental compression, shear, and facet force. At the same time, it reduced the moment resisted by passive tissues and produced only small differences in intradiscal pressure and maximum disc-fiber strain. The authors interpreted a freely selected intermediate posture as a mechanical compromise, but that remains a model-based inference rather than a clinical trial showing lower injury rates. [8](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32147242/)

A more flexed posture may increase passive-tissue contribution while reducing some active-muscle demand. A more extended posture may require more active muscle force while reducing passive flexion tension. Neither state can be labelled universally safer without specifying the task, range, tissue, repetition history, and person.

## “Squat versus stoop” is not compression versus shear

Squat lifting and stoop lifting are often presented as opposites:

- Squat equals safe and low-back neutral.
- Stoop equals unsafe and spinally loaded.

The biomechanics is more complicated.

Greater knee flexion changes the position of the pelvis, trunk, hands, and object. Depending on the starting height and the object’s bulk, a squat may keep the trunk more upright, or it may force the object farther from the body. The resulting knee, hip, and spinal demands depend on the actual geometry rather than the technique name.

The 2026 paired analysis of 106 reconstructed peak-loading postures from five studies found that load magnitude and horizontal hand reach were associated with estimated compression and shear. Knee flexion was statistically associated with shear but not consistently with compression in the hierarchical models. Other postural variables were more closely associated with compression. The analysis also found substantial between-person variability. [1](https://www.mdpi.com/3042-7126/2/4/22)

Earlier work found that stoop lifting could reduce peak extensor moment while increasing passive bending torque, again demonstrating that a technique may reduce one demand while increasing another. [22](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7962011/)

This does not mean squat and stoop are identical. It means their comparison requires more than asking which one produces the smaller compression number.

A better question is:

> **Which tissues and joints carry more of the demand under this specific squat or stoop, with this object, from this height, at this speed?**

## Asymmetry changes the force direction

Asymmetrical lifting adds frontal-plane and rotational demands. It can raise mediolateral shear, change anterior-posterior shear, and redistribute muscular activity between the sides of the trunk.

In a study of 1,832 lifts from 22 participants, increasing object asymmetry produced large increases in estimated mediolateral shear at L5-S1. Object mass and asymmetry also affected anterior-posterior shear, while object mass had the strongest overall effect on compression. [3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34126573/)

Marras and Davis found a similar directional trade-off. Ten participants lifted the same 13.7-kg box from symmetric, 30-degree, and 60-degree asymmetric positions. Compression and lateral shear rose with increasing asymmetry, while anterior-posterior shear decreased. One-handed lifting changed the force profile again. [10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9629066/)

This is why one shear value should not be reported without its direction. Saying “shear decreased” may hide the fact that sagittal shear fell while lateral shear rose.

It is also why asymmetrical training should not be called merely a “lighter version” of bilateral training. A suitcase carry or offset deadlift may use less total external weight yet create larger frontal-plane or rotational demands.

## Speed and acceleration change the problem

Accelerating a mass requires a net force; motion at constant velocity does not. Faster lifting can increase acceleration and the rate at which the nervous system must generate muscular force.

An EMG-assisted dynamic model found that estimated compression and shear increased with external load, movement velocity, and asymmetry. [11](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8522544/)

Gooyers and colleagues also found interactions among external mass, lifting speed, and load symmetry rather than one variable acting independently. Thirty-two male participants with material-handling experience completed lifting conditions while researchers estimated lumbar joint loading from kinematics, force plates, and bilateral muscle activity. The effect of changing speed depended partly on the load and whether it was positioned symmetrically. [12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29122191/)

Dolan and colleagues found that the fastest lifts increased peak extensor moment by about 60% in their experiment without increasing the estimated passive bending torque. Speed changed the active muscular demand more than the passive flexion measure. [22](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7962011/)

This does not mean slow is universally safe. At a fixed range and repetition count, slower repetitions increase time under load, so slowing a lift should not be assumed to reduce every aspect of exposure. Ballistic sport movements require speed. The mechanical dose includes magnitude, rate, duration, and repetition, not one variable in isolation.

## Compression and shear are not the only loads

Real spinal movement also includes bending and torsion. These moments and their associated deformations are distinguished from translational forces in segment testing and finite-element analysis. [26](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14706323/) [7](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0021929013003199) [8](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32147242/)

A **flexion or extension moment** creates a compression-tension gradient across the motion segment. During flexion, posterior annular fibers and posterior ligaments may be tensioned while anterior regions experience greater compression. In extension, load distribution shifts toward posterior structures and facets.

A **lateral-bending moment** creates an asymmetrical compression-tension pattern from side to side.

A **torsional moment** rotates one vertebra relative to the next. Torsion generates internal shear stresses in the disc, but torsional moment is not the same variable as translational joint shear force.

This vocabulary matters because research and coaching often use “shear” loosely to mean any non-compressive demand. A lift can have low translational shear but substantial bending or torsion. Another can have substantial anterior shear with little axial rotation.

Cadaveric and human lifting research suggests that combined loading, rather than one isolated force, often determines how tissues deform or fail. Simultaneous bending and compression can load the annulus, ligaments, endplates, and vertebral bodies differently from pure compression. [22](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7962011/) [21](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31593056/)

## Combined loading is the real loading state

Pure compression and pure shear experiments are useful because they isolate mechanisms. The living spine is rarely loaded that way.

As mechanical examples, a deadlift involves compression, sagittal shear, flexion-extension moments, muscle force, abdominal pressure, and changing lumbopelvic geometry. A one-handed carry can add lateral-bending and rotational demands. A throw can add high-speed torsion. A landing can combine compression, bending, acceleration, and rapid muscular co-contraction. [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36606738/) [3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34126573/) [28](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16099678/)

Cadaveric repetitive-lifting research shows that loading combinations and repetition history can change failure mode. In a simulated repetitive-lifting study, higher compression was associated with earlier endplate-type failures, while specimens under lower compression survived more cycles but could develop different disc injuries. These were isolated cadaveric lumbar segments, not living lifters, and the results cannot define a safe repetition limit. [21](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31593056/)

A newer flexion-compression study repeatedly loaded 16 human and 20 porcine lumbar motion segments. Human specimens were cycled between 0 and 6 degrees of flexion under compressive stresses ranging from 0.65 to 2.38 MPa; five human endplate injuries were observed. The study estimated injury risk as a function of applied stress and loading cycles. [20](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38748343/)

The important principle is:

> **Magnitude and repetition interact.**

A force tolerated once may not be tolerated for thousands of cycles without recovery. A low force that produces no visible damage in one exposure may accumulate fatigue under repeated loading. Conversely, cadaveric fatigue experiments lack active muscle control, repair, remodeling, adaptation, and pain-guided behavior.

They establish mechanical fatigue, not the exact exposure limit for a living athlete.

## Why a single “safe shear” number is insufficient

Ergonomics literature sometimes presents screening limits for lumbar compression or shear. These can be useful for comparing occupational tasks at a population level. They are not individual tissue-failure cliffs.

Several factors prevent one universal limit:

**Tissue quality varies.** Vertebral compressive strength and anterior-shear strength can correlate with bone density. [18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22578739/) [19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10593417/)

**Spinal level varies.** Different lumbar levels have different orientations and load distributions. [25](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16319750/)

**Direction varies.** Anterior, posterior, and lateral shear are resisted differently.

**Posture varies.** Flexion, extension, axial rotation, and facet contact change the load-sharing system.

**Rate varies.** Loading rate can change mechanical response; human cadaveric lumbar-disc testing has demonstrated increasing stiffness with increasing strain rate. [33](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31326631/)

**Cycles vary.** Repeated loading can reduce tolerance through fatigue.

**Living adaptation varies.** Cadaveric segments cannot remodel in response to training.

**Model estimates vary.** Different assumptions can materially change the calculated force.

The 2026 paired lifting analysis found that some lifts could fall below one common compression screening value while exceeding a shear screening value. That supports evaluating the components separately. It does not turn either reference value into a biological boundary between safe and injured. [1](https://www.mdpi.com/3042-7126/2/4/22)

## Model estimates are not direct force measurements

Most lumbar compression and shear values reported during lifting are calculated rather than measured directly.

A typical chain includes:

1. Motion capture estimates body positions.
2. Force plates estimate external ground reactions.
3. Inverse dynamics estimates net joint moments.
4. EMG, optimization, or control assumptions distribute those moments among muscles.
5. A musculoskeletal or finite-element model estimates joint-reaction forces and tissue stresses.

Each stage introduces assumptions.

The Byrne study shows how large the effect can be. In one participant performing dynamic lifting, generic lumbar-rhythm kinematics without measured vertebral translations overestimated compression by approximately 31% and shear by 39% relative to a model driven by dynamic stereo-X-ray kinematics. Changing passive stiffness and the assumed neutral position altered the estimates by more than 50% in some comparisons. [16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32070482/)

A separate comparison of two established spine-modeling approaches produced broadly similar compression for many tasks but markedly different muscle-recruitment and shear estimates. [15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19493597/)

Field-based inertial motion capture adds further uncertainty. [2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10976016/) A 2025 method-comparison study compared inertial estimates with simultaneous optical motion capture and force-plate modeling in 19 participants. Absolute differences between the model estimates were substantial for L5-S1 compression and anterior-posterior shear, even where correlation across tasks was reasonable. [17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39520071/)

This leads to an essential reading rule:

> **Do not compare two spinal-force numbers unless the models, spinal levels, coordinate systems, movement definitions, and input methods are comparable.**

A result of 5 kN from one model does not establish that the underlying task loads the spine less than a result of 6 kN from another.

## Direct measurements have limits too

Direct intradiscal pressure measurement avoids some modeling assumptions, but it measures only one variable at one location.

The Wilke study involved one volunteer and one L4-L5 disc. [4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10222525/)

Implant-load studies in surgically treated patients measure forces through instrumented implants, but those patients have altered anatomy and load sharing. [27](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24132621/)

Dual-fluoroscopy studies provide highly accurate vertebral kinematics but generally involve very small samples and still require models to calculate internal disc forces. The L3-L4 weight-lifting study involved three participants. [5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24345591/)

Cadaveric experiments can directly apply and measure forces, but they remove active muscle control, circulation, healing, pain responses, and long-term adaptation.

No single method provides a complete living-human picture. Confidence grows when several methods support the same broad conclusion, not when one exact number is repeated without its assumptions.

## The barbell example: close versus far

Holding the load close usually reduces the external flexion moment. The direct Wilke pressure experiment found approximately 1.1 MPa while holding a 20-kg object close to the chest, compared with 1.8 MPa when held 60 cm from the chest. [4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10222525/)

But “close bar” does not guarantee lower calculated compression or shear in every setup. In a pilot study, ten experienced powerlifters and weightlifters performed maximal isometric deadlift initiations with the bar positioned in two different horizontal locations; eight provided analyzable data after two technical exclusions. The setups changed knee, pelvis, torso, and muscle-activity patterns, but modeled lumbar compression and shear did not differ statistically. [13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30200300/)

The pilot was small, isometric, and limited to the starting position. It does not overturn the moment-arm principle. The findings are consistent with postural changes partly offsetting the expected effect of bar location, but do not establish equivalence or a compensatory mechanism.

The evidence-supported cue is therefore not “every centimetre from the shin produces a fixed increase in spinal force.” It is:

> **Reducing unnecessary reach generally reduces the external moment, but the final internal load depends on the whole-body response.**

## The deadlift example: one weight, many possible models

The deadlift is often discussed as if it creates one known compression and shear value. It does not.

In the 2023 heavy-deadlift paper, changing muscle moment arms and wrapping assumptions altered peak estimates substantially even though the recorded lift was unchanged. [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36606738/)

The 2018 isometric-deadlift pilot found different joint positions and EMG patterns without significant differences in modeled lumbar compression or shear between close- and far-bar conditions. [13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30200300/)

Other lifting studies show that external mass, reach, speed, asymmetry, and deposit height affect joint loading and can interact. [3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34126573/) [12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29122191/)

The correct conclusion is not that spinal loading is unknowable. Several robust trends remain:

- More mass usually increases force.
- Greater reach usually increases the external moment.
- Faster acceleration can increase muscular demand.
- Asymmetry changes mediolateral and rotational demand.
- Fatigue may change movement and muscle function. [30](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12923468/)
- Muscle leverage strongly influences internal force.

What remains uncertain is the exact kilonewton value for one athlete and the clinical meaning of that value.

## Compression is not the villain

The spine is built to carry compression. Its muscles and bone can respond to exercise: a trial in people with chronic low back pain found greater increases in paraspinal muscle size with combined motor-control and isolated lumbar-extension training than with general exercise, while a trial in postmenopausal women with low bone mass found greater lumbar-spine bone-density gains with supervised resistance and impact training than with low-intensity home exercise. These findings do not establish training-induced changes in the failure tolerance of every spinal tissue. [31](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37762861/) [32](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28975661/)

Eliminating compression would mean eliminating standing, walking, running, lifting, and most exercise. Plan training around loads the athlete can perform, recover from, and progressively increase.

Compression becomes clinically relevant when considered relative to tissue tolerance, rate, repetitions, recovery, health status, and the accompanying bending or shear. A high compressive force in a trained athlete for a brief, well-controlled effort is not mechanically equivalent to the same force applied repetitively to weak cadaveric bone or to someone with severe osteoporosis. [19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10593417/) [20](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38748343/)

The existence of high estimated compression during sport does not prove that the activity is pathological. It shows that athletic tissues and techniques must manage substantial forces.

## Shear is not the villain either

Shear is also unavoidable. Segment inclination, muscle pull, gait, lifting, carrying, pushing, pulling, and asymmetrical movement all create shear components.

The annulus, facets, ligaments, and muscles are mechanically involved in resisting shear. Increasing compression can sometimes increase resistance to translation, although it also raises other tissue loads. [6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12472249/) [26](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14706323/)

The phrase “shearing the disc” often makes ordinary movement sound like two loose vertebrae sliding across one another. The resulting translation depends on the segment’s mechanical response, not on shear force alone. Shear force is a vector component, not a diagnosis. [26](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14706323/) [7](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0021929013003199)

High or repeated shear may contribute to specific injury mechanisms under particular conditions. That does not make every shear-producing movement harmful, nor does it establish one safe shear threshold for all people.

## Pain does not reveal compression or shear

A person cannot feel kilonewtons directly. The sensation of pressure, tightness, strain, or pain does not identify which force component is dominant.

Pain is influenced by biological, psychological, and social factors. The revised IASP definition explicitly distinguishes pain from nociception and cautions against inferring pain solely from activity in sensory neurons. [24](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32694387/)

Imaging findings also do not provide a direct force history. A systematic review of 3,110 asymptomatic people found that disc degeneration, bulging, protrusion, facet degeneration, and other spinal findings were common without pain and increased with age. [23](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25430861/)

This means:

- High modeled compression does not prove pain.
- High shear does not prove instability.
- Low modeled loading does not guarantee comfort.
- Pain during flexion does not prove excessive disc shear.
- Pain relief from changing technique does not identify the original tissue force.

Assess technique changes by their effects on function, tolerance, and progression rather than by an unsupported claim that one force was eliminated.

## Athlete-specific evidence: the important gap

Direct athlete evidence remains limited.

The deadlift setup study recruited ten experienced lifters, analyzed eight, and examined isometric initiation rather than full dynamic repetitions. [13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30200300/)

The heavy-deadlift model analyzed the third cycle of one participant’s repeated 68-kg low-handle hex-bar deadlifts. [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36606738/)

The larger compression-and-shear datasets cited here come from occupational or laboratory lifting rather than maximal powerlifting, Olympic lifting, strongman, throwing, gymnastics, or contact sport. [3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34126573/)

The studies cited here do not prospectively connect individually measured compression or shear during lifting to future spinal injury in trained athletes. That gap matters. Mechanistic estimates should not be presented as athlete injury probabilities.

The athlete translation is therefore narrower:

> **Spinal forces in heavy training can be large. Technique changes redistribute those forces. These studies do not provide one safe compression or shear number that predicts injury for an individual athlete.**

## The assessment implication

An evidence-informed loading assessment should begin with the whole task.

### Define the external demand

Record:

- External mass.
- Object or bar location.
- Starting and finishing height.
- Range of motion.
- Speed and acceleration.
- Symmetry.
- Repetition count.
- Set duration.
- Rest interval.
- Fatigue state.

A weight without its geometry and time profile is an incomplete exposure description.

### Observe motion sharing

Assess how the lumbar spine, pelvis, hips, knees, ankles, thorax, and shoulders contribute.

More hip or knee contribution may change lumbar moment and shear, but it may also shift demand to the lower limbs. The goal is not to make one region carry zero load. It is to choose a distribution the person can tolerate and progressively develop.

### Separate sagittal and frontal-plane demand

Bilateral sagittal lifting and offset or rotational lifting are not mechanically equivalent. [3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34126573/) [10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9629066/) Assess bilateral and asymmetrical tasks separately rather than assuming that tolerance of one establishes tolerance of the other.

### Assess the response, not only the appearance

Record tolerance alongside the observed spinal position. A neutral-looking position can still require substantial muscular force, and visual posture alone cannot quantify internal force. [8](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32147242/) [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36606738/)

Useful outcomes include:

- Pain response during and after the task.
- Strength and velocity.
- Repetition tolerance.
- Confidence.
- Recovery by the next session.
- Neurological symptoms.
- Change under modified load, range, stance, tempo, or grip.

### Compare within the same person

Model uncertainty makes within-person comparisons more useful than treating published kilonewton values as personal measurements. Does moving the load closer improve performance? Does reducing asymmetry reduce symptoms? Does a slower descent improve control but increase fatigue? Does a different stance redistribute the task helpfully?

These tests generate programming information without pretending to measure exact internal stress.

## The programming implication

These practical programming principles are informed by biomechanics; the cited studies do not establish a validated training dose for increasing the injury tolerance of individual spinal tissues.

### Manage moment arms before fearing weight

When the goal is temporarily reducing lumbar demand, keeping a load closer, raising its starting position, using supports, or reducing unnecessary reach can lower the external moment without eliminating the movement. Direct pressure and lifting-model studies support the importance of horizontal distance. [4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10222525/) [25](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16319750/)

That does not mean all long-moment-arm exercise is bad. Long-moment-arm tasks can be deliberately trained when the athlete needs that capacity.

### Build compression tolerance progressively

Squats, deadlifts, carries, rowing variations, jumping, and many sport tasks expose the spine to compression. Progress can occur through external mass, volume, range, speed, exercise complexity, and frequency.

The objective is not zero compression. It is an exposure that the athlete can recover from and repeat, with progression guided by the athlete’s response.

### Build shear and asymmetrical capacity deliberately

Offset carries, split-stance lifts, rotational work, unilateral loading, change of direction, and throwing can increase frontal-plane or rotational demand even when external weight is modest.

Introduce these variables progressively rather than assuming that a lighter object makes the task universally easier.

### Progress speed separately from weight

Because acceleration changes force and muscular demand, increasing load and speed simultaneously can create a larger exposure jump than either variable alone. [11](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8522544/)

Power training eventually requires speed. The progression should make speed a planned variable rather than an accidental consequence of poor control.

### Consider repetition and duration

Cadaveric fatigue studies show that repeated combined loading can produce failure at loads below one-time failure tolerance. These experiments do not measure the recovery or adaptation of a living lifter. [20](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38748343/)

Heavy spinal days should therefore be programmed through the interaction of intensity, repetitions, sets, frequency, and recovery, not by intensity alone.

### Avoid turning one cue into a universal law

Treat “neutral spine,” “chest up,” “hips back,” “knees out,” and “bar close” as task-specific cues to test. None should be assumed to guarantee lower compression, lower shear, lower stress, or lower injury risk in every person.

A cue earns its place by improving the target movement, performance, or tolerance.

## What compression and shear are not

Compression is **not the weight on the bar**. Muscular force and body-segment mechanics can make internal compression much greater than the external load.

Shear is **not synonymous with spinal flexion**. Shear depends on segment orientation, force direction, muscle action, and three-dimensional loading.

Intradiscal pressure is **not total spinal compression**. It is one pressure measure within one part of the disc.

A joint-reaction force is **not tissue stress**. Stress depends on force distribution and area.

Reduced motion is **not necessarily reduced load**. Greater trunk-muscle co-contraction can increase stiffness while also increasing estimated spinal compression. [28](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16099678/) [29](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10828922/)

A neutral-looking spine is **not an unloaded spine**.

A flexed spine is **not automatically experiencing the largest compression**. Some postures reduce active-muscle demand while increasing passive bending or strain.

More knee bend is **not guaranteed to reduce all spinal forces**.

A lower anterior-posterior shear value is **not automatically a lower-shear task** if mediolateral shear rises.

A model estimate is **not a direct measurement or an injury threshold**.

High spinal force is **not automatically tissue damage**.

Pain is **not a compression or shear meter**. [24](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32694387/)

## Working vocabulary for Tier 3

These terms distinguish forces, moments, pressure, deformation, and load sharing in spinal biomechanics. [8](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32147242/) [9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36606738/) [26](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14706323/)

**External load** is force or moment applied to the body from an external source, including gravity and contact with an object, barbell, ground, or other support. Acceleration affects the forces and moments required.

**Internal load** is force and moment transmitted through muscles, joints, bones, discs, ligaments, fascia, and other tissues.

**Compression** is a force component acting toward opposing joint or tissue surfaces.

**Anterior-posterior shear** is force acting parallel to a local joint plane in the forward-backward direction.

**Mediolateral shear** is force acting parallel to a local joint plane in the side-to-side direction.

**Resultant force** is the vector sum of all force components.

**Moment**, or torque, is the rotational effect of a force around an axis.

**Moment arm** is the perpendicular distance between a force’s line of action and the axis about which it creates rotation.

**Stress** is internal force per unit area on a specified plane. Force divided by area gives an average stress; the local stress state includes normal and shear components.

**Strain** describes deformation relative to a reference configuration, including relative length change and angular distortion.

**Intradiscal pressure** is pressure measured or estimated within the intervertebral disc. It is not synonymous with total compressive force.

**Joint-reaction force** is the resultant force transmitted across a modeled joint. Its interpretation depends on how muscle forces, passive tissues, and joint constraints are represented.

**Co-contraction** is simultaneous muscular activity from muscles producing different or opposing moments. In controlled experiments and biomechanical models, greater trunk co-contraction can increase stiffness and estimated spinal compression. [28](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16099678/) [29](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10828922/)

**Follower load** is an experimental compressive load directed approximately along the curvature of a spinal column, minimizing buckling moments. It is a biomechanical testing concept rather than a perfectly measured in-vivo force path. [14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10332793/)

**Bending moment** is a moment tending to flex, extend, or laterally bend a motion segment.

**Torsional moment** is a rotational moment around the spinal axis. It can generate internal shear stresses but is not the same quantity as translational shear force.

**Load sharing** describes the distribution of force among the disc, endplates, facets, ligaments, muscles, bones, and other structures.

**Fatigue loading** is repeated loading that can progressively reduce mechanical tolerance even when each individual cycle is below one-time failure strength. [20](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38748343/) [21](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31593056/)

## The modern verdict

### Strongly supported

Compression and shear are separate components of spinal joint loading.

External mass, horizontal reach, posture, speed, and asymmetry affect the components differently. [1](https://www.mdpi.com/3042-7126/2/4/22) [11](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8522544/) [10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9629066/)

Muscle force contributes substantially to spinal compression and can alter shear.

Compression and shear are distributed through different combinations of disc, endplate, annulus, facet, ligament, and bony structures. [6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12472249/) [7](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0021929013003199) [26](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14706323/)

Intradiscal pressure, total compression, shear, facet force, passive moment, and annular strain can change by different amounts during the same posture change. [8](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32147242/)

Loading magnitude, direction, speed, and repetition can interact.

### Mechanically supported but individually uncertain

Keeping a load closer generally reduces the external flexion moment and often reduces internal demand, although whole-body compensation can alter the result. [4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10222525/)

Asymmetry generally increases frontal-plane and rotational demands, but one shear component may fall while another rises. [3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34126573/) [10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9629066/)

Depending on the load path and boundary conditions, compression can increase resistance to shear or buckling, while also raising other tissue demands.

Posture can shift loading between active muscles, passive tissues, discs, and facets rather than uniformly increasing or decreasing “spinal load.” [8](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32147242/)

### Not established

The cited studies do not establish a compression value that is safe for every person.

The cited studies do not establish a universally safe shear value.

The cited studies do not establish one lifting posture that minimizes every spinal force and tissue stress.

There is no direct conversion from bar weight to disc stress.

Visual posture alone does not reliably quantify internal spinal force.

The cited evidence does not establish an athlete-specific injury threshold based on one modeled compression or shear value.

The presence of flexion, compression, or shear alone does not establish pathology.

The updated model is:

> **External loading creates forces and moments. Muscles and passive tissues contribute to the response. The resulting segmental loads include compression and shear forces, together with bending and torsional moments, at each spinal level. Those loads are distributed unevenly across the disc, endplates, facets, ligaments, bones, and muscles.**

## The takeaway

Compression and shear are not competing labels for the same force.

Compression acts mainly toward the joint surfaces. Shear acts mainly along them. Both occur during normal movement, and both are affected by much more than the weight being lifted.

The same barbell can create different spinal loading when:

- It is moved farther from the body.
- The trunk or pelvis changes orientation.
- The lift becomes asymmetrical.
- Speed changes.
- Fatigue changes muscle function or movement strategy. [30](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12923468/)
- A different athlete uses different muscular leverage.
- The force is distributed across a different spinal level or tissue area.

A lower compression estimate does not guarantee lower shear. A lower anterior-posterior shear estimate may accompany higher lateral shear. Lower intradiscal pressure does not guarantee lower total joint force. Less visible spinal motion does not guarantee lower tissue loading.

For training and rehabilitation, a practical programming principle is:

> **Do not chase the lowest possible spinal-load number. Build the capacity to manage the specific combination of compression, shear, bending, speed, range, and repetition required by the task.**

The goal is not an unloaded spine.

The goal is a spine whose tissues and control system are prepared for the loads that matter.
