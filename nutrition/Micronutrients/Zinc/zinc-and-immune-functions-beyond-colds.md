# Zinc and Immune Function Beyond Colds

Zinc is required for normal innate and adaptive immune function, and experimentally induced zinc deficiency produces measurable T-cell dysfunction in humans. That does not mean adding zinc above an adequate intake makes an already healthy immune system work better. ([NIH Office of Dietary Supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-HealthProfessional/)) ([Beck et al., 1997](https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpendo.1997.272.6.E1002)) ([Hodkinson et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1093/gerona/62.6.598))

The human evidence strongly supports **correcting low zinc status**. It does not establish a general “immune boost” or a reliable reduction in infection risk from routine supplementation in adults without evidence of low zinc status. ([Beck et al., 1997](https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpendo.1997.272.6.E1002)) ([Hodkinson et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1093/gerona/62.6.598)) ([Crawford et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu14214604))

## Deficiency correction and “immune boosting” are different questions

A supplementation trial enrolling people with low zinc status tests whether replacing a limiting nutrient restores function. It does not answer whether the same supplement improves immunity in people who already have adequate zinc. ([Beck et al., 1997](https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpendo.1997.272.6.E1002)) ([Hodkinson et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1093/gerona/62.6.598))

This distinction is complicated by zinc assessment. An updated review and meta-analysis found that serum or plasma zinc is useful for detecting population-level differences and responses to supplementation, but no single biomarker reliably diagnoses mild zinc deficiency in an individual. Low serum or plasma zinc is therefore compatible with inadequate status, but it is not a stand-alone measure of whole-body deficiency. Values can also vary with age, sex, sampling time, infection, and illness, which is especially important in hospitalized populations. ([Ceballos-Rasgado et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/nutrit/nuae072)) ([NIH Office of Dietary Supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-HealthProfessional/))

That limitation can affect zinc trials: studies may classify participants differently, and people described as “healthy” can still include a low-zinc subgroup. ([Ceballos-Rasgado et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/nutrit/nuae072)) ([Jafari et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/10408398.2020.1862048)) ([Crawford et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu14214604))

## T-cell function: direct human evidence

In an experimental human study of five men, inducing mild zinc deficiency altered T-cell subpopulations and reduced production of T-helper-1 cytokines, including interleukin-2 and interferon-γ. Repletion reversed these changes. This provides direct human mechanistic evidence that inadequate zinc can alter cell-mediated immunity rather than merely correlate with it. However, the very small study had no concurrent control group and does not establish the magnitude, prevalence, or clinical consequences of those effects in the wider population. ([Beck et al., 1997](https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpendo.1997.272.6.E1002))

A 2016 randomized, double-blind trial provides a clinically relevant example. Of 53 eligible nursing-home residents, 31 had serum zinc below 70 µg/dL and were randomized to multivitamins containing either 30 mg or 5 mg zinc per day for three months. Because the comparison multivitamin supplied 5 mg zinc, this was a higher-dose versus low-dose comparison, not zinc versus a zinc-free placebo. The higher-zinc group had a greater increase in serum zinc, stimulated T-cell proliferation, and peripheral T-cell number. However, when proliferation was normalized to the number of T cells, the between-group difference disappeared. This suggests that the apparent functional improvement was substantially explained by having more T cells, rather than each cell becoming more responsive. The trial was small, suffered substantial loss of usable immune-assay data, and did not measure infections. ([Barnett et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.115.115188))

A 2022 study compared 23 young adults with 31 hospitalized older adults and then supplemented only ten seniors classified as having low zinc by the study's criteria. Approximately six days of 10 mg/day zinc increased serum zinc, reduced expression of the IL-2-suppressing transcription factor CREMα, and increased stimulated IL-2 production. But this was a very small uncontrolled pre-post intervention in hospitalized patients, not a randomized infection-prevention trial. Acute illness can also affect serum zinc, so the classification should not be treated as a definitive diagnosis of whole-body deficiency. ([Baarz et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12979-022-00295-8))

**Conflict note:** The Baarz study reported no external funding, but senior author Lothar Rink disclosed consultancy work for Zinpro and Köhler Pharma and research grants from Zinpro and Wörwag Pharma. A named Köhler Pharma zinc preparation was used. The mechanistic result is still informative, but the commercial relationships and uncontrolled design warrant caution. ([Baarz et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12979-022-00295-8))

A 2026 study reported an assay-dependent interferon-γ response after short-term supplementation in hospitalized older adults classified as having low zinc. Stimulated whole-blood interferon-γ increased after supplementation (n = 25, p = 0.0083), while the isolated peripheral blood mononuclear cell assay did not show a significant increase (n = 14, p = 0.3910). Baseline interferon-γ also did not differ significantly between older participants classified as zinc-adequate and low-zinc. The study used a short, uncontrolled intervention and did not test whether participants developed fewer infections. It reported no external funding or competing interests. ([Olah et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms27021039))

A 2026 report found lower IL-2 production and higher CREMα expression in vegetarians and vegans classified as zinc-deficient by the study's criteria. This is consistent with the deficiency model, but it should not be interpreted as evidence that zinc-replete vegetarians, vegans, or omnivores would benefit from additional supplementation. ([Maywald et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jnutbio.2026.110397))

Overall, the human T-cell evidence supports the first part of the deficiency claim: **low zinc can impair T-cell cytokine production and alter T-cell populations, and correction can restore selected functions**. ([Beck et al., 1997](https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpendo.1997.272.6.E1002)) ([Barnett et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.115.115188)) ([Baarz et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12979-022-00295-8))

## NK cells and antiviral interferons

The human evidence for natural killer cell activity does not support a uniform pattern of suppression with low zinc status. ([Amling et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-025-06259-y))

In a 2025 study of healthy young adults, baseline zinc status was not significantly associated with NK-cell counts or cytotoxicity. Investigators then gave 10 mg/day zinc for 14 days to 29 participants classified as low or at risk of low zinc. NK-cell cytotoxicity increased after supplementation, but the intervention had no placebo group, ten eligible participants did not complete it, and some functional assays used much smaller subsamples. It measured laboratory cell activity, not infections. ([Amling et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-025-06259-y))

This study therefore provides a possible low-status supplementation signal, but it does not show that baseline low zinc invariably produces impaired NK function or that supplementing zinc-replete adults improves resistance to disease. ([Amling et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-025-06259-y))

A separate 2025 study found that vegetarians and vegans classified as zinc-deficient had a reduced antiviral interferon-α response and that 14 days of 10 mg/day zinc increased serum zinc, interferon regulatory factor 3, and interferon-α-related responses. Once again, the supplemented group was deliberately selected for low zinc, the study lacked a placebo-treated low-zinc group, and no clinical infection outcome was measured. ([Vallboehmer et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clnu.2025.06.010))

The NK-cell and antiviral-interferon literature therefore fits the same pattern as the T-cell literature: **specific laboratory functions may improve when low zinc is corrected, but that is not evidence of a universal boost in zinc-replete people**. ([Amling et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-025-06259-y)) ([Vallboehmer et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clnu.2025.06.010))

## Antibody responses

Direct adult human evidence that zinc deficiency or zinc supplementation meaningfully changes antibody production is limited and less direct than the T-cell evidence, especially in zinc-alone clinical trials. ([Girodon et al., 1999](https://doi.org/10.1001/archinte.159.7.748)) ([Crawford et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu14214604))

A large relevant trial enrolled 725 institutionalized older adults in a two-year, double-blind factorial study. Participants received zinc plus selenium, antioxidant vitamins, both combinations, or placebo. The zinc-and-selenium intervention improved selected influenza-vaccine antibody titers and seroprotection outcomes. However, zinc cannot be separated from selenium in this comparison. Respiratory-infection results were also mixed: there was no significant reduction during the seven months following vaccination, while the overall analysis produced only a borderline difference in the proportion remaining infection-free. There was no survival benefit. ([Girodon et al., 1999](https://doi.org/10.1001/archinte.159.7.748))

**Conflict note:** This trial was funded by Produits Roche SA and Labcatal Laboratories, manufacturers involved in vitamin and trace-element products. Its favorable vaccine-antibody result should be interpreted with that commercial funding and the combined zinc-selenium intervention in mind. ([Girodon et al., 1999](https://doi.org/10.1001/archinte.159.7.748))

The most accurate conclusion is therefore that zinc may contribute to normal antibody responses, especially when status is low, but the adult supplementation evidence does not isolate a clear zinc-specific antibody benefit. ([Girodon et al., 1999](https://doi.org/10.1001/archinte.159.7.748)) ([Crawford et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu14214604))

## Healthy adults without identified zinc deficiency

The claim that zinc produces “little measurable boost” in adults without identified zinc deficiency is directionally reasonable but too categorical. Some immune and inflammatory markers do change in supplementation trials. ([Jafari et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/10408398.2020.1862048)) ([Hodkinson et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1093/gerona/62.6.598))

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 35 randomized trials involving 1,995 adults found reductions in C-reactive protein, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, TNF-α, IL-6, and neutrophils, along with an increase in CD4 measures. The pooled CD3 estimate was not statistically significant (standardized mean difference 0.37, 95% confidence interval -0.49 to 1.22; p = 0.399), despite contrary wording in the abstract's conclusion. These findings show that zinc can produce measurable biological effects. However, the trials included heterogeneous populations, baseline zinc status was not consistently adequate or measured comparably, and most outcomes were laboratory markers rather than infections. ([Jafari et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/10408398.2020.1862048))

A more directly applicable randomized trial studied apparently healthy adults aged 55 to 70. Participants received placebo, 15 mg/day zinc, or 30 mg/day zinc for six months. The 15 mg dose increased the CD4-to-CD8 ratio at one time point, while 30 mg transiently lowered the B-lymphocyte count. Across the full range of immune measures, the investigators concluded that total zinc intakes up to approximately 40 mg/day had no significant long-term effect on immune status in these apparently healthy adults. ([Hodkinson et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1093/gerona/62.6.598))

A systematic review specifically examining immune-related supplement ingredients in otherwise healthy people found only a small, heterogeneous set of eligible zinc studies. Differences in formulation, study context, endpoints, and participant status made a general immune-protection conclusion difficult. ([Crawford et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu14214604))

The evidence therefore does not show that zinc has no biological effect in adults without identified zinc deficiency. It shows something narrower: ([Jafari et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/10408398.2020.1862048)) ([Hodkinson et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1093/gerona/62.6.598))

**Selected biomarkers can change, but there is no consistent evidence that routine zinc supplementation makes healthy adults without evidence of low zinc status meaningfully more resistant to infections.** ([Jafari et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/10408398.2020.1862048)) ([Hodkinson et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1093/gerona/62.6.598)) ([Crawford et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu14214604))

## Older adults and infection rates

Older adults are a biologically and clinically plausible group in which zinc supplementation might reduce infections, partly because low zinc status has been observed in frail or institutionalized samples. In the Barnett nursing-home trial, 31 of 53 eligible participants, or 58 percent, had serum zinc below the study's cutoff. But this finding cannot be generalized to all independently living older adults, and serum zinc is itself an imperfect individual diagnostic marker. ([Barnett et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.115.115188); [Ceballos-Rasgado et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/nutrit/nuae072))

A notable positive infection signal comes from a small 12-month randomized trial of approximately 50 older adults given 45 mg/day zinc or placebo. Seven of 24 participants in the zinc group, or 29 percent, experienced at least one infection, compared with 22 of 25 participants, or 88 percent, in the placebo group. Investigators also reported 7 infection events in the zinc group versus 35 in the placebo group, but participants could contribute to more than one infection category, so those event totals are not counts of unique infected people. The trial also reported changes in inflammatory cytokines and oxidative-stress measures. ([Prasad et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/85.3.837))

**Funding note:** The Prasad trial was supported by an NIH grant and Labcatal Laboratories, which also supplied the zinc and placebo capsules. The large effect estimate, small sample, high dose, and manufacturer support make it an uncertain basis for general recommendations. ([Prasad et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/85.3.837))

The 45 mg/day supplemental dose also exceeded the U.S. adult tolerable upper intake level of 40 mg/day from all sources. The upper limit is not a toxicity threshold and does not apply in the same way to medically supervised treatment, but doses of 50 mg/day or more for weeks can inhibit copper absorption, and excessive zinc intake can reduce immune function. This research dose should not be treated as a routine preventive dose. ([Prasad et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/85.3.837)) ([NIH Office of Dietary Supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-HealthProfessional/))

The much larger Girodon trial is less decisive. Zinc plus selenium improved the vaccine-antibody response, but its respiratory-infection analyses were mixed and the intervention did not isolate zinc. It was also commercially funded. ([Girodon et al., 1999](https://doi.org/10.1001/archinte.159.7.748))

Meanwhile, the Barnett trial demonstrated improved zinc status and T-cell numbers in low-zinc nursing-home residents but did not measure infection incidence. The newer IL-2, NK-cell, interferon-α, and interferon-γ studies are also mechanistic rather than clinical-outcome trials. ([Barnett et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.115.115188)) ([Baarz et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12979-022-00295-8)) ([Amling et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-025-06259-y)) ([Vallboehmer et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clnu.2025.06.010)) ([Olah et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms27021039))

The older-adult evidence is therefore **promising but not conclusive**. Benefits may be concentrated among frail, institutionalized, or demonstrably low-zinc adults, but current trials do not establish routine supplementation, particularly routine high-dose supplementation, for every older adult. ([Prasad et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/85.3.837)) ([Girodon et al., 1999](https://doi.org/10.1001/archinte.159.7.748)) ([Barnett et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.115.115188)) ([Crawford et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu14214604))

## Immune markers are not infection outcomes

IL-2 production, interferon release, NK-cell cytotoxicity, lymphocyte counts, inflammatory proteins, and vaccine-antibody titers can demonstrate biological activity. They are useful for establishing that zinc status affects immune pathways. ([Jafari et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/10408398.2020.1862048)) ([Barnett et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.115.115188)) ([Girodon et al., 1999](https://doi.org/10.1001/archinte.159.7.748))

They do not by themselves prove that a person will contract fewer infections, recover faster, avoid hospitalization, or live longer. ([Jafari et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/10408398.2020.1862048)) ([Crawford et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu14214604))

This distinction is especially important for the recent literature. Several of the 2022-2026 mechanistic studies selected low-zinc participants, used short pre-post interventions, and measured ex vivo immune responses. Several lacked randomization or placebo controls, and none of these mechanistic studies established a reduction in infection incidence. ([Baarz et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12979-022-00295-8)) ([Amling et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-025-06259-y)) ([Vallboehmer et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clnu.2025.06.010)) ([Olah et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms27021039)) ([Maywald et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jnutbio.2026.110397))

Marker normalization can provide evidence of **deficiency correction**. It is not equivalent to evidence of a clinically meaningful “immune boost.” ([Beck et al., 1997](https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpendo.1997.272.6.E1002)) ([Baarz et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12979-022-00295-8))

## What the “immune support” market actually shows

A 2022 cross-sectional audit purchased 30 dietary supplements prominently marketed on Amazon as supporting or boosting immunity. Zinc appeared on the labels of 25 of the 30 products in this convenience sample. ([Crawford et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2022.26040))

Seventeen products had inaccurate labels, 13 were considered misbranded under the study’s criteria, nine contained undeclared substances, and none displayed third-party certification. Twenty-four either lacked an established daily value for at least one ingredient or supplied more than 200 percent of the daily value. ([Crawford et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2022.26040))

The audit had important limitations. Its convenience sample of 30 featured Amazon products was not representative of the entire supplement market, and the analytical method could not quantify elemental zinc or verify whether the listed zinc dose itself was accurate. It therefore identified labeling and quality-control problems within the sampled products, not zinc-specific adulteration or a market-wide prevalence estimate. ([Crawford et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2022.26040))

The study also did not measure company profits, investigate internal marketing decisions, or trace claims back to particular deficiency studies. It cannot prove that companies deliberately generalize deficiency evidence for financial gain. ([Crawford et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2022.26040))

It does support a more defensible conclusion: **in this convenience sample, zinc was frequently included in products marketed broadly to consumers, while much of the direct human mechanistic evidence summarized here is concentrated in people with low zinc status and the labeling quality of immune-support products can be unreliable**. ([Crawford et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2022.26040)) ([Beck et al., 1997](https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpendo.1997.272.6.E1002)) ([Barnett et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.115.115188)) ([Baarz et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12979-022-00295-8))

## Limits and open questions

The current literature has several recurring limitations:

- Baseline zinc status is inconsistently measured, and no single marker perfectly identifies mild individual deficiency. Infection, illness, and sampling conditions can also affect serum or plasma zinc. ([Ceballos-Rasgado et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/nutrit/nuae072))

- Many trials test inflammatory proteins or ex vivo cell responses rather than prospectively measured infections. ([Jafari et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/10408398.2020.1862048))

- Several recent positive studies are small, uncontrolled pre-post experiments restricted to participants classified as having low zinc. ([Baarz et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12979-022-00295-8)) ([Amling et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-025-06259-y)) ([Vallboehmer et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clnu.2025.06.010)) ([Olah et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms27021039))

- Some older infection studies used zinc together with other micronutrients, preventing a zinc-specific conclusion. ([Girodon et al., 1999](https://doi.org/10.1001/archinte.159.7.748))

- The small positive Prasad infection trial used capsules supplied by a commercial laboratory, while the larger Girodon trial was directly industry-funded. ([Prasad et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/85.3.837); [Girodon et al., 1999](https://doi.org/10.1001/archinte.159.7.748))

- At least one trial used a supplemental dose above the U.S. adult tolerable upper intake level, limiting its suitability as the basis for a routine public-health recommendation. ([Prasad et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/85.3.837)) ([NIH Office of Dietary Supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-HealthProfessional/))


A decisive trial would stratify participants by baseline zinc status, test zinc alone, use an independent placebo, preregister clinical infection outcomes, and be large enough to compare low-zinc with clearly zinc-adequate adults. Older institutionalized adults and independently living older adults should also be analyzed separately.

## Quick reference: assessment of the competing claims

- **“Zinc deficiency impairs T-cell function, NK activity, and antibody production.”**
    Supported most clearly for T-cell cytokine production and T-cell populations. The human NK-cell evidence is suggestive but inconsistent, while the adult antibody evidence is less direct and partly comes from a combined zinc-selenium trial. ([Beck et al., 1997](https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpendo.1997.272.6.E1002)) ([Amling et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-025-06259-y)) ([Girodon et al., 1999](https://doi.org/10.1001/archinte.159.7.748))

- **“In non-deficient adults, zinc provides little measurable immune boost.”**
    Mostly correct when “boost” means better clinical protection from infections. Too strong when applied to all biomarkers, because meta-analysis shows changes in some inflammatory and lymphocyte measures, although its pooled CD3 result was not statistically significant. ([Jafari et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/10408398.2020.1862048)) ([Hodkinson et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1093/gerona/62.6.598)) ([Crawford et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu14214604))

- **“Zinc reduces infections in older adults, plausibly because their status is often borderline.”**
    Plausible and supported by one small positive infection trial plus low-zinc immune-function studies, but not conclusively established. A larger clinical trial used zinc with selenium and produced mixed infection results. ([Prasad et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/85.3.837); [Girodon et al., 1999](https://doi.org/10.1001/archinte.159.7.748); [Barnett et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.115.115188); [Baarz et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12979-022-00295-8))

- **“The supplement category generalizes deficiency findings to healthy adults.”**
    Broad marketing to consumers is documented, and zinc was common in the audited convenience sample of immune-support products. The audit did not trace product claims to deficiency studies and does not establish corporate motive or quantify profits. ([Crawford et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2022.26040))


## Practical interpretation

- The best-supported immune claim for zinc is **maintenance and restoration of normal function when zinc status is low**, not demonstrated enhancement beyond normal function in zinc-replete adults. ([Beck et al., 1997](https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpendo.1997.272.6.E1002)) ([Hodkinson et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1093/gerona/62.6.598)) ([Crawford et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu14214604))

- In healthy adults without evidence of low zinc status, routine supplementation has not been shown to produce a consistent, clinically meaningful reduction in infections beyond the common-cold context. ([Crawford et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu14214604)) ([Hodkinson et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1093/gerona/62.6.598))

- Older adults classified as having low zinc may experience improvements in T-cell numbers or cytokine responses after supplementation, but the evidence that this reliably prevents infections remains limited. ([Barnett et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.115.115188)) ([Prasad et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/85.3.837)) ([Girodon et al., 1999](https://doi.org/10.1001/archinte.159.7.748))

- The 45 mg/day dose used in the small positive infection trial exceeds the U.S. adult tolerable upper intake level of 40 mg/day from all sources and should not be treated as a generally established preventive dose. Because the study was small, independent replication would strengthen confidence in the result. ([Prasad et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/85.3.837)) ([NIH Office of Dietary Supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-HealthProfessional/))

- A scientifically defensible product claim would identify the target population and endpoint, for example correcting low zinc status, rather than implying that additional zinc universally “boosts” immunity.
