# Does Meal Regularity Matter?

This article examines day-to-day variation in meal times and eating windows. It does not evaluate whether the average meal time should be early or late, except where average timing must be separated from timing variability.

The evidence supports a cautious conclusion: regular eating patterns may affect short-term postprandial physiology, and greater day-to-day variability is associated with several unfavorable cardiometabolic markers. However, no trial has shown that keeping meals at consistent clock times, independently of meal frequency, eating-window duration, average timing, sleep, diet quality, energy intake, and work schedule, prevents weight gain, diabetes, or cardiovascular disease. ([Ali et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/jhn.13260); [Suyoto et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.advnut.2024.100310))

## What “meal regularity” means

Meal regularity is not a single exposure. It can refer to:

- Eating the same number of meals or snacks each day

- Eating the first or last meal at a similar clock time

- Keeping the duration or midpoint of the eating window stable

- Distributing calories similarly across the day

- Keeping weekday and weekend meal times similar


These measures are related but not interchangeable. Someone can eat dinner at 8:00 pm every day while changing breakfast by several hours, or maintain a ten-hour eating window while shifting the entire window later on weekends. ([Ali et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/jhn.13260); [Suyoto et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.advnut.2024.100310))

A 2024 systematic review identified 34 papers, including 28 observational papers and six intervention papers. Twenty-four reported an association between greater between-day variability and at least one unfavorable weight, metabolic, or cognitive outcome. However, the review found major inconsistency in how regularity was defined. Some studies used a single questionnaire item, while others calculated variability from multiple days of food records. Some assumed that everyone consumed three conventional meals, which could disconnect the measure from the actual clock time of eating. ([Ali et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/jhn.13260))

A separate 2024 systematic review included 80 articles from 43 observational and 37 experimental studies involving 89,178 participants. It had to use qualitative rather than quantitative synthesis because the studies examined substantially different exposures, including meal omission, eating-window duration, meal timing, meal-size distribution, and between-day variability. The results were mixed and frequently depended on the population and the specific definition of variability. ([Suyoto et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.advnut.2024.100310))

## What the randomized trials actually tested

The principal tightly controlled metabolic trials did not simply move otherwise identical meals to unpredictable clock times. They compared six eating occasions every day with a schedule that varied between three and nine eating occasions per day while preserving approximately the same average weekly number of eating occasions. Regularity was therefore changed together with daily meal frequency, meal size, and the distribution of fasting intervals. ([Farshchi et al., 2004a](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.ijo.0802616); [Farshchi et al., 2004b](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.ejcn.1601935); [Alhussain et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.115.125401); [Alhussain et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/nqab323))

These four Nottingham experiments lasted only 14 days per eating pattern, enrolled between nine and eleven participants per cohort, included only women, and came from the same research lineage. The two 2004 papers appear to be companion analyses of the same nine-woman protocol rather than independent replications. ([Farshchi et al., 2004a](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.ijo.0802616); [Farshchi et al., 2004b](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.ejcn.1601935); [Farshchi et al., 2005](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/81.1.16); [Alhussain et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.115.125401); [Alhussain et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/nqab323))

### Lean and normal-weight women

In nine lean women, 14 days of irregular eating did not significantly change body weight, mean three-day energy intake, subjective appetite, or fasting resting metabolic rate compared with regular eating. However, the thermic effect of a standardized test meal measured over three hours was lower after the irregular period. ([Farshchi et al., 2004a](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.ijo.0802616))

A companion report found no significant effect on fasting glucose or fasting insulin. Peak insulin and the insulin area under the curve after a high-carbohydrate test meal were higher after irregular eating, while fasting total and LDL cholesterol were also higher. These were short-term biomarker changes in nine healthy women whose values generally remained within normal ranges, not evidence of incident diabetes or cardiovascular disease. ([Farshchi et al., 2004b](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.ejcn.1601935))

A later trial provided identical isoenergetic foods to eleven normal-weight women. Regular eating produced a greater three-hour thermic effect of food and lower glucose responses after the standardized test drink and after some free-living meals. Body weight, body composition, fasting lipids, and gut-hormone responses to the test drink did not differ between patterns. Participants reported somewhat lower hunger and greater fullness during the regular period. ([Alhussain et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.115.125401))

### Women with obesity or insulin resistance

In ten women with obesity, the regular six-meal pattern was associated with greater postprandial thermogenesis, lower postprandial insulin responses, and lower fasting total and LDL cholesterol. However, the participants selected their own foods, and reported energy intake was lower during the regular period. The study therefore could not fully separate meal-pattern regularity from differences in energy intake and reporting. ([Farshchi et al., 2005](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/81.1.16))

The most recent Nottingham metabolic trial enrolled nine women with overweight or obesity and confirmed insulin resistance. Foods and energy intake were matched. The three-hour thermic effect of food was 97.7 kJ after the regular pattern and 76.7 kJ after the irregular pattern, with P = 0.048. The nominally statistically significant absolute difference was approximately 21 kJ, or 5 kcal, over the three-hour measurement period. Postprandial GLP-1 exposure was higher after regular eating, but HOMA-IR did not differ significantly between conditions. The trial did not detect statistically significant differences in body weight, body composition, waist circumference, fasting lipids, glucose, insulin, appetite, or subsequent food intake. With only nine participants, these null findings do not establish that the conditions were equivalent. ([Alhussain et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/nqab323))

### Broader behavioral interventions

The six intervention papers in the Ali review were not all reports from the Nottingham metabolic protocol. A 2015 pilot randomized 25 adults with overweight or obesity to a four-week routine-based intervention targeting both regular eating and sleep or to an education control before an 18-week behavioral weight-loss program. The routine intervention increased on-schedule eating but not on-schedule sleep. Contrary to the investigators' hypothesis, the routine group lost less weight during the first six weeks of the weight-loss program, while the between-group difference was not statistically significant at 18 weeks. Because the intervention combined eating and sleep routines and was followed by a broader behavioral program, it did not isolate meal-time regularity itself. ([Demos et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1002/osp4.16))

A later eLife Reviewed Preprint reported a six-week randomized pilot of personalized meal-time windows. Of 121 randomized participants, 100 completed the study and were included in the reported analysis. The intervention group became more regular and reported greater short-term weight loss than controls. However, body weight was self-measured using participants' home scales under remote video observation, while energy intake was self-reported; baseline BMI differed between groups, and the intervention restricted eating opportunities in ways that could also change eating-window structure and actual energy intake. Public peer reviewers specifically questioned the reliability of the calorie-intake claim and the study's measurement procedures. The study therefore does not establish that regularity alone caused the weight loss. ([Wilming et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.96837.3))

## What the thermic-effect finding means

A lower thermic response after irregular eating is the most consistent experimental signal. It appeared in lean, normal-weight, obese, and insulin-resistant female cohorts. However, these were repeated findings from one research lineage, not broad independent replication across multiple laboratories, sexes, age groups, or cultural eating patterns. ([Farshchi et al., 2004a](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.ijo.0802616); [Farshchi et al., 2005](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/81.1.16); [Alhussain et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.115.125401); [Alhussain et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/nqab323))

The trials measured the response to a standardized drink or meal for three hours. They did not demonstrate a sustained difference in total daily energy expenditure, long-term energy balance, or body weight. Extrapolating a small postprandial difference into meaningful long-term weight gain remains a hypothesis rather than an established outcome. ([Ali et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/jhn.13260); [Alhussain et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/nqab323))

## Does regularity matter regardless of whether meals are early or late?

No randomized trial has cleanly crossed regular versus irregular timing with early versus late timing. The available evidence therefore cannot establish that regularity has the same effect regardless of where the meals fall in the biological day. ([Ali et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/jhn.13260); [Suyoto et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.advnut.2024.100310))

Evidence from simulated night work suggests that average biological timing still matters. In a controlled 14-day laboratory study of 19 adults, participants who ate during both the daytime and nighttime developed misalignment between central circadian timing and the daily glucose rhythm, together with impaired glucose tolerance. Restricting eating to the daytime prevented those changes despite the simulated night-work schedule. This was a test of daytime versus nighttime eating, not regular versus irregular eating. ([Chellappa et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abg9910))

A July 2026 systematic review found only four randomized trials of meal-timing interventions in shift-work settings. Three imposed some form of fasting during the night shift and one used a self-selected ten-hour eating window. Only one trial reported a significant change in fasting insulin or HOMA-IR, no trial found a significant HDL cholesterol effect, and the reviewers judged the evidence too sparse and methodologically limited for robust conclusions. ([Wulandari et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261464874))

Regularity therefore cannot currently be treated as protection against eating at biologically unfavorable times. Average timing and day-to-day regularity may both matter, but they have not been experimentally separated.

## Average timing versus day-to-day variability

The available observational studies do not consistently show that either average timing or variability is more important.

In 644 adults who completed six 24-hour dietary recalls, each one-hour later average time of first intake was associated with a 0.44 kg/m² higher BMI. Independently, each one-hour increase in the within-person standard deviation of first-intake time was associated with a 1.12 kg/m² higher BMI. Greater first-intake and eating-midpoint variability were also associated with higher GlycA, a marker of systemic inflammation. Because the study was cross-sectional, these coefficients cannot be interpreted as causal effects or used to conclude that variability is more important than average timing. ([Guinter et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114519002125))

In 73 adults at increased risk of type 2 diabetes who completed seven to fourteen days of food logging, accelerometry, and continuous glucose monitoring, greater variability in first-meal time was associated with higher body fat. Greater variability in the interval between waking and the first meal was associated with higher HbA1c. Variability in the last meal and total eating-window duration was not significantly associated with those outcomes, and no meal-pattern component was associated with mean continuous glucose. The different timing predictors were generally modeled separately, limiting conclusions about their independent relative importance. ([Zhao et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114521002944))

Among 115 women assessed at baseline, 99 returned for the one-year follow-up, and 95 contributed at least six valid food-record days at follow-up. Greater day-to-day variability in the proportion of energy consumed after 8:00 pm was associated with subsequent increases in BMI and waist circumference. Weekday-weekend differences in late-evening calorie intake were also associated with BMI change. However, the study examined numerous correlated timing measures in a small female cohort, and it could not determine whether irregular intake caused the changes. ([Makarem et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1161/JAHA.121.022024))

A 2026 study combined 14 days of meal logs with continuous glucose monitoring in 96 adults aged 18 to 27. Days in the higher-variability glucose-profile cluster were characterized by fewer eating occasions, later breakfast, and a greater proportion of energy consumed at dinner, even though reported total energy intake was lower. The study examined meal timing, energy distribution, and frequency on those days, not between-day meal-time consistency as the principal exposure. It therefore does not show whether greater day-to-day meal-time variability caused the glucose-profile differences. ([Jeong and Song, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41387-026-00424-y))

## Is regularity of the entire eating window more important than dinner regularity?

The evidence does not establish a consistent hierarchy.

In the Guinter study, variability in eating-window duration and midpoint was initially associated with BMI, but these associations weakened after adjustment for variability in the first intake. This suggested that first-meal variability accounted for at least part of the whole-window association in that cohort. ([Guinter et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114519002125))

In the Zhao cohort, variability in first-meal timing and the wake-to-first-meal interval was associated with body fat or HbA1c, while variability in last-meal timing and eating-window duration was not. ([Zhao et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114521002944))

By contrast, the Makarem study implicated variability in late-evening calorie distribution, as well as variability in some measures of eating-window timing and fasting duration. It measured the percentage of energy consumed after 5:00 pm and 8:00 pm rather than dinner clock time alone. ([Makarem et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1161/JAHA.121.022024))

A study of 1,106 university students used the weekday-weekend difference in eating midpoint as a composite measure of “eating jet lag.” A larger shift was associated with higher BMI after adjustment for factors including chronotype, social jet lag, sleep duration, physical activity, and diet quality. The composite midpoint was the principal positive signal, while shifts in individual meal times were less consistently associated with BMI. This does not prove that regularity of the entire window is more important, because the study was cross-sectional and relied on reported usual meal times and body measurements. ([Zerón-Rugerio et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu11122980))

No trial has directly randomized people to regular versus irregular dinner while independently holding first-meal time, eating-window duration, meal frequency, food composition, and total energy intake constant. The claim that whole-window regularity matters more than dinner regularity, or vice versa, remains unresolved. ([Ali et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/jhn.13260); [Suyoto et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.advnut.2024.100310))

## Weekday-weekend changes and circadian alignment

“Eating jet lag” usually describes a difference in eating timing between weekdays and weekends, often calculated from the midpoint of the eating window. It is analogous to social jet lag but is not itself a direct measure of circadian phase. ([Zerón-Rugerio et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu11122980); [Makarem et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1161/JAHA.121.022024))

The university-student study found that greater eating jet lag was associated with higher BMI even after adjustment for social jet lag and chronotype. The one-year study in women similarly found associations between weekday-weekend differences in late-evening calorie intake and subsequent cardiometabolic changes. These findings suggest that weekend shifts contain information not fully captured by average timing alone. They do not establish that the shifts caused circadian misalignment or weight gain. ([Zerón-Rugerio et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu11122980); [Makarem et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1161/JAHA.121.022024))

Neither study measured melatonin timing, core-body-temperature phase, or another direct marker of the internal circadian clock. The controlled night-work experiment shows that changing the biological timing of food can alter internal alignment, but that result cannot automatically be transferred to ordinary weekend meal shifts. ([Chellappa et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abg9910))

## Work schedules, sleep, and other sources of confounding

Irregular meals may be a cause of metabolic disruption, a consequence of another disruption, or both.

A 2026 exploratory analysis included more than 20,000 adults and over 2.5 million food and beverage logs collected over two weeks with the myCircadianClock app. Eating-window duration and day-to-day first and last intake times varied substantially, and work schedules were associated with both eating patterns and food choices. The study was descriptive and did not test whether those patterns caused health outcomes. ([Tran et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42255-026-01504-0))

Shift work can simultaneously change sleep timing, sleep duration, light exposure, food availability, stress, physical activity, and the biological timing of meals. Even when an observational model adjusts for some of these variables, measurement error and residual confounding can remain. The 2026 shift-worker review found too few randomized meal-timing trials to determine how much of the cardiometabolic risk attributed to shift work is specifically mediated by meals. ([Wulandari et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261464874))

Several observational associations survived adjustment for measured sleep, chronotype, diet quality, activity, employment, smoking, or alcohol. This argues against the simplest claim that all associations are explained by one measured lifestyle factor. It does not eliminate unmeasured work conditions, socioeconomic constraints, health-related schedule changes, reporting error, or reverse causation. ([Guinter et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114519002125); [Zerón-Rugerio et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu11122980); [Makarem et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1161/JAHA.121.022024))

## Funding, conflicts of interest, and evidence independence

The core meal-regularity trials did not test a branded food, supplement, or commercial meal-timing program. The 2004 lean-women lipid study reported sponsorship from the Iranian Ministry of Health and Medical Education, while the later controlled studies received university support, including support from King Saud University. ([Farshchi et al., 2004b](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.ejcn.1601935); [Alhussain et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.115.125401); [Alhussain et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/nqab323))

The 2022 trial was funded by King Saud University. Ian Macdonald disclosed advisory relationships involving Nestlé Research, Novozymes, AIJN (European Fruit Juice Consortium), ILSI Europe, Mars, and Waltham. The other authors reported no conflicts. No commercial product was being evaluated, but the food-industry advisory relationships remain relevant when weighing the interpretation of a very small positive trial. ([Alhussain et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/nqab323))

The 2024 Ali systematic review declared no conflicts, and its lead author was supported through a PhD studentship from King Abdullah Medical City. However, review authors Ian Macdonald and Moira Taylor were also authors of the principal Nottingham intervention studies. This is not an industry conflict, but it means the review is not fully independent of the experimental evidence it evaluates. ([Ali et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/jhn.13260))

The separate 2024 systematic review by Suyoto and colleagues reported no conflicts of interest and non-commercial research support. ([Suyoto et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.advnut.2024.100310))

In the Zhao cohort, Satchidananda Panda disclosed royalties from the book _The Circadian Code_. In the final Tran publication, Panda disclosed authorship of _The Circadian Code_ and _The Circadian Diabetes Code_ and co-founding Circadian Biosystems; the other authors declared no competing interests. These disclosures do not invalidate the descriptive findings, but they should be considered when the findings are used to support commercial circadian interventions. ([Zhao et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114521002944); [Tran et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42255-026-01504-0))

## Limits and open debates

The strongest experimental evidence concerns meal-frequency regularity, not pure clock-time regularity. Varying between three and nine eating occasions changes meal size and fasting intervals as well as predictability. ([Farshchi et al., 2004a](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.ijo.0802616); [Alhussain et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.115.125401))

The four Nottingham metabolic studies lasted 14 days per condition and enrolled only nine to eleven women per cohort. They cannot establish effects on long-term weight change, diabetes incidence, cardiovascular events, or mortality. ([Farshchi et al., 2005](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/81.1.16); [Alhussain et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/nqab323))

No independently replicated or clinically validated threshold has been identified within which meals must occur. There is no established evidence-based rule that breakfast or dinner must remain within 30, 60, or 90 minutes of the previous day’s time. The observational studies generally analyze variability continuously or divide participants using study-specific categories. A later personalized scheduling pilot initially assigned a total 60-minute window around each specified meal time and reported a post hoc analysis using a total 90-minute window, but these were study-specific intervention choices rather than validated universal biological cutoffs. ([Ali et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/jhn.13260); [Suyoto et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.advnut.2024.100310); [Wilming et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.96837.3))

Average timing and variability are correlated in many populations. Later eaters may also have longer, shorter, or more variable windows, making it difficult to isolate each dimension statistically. ([Guinter et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114519002125); [Makarem et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1161/JAHA.121.022024); [Tran et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42255-026-01504-0))

## Quick evidence reference

- **Direct metabolic regular-versus-irregular trials:** Four very small female Nottingham cohorts, with 14 days per condition and nine to eleven participants per cohort. ([Farshchi et al., 2004a](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.ijo.0802616); [Farshchi et al., 2005](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/81.1.16); [Alhussain et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.115.125401); [Alhussain et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/nqab323))

- **Broader behavioral interventions:** A 25-person eating-and-sleep routine trial did not improve subsequent weight loss, while a later 100-completer Reviewed Preprint reported short-term weight loss after personalized meal scheduling but did not isolate regularity from changes in eating opportunities, eating-window structure, and possible energy intake. ([Demos et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1002/osp4.16); [Wilming et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.96837.3))

- **Most consistent metabolic-trial finding:** A greater three-hour thermic effect of food after the regular pattern. This has not been shown to cause long-term weight loss or prevent weight gain. ([Farshchi et al., 2004a](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.ijo.0802616); [Farshchi et al., 2005](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/81.1.16); [Alhussain et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.115.125401); [Alhussain et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/nqab323))

- **Insulin and glucose:** Some short trials found more favorable postprandial responses with regular eating, but fasting insulin resistance did not consistently improve. ([Farshchi et al., 2004b](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.ejcn.1601935); [Farshchi et al., 2005](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/81.1.16); [Alhussain et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.115.125401); [Alhussain et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/nqab323))

- **Average timing versus variability:** Both have been associated with outcomes; neither has been established as the dominant causal factor. ([Guinter et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114519002125))

- **Whole-window versus dinner regularity:** No direct comparison establishes that one is more important. ([Ali et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/jhn.13260); [Suyoto et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.advnut.2024.100310))

- **Weekday-weekend eating jet lag:** Associated with BMI and some cardiometabolic changes, but the evidence is observational and does not directly measure circadian phase. ([Zerón-Rugerio et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu11122980); [Makarem et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1161/JAHA.121.022024))

- **Shift work:** Daytime eating during simulated night work has produced favorable glucose and circadian effects, but a 2026 review found only four relevant randomized trials. ([Chellappa et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abg9910); [Wulandari et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261464874))

- **Validated timing tolerance:** None. Current evidence does not support a universal requirement to eat each meal within a particular number of minutes every day. Study-specific 60- or 90-minute windows should not be treated as validated biological cutoffs. ([Ali et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/jhn.13260); [Wilming et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.96837.3))


## Practical interpretation

- Broad consistency is reasonable when it fits a person’s work, sleep, and social schedule, but the evidence does not justify rigid minute-by-minute meal timing. ([Ali et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/jhn.13260); [Suyoto et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.advnut.2024.100310))

- Regularity should not be interpreted as making average timing irrelevant. Controlled night-work evidence indicates that consistently eating during the biological night may still impair glucose regulation. ([Chellappa et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abg9910))

- These observational findings do not show that occasional deviations from a usual meal schedule cause weight gain. The positive weekday-weekend findings come from associations rather than randomized interventions. ([Zerón-Rugerio et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu11122980); [Makarem et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1161/JAHA.121.022024))

- Irregular eating should also be treated as a possible marker of shift work or work-schedule constraints, rather than automatically being treated as an independent behavioral failure. ([Tran et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42255-026-01504-0); [Wulandari et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261464874))

- Meal regularity is one dimension of an eating pattern. Current evidence does not establish that it has effects independent of total energy intake, diet quality, eating-window structure, and average meal timing. ([Suyoto et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.advnut.2024.100310))
