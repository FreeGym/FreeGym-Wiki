# Does Meal Timing Affect Long-Term Disease and Longevity?

Meal timing may influence long-term health, but the human evidence is not strong enough to establish a causal ideal dinner time, breakfast time, or eating-window duration. ([Feit et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-026-01755-w); [Garegnani et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD015610.pub2); [Hu et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-026-01796-1))

Prospective cohorts have linked later first meals, very late last meals, short or long eating windows, and irregular eating patterns with some disease and mortality outcomes. However, the findings do not align consistently across populations. Many studies measure timing from only one or two dietary-recall days, several mortality analyses use overlapping data from the same US survey, and the randomized trials included in the 2026 Cochrane review lasted no longer than 12 months. That review focused on adults with overweight or obesity. Randomized trials of intermittent fasting and time-restricted eating have generally examined weight and metabolic risk factors over months rather than diabetes, cardiovascular events, cancer, or death over the long term. ([Feit et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-026-01755-w); [Palomar-Cros et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyad081); [Meth et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2022.889926); [Hu et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-026-01796-1); [Mao et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/acel.70230); [Garegnani et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD015610.pub2); [Liu et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2114833))

The most defensible conclusion is narrower: in a 2026 NHANES analysis based on a single baseline dietary recall, a first meal after midday and a last meal after midnight were associated with higher mortality. An earlier, overlapping NHANES analysis also associated later first eating with higher mortality. More ordinary differences in dinner timing produce mixed results, and observational evidence has not identified a particular eating-window length that extends life. ([Hu et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-026-01796-1); [Kant and Graubard, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/jn/nxab327); [Dashti et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43856-025-01035-x); [Feit et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-026-01755-w); [Mao et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/acel.70230); [Chen et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dsx.2025.103278); [Cheng et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12966-023-01556-5); [Li et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114524001739))

## Why long-term meal-timing evidence is difficult to interpret

A prospective cohort can establish that meal timing was recorded before a disease was diagnosed or a death occurred, but it cannot establish that timing preceded the biological onset of disease or that timing caused the outcome. People who eat later may also differ in sleep, chronotype, occupation, physical activity, diet quality, smoking, alcohol use, socioeconomic position, existing disease, or appetite. Statistical adjustment reduces these differences but cannot guarantee that they have been fully measured or removed. ([Feit et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-026-01755-w); [Palomar-Cros et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-023-43444-3); [Cahill et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jnha.2026.100825); [Dashti et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43856-025-01035-x))

Exposure measurement is another problem. The French NutriNet-Santé studies averaged approximately 5.7 dietary records per participant during the first two years, providing a better estimate of habitual timing than a single recall. By contrast, many US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey analyses estimated each person's eating window from one or two 24-hour recalls collected at baseline. A short eating window recorded on one day is not necessarily an intentional or habitual time-restricted eating practice. ([Palomar-Cros et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyad081); [Hu et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-026-01796-1); [Mao et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/acel.70230))

Several widely discussed mortality papers also use substantially overlapping NHANES cycles. Kant and Graubard used NHANES III from 1988 to 1994 and continuous NHANES from 1999 to 2014, Hu et al. used 1999 to 2018, Mao et al. and Chen et al. used 2003 to 2018, and Li et al. used 1999 to 2018. These are different analyses, but they should not be counted as fully independent cohort replications because many of the same participants contribute to more than one result. ([Kant and Graubard, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/jn/nxab327); [Hu et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-026-01796-1); [Mao et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/acel.70230); [Chen et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dsx.2025.103278); [Li et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114524001739))

## Type 2 diabetes

The largest repeated-record prospective analysis discussed here comes from the NutriNet-Santé cohort. The analysis included 103,312 adults, approximately 79 percent of whom were women. Meal timing was averaged across repeated dietary records obtained during the first two years. During a median follow-up of 7.3 years, 963 participants developed type 2 diabetes. ([Palomar-Cros et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyad081))

After adjustment for demographic factors, smoking, physical activity, alcohol, body mass index, dietary patterns, energy intake, eating frequency, and last-meal timing, having the first meal after 9 AM was associated with a higher diabetes incidence than having it before 8 AM: HR 1.59, 95 percent CI 1.30 to 1.94. ([Palomar-Cros et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyad081))

Dinner timing was less convincing. A last meal after 9 PM was initially associated with higher diabetes incidence, but the association weakened after first-meal timing and eating frequency were included in the model: HR 1.11, 95 percent CI 0.92 to 1.34. Night-time fasting duration was also not independently associated with diabetes overall. Compared with fasting for 12 hours or less, fasting for more than 13 hours produced an HR of 1.03, 95 percent CI 0.80 to 1.33. ([Palomar-Cros et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyad081))

A subgroup that combined a first meal at or before 8 AM with a fast longer than 13 hours had a lower estimated diabetes incidence, HR 0.47, but this result was based on only 15 cases in the long-fasting category. It should be treated as exploratory rather than evidence for a precise fasting prescription. ([Palomar-Cros et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyad081))

The findings were not reproduced in the same direction in an older US population. In 3,747 adults aged 65 or older from the Cardiovascular Health Study, 547 diabetes cases occurred during a median follow-up of 10.7 years, with a maximum follow-up of 17.6 years. Eating breakfast every day was not associated with diabetes incidence. A first eating occasion after 9 AM was associated with a lower, not higher, hazard than eating between 7 AM and 9 AM: HR 0.71, 95 percent CI 0.51 to 0.99. The estimate was borderline and weakened after additional adjustment for body mass index. It appeared mainly among participants with impaired fasting glucose, but the formal interaction by impaired fasting glucose was not statistically significant. Timing was measured using a baseline questionnaire rather than repeated dietary records. ([Carew et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/nqac087))

The diabetes evidence therefore does not support a universal breakfast cutoff. The NutriNet-Santé study provides a substantial association with later first meals, but an older cohort produced a borderline inverse association that was sensitive to model specification and not supported by a statistically significant subgroup interaction. Dinner timing and eating-window duration were not independently associated with diabetes in the main NutriNet-Santé analyses. ([Palomar-Cros et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyad081); [Carew et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/nqac087))

## Cardiovascular disease

In another NutriNet-Santé analysis, 103,389 adults were followed for a median of 7.2 years, during which 2,036 cardiovascular events were documented. Each hour of later first-meal timing was associated with a 6 percent higher hazard of overall cardiovascular disease. ([Palomar-Cros et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-023-43444-3))

The last-meal findings depended on the cardiovascular endpoint. A last meal after 9 PM versus before 8 PM was not clearly associated with overall cardiovascular disease after full adjustment: HR 1.13, 95 percent CI 0.99 to 1.29. However, later last-meal timing was associated with cerebrovascular disease. Eating after 9 PM versus before 8 PM produced an HR of 1.28, 95 percent CI 1.05 to 1.55, for cerebrovascular events. Longer night-time fasting was associated with lower cerebrovascular incidence, but not with overall cardiovascular disease or coronary heart disease. ([Palomar-Cros et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-023-43444-3))

The cohort used repeated dietary records and adjusted for numerous dietary and lifestyle variables. In the smaller subgroup with chronotype and sleep information, the association between later first meals and overall cardiovascular disease was moderately attenuated. The association between later last meals and cerebrovascular disease was also attenuated and was no longer statistically significant, although statistical power was lower. Associations were stronger in women and were not statistically significant in men. A negative-control analysis using basal-cell carcinoma did not produce the same associations. These steps strengthen the analysis, but the authors still identified residual confounding, volunteer selection, and the observational design as important limitations. ([Palomar-Cros et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-023-43444-3))

A 2026 Cardiovascular Health Study analysis found no association between breakfast frequency or usual breakfast time and coronary artery disease or myocardial infarction in 4,070 adults aged 65 or older. During follow-up of up to 26 years, 1,617 coronary artery disease cases, including 795 myocardial infarctions, occurred. The null findings remained after multivariable adjustment and analyses excluding early events. ([Cahill et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jnha.2026.100825))

Breakfast patterns in this older cohort were strongly associated with education, socioeconomic status, marital status, smoking, and fruit and vegetable intake. That illustrates how easily an apparent meal-timing effect can partly reflect the wider lifestyle and social pattern surrounding the meal. ([Cahill et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jnha.2026.100825))

Overall, one well-characterized French cohort suggests that later eating, particularly later first meals and last meals in relation to cerebrovascular events, may be relevant. The absence of a coronary association in the older US cohort means that the evidence is not yet consistent across ages, populations, and cardiovascular outcomes. ([Palomar-Cros et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-023-43444-3); [Cahill et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jnha.2026.100825))

## Cancer

The NutriNet-Santé cancer analysis included 41,389 day-working adults who completed at least three 24-hour dietary records during the first two years. During follow-up, 1,732 first primary cancers occurred, including 428 breast cancers and 179 prostate cancers. ([Srour et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1002/ijc.31584))

After adjustment for diet and lifestyle variables, including sleep duration, a last eating episode after 9:30 PM was associated with breast cancer, HR 1.48, 95 percent CI 1.02 to 2.17, and prostate cancer, HR 2.20, 95 percent CI 1.28 to 3.78. No clear associations were found for the number of eating episodes, night-time fasting duration, first-meal timing, or macronutrient composition of the final eating episode. ([Srour et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1002/ijc.31584))

These estimates were based on relatively small numbers of site-specific cancers, especially prostate cancer, and the study could not exclude residual confounding. The finding is therefore a signal requiring replication, not evidence that eating after 9:30 PM causes either cancer. ([Srour et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1002/ijc.31584))

A separate Swedish cohort examined eating duration and regularity in 941 men who were approximately 71 years old. Meal timing was recorded in seven-day food diaries. During an average of 13.4 years, 277 men developed cancer, and 191 died from cancer over an average of 14.7 years. ([Meth et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2022.889926))

Among the 496 men judged to have reliable dietary reports, an eating interval of approximately 13 hours was associated with higher fatal cancer risk than an interval of approximately 11 hours: HR 2.33, 95 percent CI 1.22 to 4.44. Day-to-day timing variability of approximately 48 to 74 minutes was associated with about twice the fatal cancer risk of variability averaging 23 minutes. However, these timing variables were not associated with cancer incidence, and the associations were absent among men with unreliable dietary reports. ([Meth et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2022.889926))

The two cancer studies discussed here provide observational signals that answer somewhat different questions. One linked a late last meal to breast and prostate cancer incidence. The other linked a longer and less regular eating pattern to cancer mortality, but not cancer incidence, in a small cohort of older men. Taken together, these two studies do not establish a preventive cutoff or schedule. ([Srour et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1002/ijc.31584); [Meth et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2022.889926))

## Ageing, changing health, and reverse causation

A 2025 British study provides unusually valuable repeated timing data. It included 2,945 adults with a mean baseline age of 64 years who completed meal-timing assessments at baseline and at least once more, with some completing up to five assessments. Over a mean follow-up of 22 years, 2,361 deaths occurred. ([Dashti et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43856-025-01035-x))

Breakfast shifted approximately 2.9 minutes later and dinner approximately 3.7 minutes later per decade after adjustment. More importantly, fatigue, depression, anxiety, multimorbidity, oral-health problems, poor sleep, and difficulty preparing meals were associated with changes in meal timing, particularly a later breakfast or shorter eating window. ([Dashti et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43856-025-01035-x))

Each hour of later breakfast timing was associated with an HR of 1.08 for all-cause mortality in the fully adjusted model, with a 95 percent CI of 1.00 to 1.17. Dinner timing, eating midpoint, and eating-window duration were not clearly associated with mortality in that model. Ten-year survival was 86.7 percent in the later-eating trajectory group and 89.5 percent in the earlier group. ([Dashti et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43856-025-01035-x))

These findings are compatible with more than one explanation. Later breakfast might contribute to poor health, but deteriorating health, fatigue, sleep disruption, depression, oral problems, and difficulty making food could also cause breakfast to move later. The investigators emphasized that meal-timing changes may serve as a marker of broader health changes during ageing. ([Dashti et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43856-025-01035-x))

## Meal timing and all-cause mortality

An earlier NHANES analysis of 34,609 adults aged 40 or older found that earlier first eating episodes were associated with modestly lower all-cause mortality. Compared with the latest first-eating group, whose median time was approximately 10 AM, the two earliest groups, with median times around 5:45 AM and 7 AM, both had an HR of 0.88. Timing was estimated from a baseline 24-hour recall. ([Kant and Graubard, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/jn/nxab327))

A 2026 analysis published on July 31, 2026, included 31,044 NHANES participants aged 40 or older. Meal timing was estimated from one 24-hour recall. During a median follow-up of 8.6 years, 7,129 deaths were recorded. ([Hu et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-026-01796-1))

Compared with a first meal between 7 AM and 8 AM, the HR for all-cause mortality was 1.10 for a first meal between 8 AM and 10 AM, 1.19 between 10 AM and midday, and 1.29 after midday. Compared with a last meal between 7 PM and 8 PM, eating after midnight was associated with an HR of 1.27. However, eating the last meal before 7 PM was also associated with higher mortality, HR 1.13, indicating that the association was not a simple earlier-is-always-better gradient. ([Hu et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-026-01796-1))

The investigators estimated that, at age 50, life expectancy was 2.46 years shorter among people whose first meal was after midday and 2.35 years shorter among those whose last meal was after midnight. These are model-derived differences between observational groups. They do not mean that experimentally moving a meal would necessarily add the corresponding number of years to a person's life. ([Hu et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-026-01796-1))

This study adds another large NHANES mortality analysis, but its central weakness is that a single recall was used to represent long-term timing. A first meal after midday on the recall day could reflect a habitual late pattern, but it could also reflect an unusual day, meal skipping, shift work, or a health-related change in appetite or meal preparation. ([Hu et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-026-01796-1); [Feit et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-026-01755-w); [Dashti et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43856-025-01035-x))

## Does eating-window duration independently predict longevity?

A recent systematic review of observational time-restricted eating studies was published in May 2026 and searched the literature through January 13, 2025. It included 12 cross-sectional studies and six cohort studies. ([Feit et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-026-01755-w))

The pooled cross-sectional analyses found no significant association between an eating window of 12 hours or less and abdominal obesity, metabolic syndrome, elevated blood pressure, low HDL cholesterol, elevated triglycerides, or prediabetes and type 2 diabetes. The prospective studies were too few and heterogeneous to support pooled conclusions about long-term outcomes. ([Feit et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-026-01755-w))

Several mortality studies published around or after the review's search date reported concerning associations with very short windows:

- In 33,052 NHANES participants, Mao et al. found a U-shaped association, with the lowest estimated all-cause mortality around an 11-hour to 12-hour window. Windows of 8 hours or less and 15 hours or more were both associated with higher mortality. ([Mao et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/acel.70230))

- In 19,831 NHANES participants, Chen et al. found that a window shorter than 8 hours, compared with 12 to 14 hours, was associated with cardiovascular mortality, HR 2.35. It was not associated with all-cause or cancer mortality. ([Chen et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dsx.2025.103278))

- Cheng et al. found U-shaped associations for night-time fasting, with both fasting for less than 10 hours and for more than 14 hours associated with higher all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. ([Cheng et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12966-023-01556-5))

- Li et al. found that longer eating duration was associated with modestly lower all-cause mortality, with the modeled low point at approximately 12.6 hours. Stratified curves differed descriptively across energy-intake and physical-activity groups, but formal interaction tests were not statistically significant. ([Li et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114524001739))


These results do not establish that an intentionally chosen 8-hour eating window increases mortality. The papers use overlapping NHANES populations, define windows and reference groups differently, and generally use one or two baseline recalls. A short window recorded in observational data may reflect illness, reduced appetite, meal skipping, low total intake, or another nonhabitual pattern and may not have the same implications as a planned eating window in an otherwise healthy person. ([Feit et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-026-01755-w); [Mao et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/acel.70230); [Chen et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dsx.2025.103278); [Dashti et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43856-025-01035-x))

The current evidence therefore does not identify an optimal eating-window duration for longevity. It also does not support interpreting observational mortality associations as proof that time-restricted eating itself is harmful. ([Feit et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-026-01755-w); [Mao et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/acel.70230); [Chen et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dsx.2025.103278); [Cheng et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12966-023-01556-5); [Li et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114524001739))

## Meal regularity and meal frequency

Among the studies discussed here, evidence about day-to-day clock-time regularity is much thinner than evidence about average meal timing. The Swedish study of older men found higher fatal cancer risk among participants whose daily eating timing varied by approximately 48 to 74 minutes compared with those whose variability averaged 23 minutes. The association appeared only among men with reliable dietary reports and was not accompanied by an association with cancer incidence. ([Meth et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2022.889926); [Tan and Shin, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1368980025000357))

A much larger South Korean cohort examined meal frequency rather than precise clock-time regularity. It included 148,438 adults categorized as eating once, twice, three times, four times, or irregularly per day. Compared with three meals per day, irregular frequency was associated with cardiovascular mortality, HR 1.83, but not all-cause mortality, HR 1.02, or cancer mortality, HR 0.68. Eating once or four times per day was associated with higher all-cause mortality, but these were relatively small exposure groups. ([Tan and Shin, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1368980025000357))

The authors reported that waist circumference statistically mediated much of the relationship between meal frequency and mortality. Because both the exposure and proposed mediator were measured observationally, this mediation analysis cannot establish that changing meal frequency would reduce waist circumference and thereby prevent death. ([Tan and Shin, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1368980025000357))

An irregular meal-frequency category is also not equivalent to measuring whether breakfast or dinner occurs at a consistent clock time each day. The available evidence is therefore suggestive but insufficient to establish that meal regularity independently improves longevity. ([Meth et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2022.889926); [Tan and Shin, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1368980025000357))

## Can short-term metabolic responses predict long-term disease?

Short-term feeding trials can test whether changing meal timing affects weight, glucose, insulin, blood pressure, lipids, appetite, or energy intake. They cannot directly determine whether the same intervention prevents clinical events decades later unless the measured changes are known to translate reliably into those outcomes. ([Garegnani et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD015610.pub2); [Liu et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2114833))

The 2026 Cochrane review of intermittent fasting in adults with overweight or obesity included 22 randomized trials and 1,995 participants. Compared with regular dietary advice, intermittent fasting produced little or no additional difference in weight loss or quality of life. Evidence about adverse events was uncertain. None of the included trials reported diabetes status or an overall measure of comorbidity, and the evidence extended only to 12 months. The review received no dedicated funding. ([Garegnani et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD015610.pub2))

In a 12-month randomized trial of 139 adults with obesity, restricting food intake to between 8 AM and 4 PM was added to calorie restriction and compared with calorie restriction alone. The additional difference in weight loss was 1.8 kg, with a 95 percent CI from 4.0 kg more weight loss to 0.4 kg less, and was not statistically significant. Differences in body fat and metabolic risk factors were also not substantial. ([Liu et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2114833))

These trials are important for testing feasibility and short-term physiology. The randomized evidence summarized here has not established whether earlier or shorter eating reduces diabetes, cardiovascular events, cancer, biological ageing, or mortality over the long term. ([Garegnani et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD015610.pub2); [Liu et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2114833))

## Residual confounding and reverse causation

Reverse causation is especially important in mortality and ageing analyses. Frailty, depression, fatigue, poor sleep, oral disease, difficulty preparing food, medication schedules, or loss of appetite can change when and how often a person eats. A later first meal or shorter eating window can therefore appear before death because health is already deteriorating, even when no diagnosed terminal disease was present at baseline. ([Dashti et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43856-025-01035-x))

Excluding deaths during the first one or two years and adjusting for diagnosed disease can reduce this problem, but it cannot remove undiagnosed illness, slowly developing frailty, or measurement error. Repeated timing measurements, repeated health assessments, negative-control outcomes, and analyses of changes within the same person are more informative, but even these do not substitute for randomization. ([Feit et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-026-01755-w); [Palomar-Cros et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-023-43444-3); [Dashti et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43856-025-01035-x); [Hu et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-026-01796-1))

This is why the repeated-measure ageing study is useful even though it does not resolve causation. It shows directly that meal timing changes alongside morbidity, sleep, psychological health, and meal-preparation difficulty. Meal timing may be part of the causal pathway, a consequence of declining health, or both. ([Dashti et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43856-025-01035-x))

## Quick reference

- **Later first meals:** Associated with higher diabetes and cardiovascular risk in NutriNet-Santé and with higher mortality in several NHANES analyses, but associated with lower diabetes incidence and no coronary benefit or harm in older US cohorts. ([Palomar-Cros et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyad081); [Palomar-Cros et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-023-43444-3); [Carew et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/nqac087); [Cahill et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jnha.2026.100825); [Kant and Graubard, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/jn/nxab327); [Hu et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-026-01796-1))

- **Late last meals:** Associated with cerebrovascular events in one cohort and with breast and prostate cancer in another. The association with diabetes disappeared after mutual adjustment, and overall cardiovascular disease results were not definitive. ([Palomar-Cros et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyad081); [Palomar-Cros et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-023-43444-3); [Srour et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1002/ijc.31584))

- **Post-midnight eating:** Associated with higher all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in a 2026 NHANES analysis, but timing was measured from one recall and causation remains unproven. ([Hu et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-026-01796-1))

- **Eating-window duration:** Prospective results are sparse and conflicting. Several prominent mortality analyses use overlapping NHANES cycles, and both very short and very long windows have been associated with mortality in some models. ([Feit et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-026-01755-w); [Hu et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-026-01796-1); [Mao et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/acel.70230); [Chen et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dsx.2025.103278); [Cheng et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12966-023-01556-5); [Li et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114524001739))

- **Meal regularity:** Limited evidence links greater day-to-day timing variability with fatal cancer and irregular meal frequency with cardiovascular mortality. These studies do not independently replicate the same exposure-outcome relationship because one measured clock-time variability and the other measured meal frequency. ([Meth et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2022.889926); [Tan and Shin, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1368980025000357))

- **Long-term causality:** Not established. The randomized evidence summarized here addresses short-term weight and metabolic outcomes, not disease events or longevity. ([Garegnani et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD015610.pub2); [Liu et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2114833))


## Practical interpretation

- There is no evidence-based universal rule that everyone must eat dinner before a particular clock time to prevent chronic disease. ([Palomar-Cros et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyad081); [Palomar-Cros et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-023-43444-3); [Srour et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1002/ijc.31584); [Hu et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-026-01796-1))

- In a 2026 NHANES analysis based on a single baseline dietary recall, a first meal after midday and eating after midnight were associated with higher mortality. An earlier, overlapping analysis also associated later first eating with higher mortality. These findings deserve further attention, but current studies cannot establish habitual exposure or show that moving those meals would extend life. ([Hu et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-026-01796-1); [Kant and Graubard, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/jn/nxab327))

- An 8-hour eating window should not be promoted or rejected specifically as a longevity intervention on the basis of current mortality cohorts. Window placement, energy intake, health status, appetite, and the reason for the short window may all matter. ([Feit et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-026-01755-w); [Mao et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/acel.70230); [Chen et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dsx.2025.103278); [Dashti et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43856-025-01035-x))

- In older adults, a progressive shift toward later meals or a shorter eating window may be useful as a marker of changing sleep, mood, oral health, meal-preparation ability, appetite, or multimorbidity rather than as an isolated behavior. ([Dashti et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43856-025-01035-x))

- Short-duration feeding studies can establish physiological plausibility. They cannot determine the magnitude or direction of effects on diabetes, cardiovascular events, cancer, ageing, or mortality over decades. ([Garegnani et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD015610.pub2); [Liu et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2114833))


The long-term evidence is not null, but it remains observational and internally inconsistent. It supports investigating habitual late and irregular eating with better repeated measurements and long-duration trials. It does not yet support claiming that a particular dinner time or fasting duration has been proven to prevent disease or prolong life. ([Feit et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-026-01755-w); [Dashti et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43856-025-01035-x); [Hu et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-026-01796-1); [Garegnani et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD015610.pub2))
