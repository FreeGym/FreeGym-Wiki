# Who May Need a Different Meal-Timing Pattern?

The same meal-timing rule should not be applied across every clinical, nutritional, occupational, and performance setting. A late snack can be rational when it addresses a defined problem, such as accelerated fasting metabolism in cirrhosis, inadequate daily intake, a short post-exercise recovery interval, or a medication-linked risk. That does not establish that a full late dinner is beneficial, or that everyone in one diagnostic category needs to eat before bed. ([Guo et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1155/2018/9189062); [Roach et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/nqac245); [Craven et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-020-00297-0); [Centofanti et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00125-024-06279-1))

The most defensible conclusion is that meal timing should be individualized according to the reason for eating, total energy and protein requirements, medication exposure, glucose patterns, training demands, and the person's actual sleep-work cycle. The strength and meaning of the evidence differ substantially by population. This article examines those exceptions; it does not determine whether an early or late dinner is better for the general population. ([Thomson et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2666-7568%2822%2900171-4); [Obermayer et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.2337/dc22-1622); [Wulandari et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261464874))

## A late snack is not a late dinner

"Late eating" is not one exposure. In a 2025 simulated night-work trial, the night snack supplied 10 percent of daily energy, whereas the night meal supplied 30 percent. After four night shifts, deterioration in glucose tolerance was greatest with the meal, intermediate with the snack, and smallest with fasting. Insulin sensitivity still worsened across all three conditions, so fasting did not remove the metabolic effect of night work itself. ([Centofanti et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00125-024-06279-1))

Other late-snack studies ask different questions. A 2024 trial in undernourished older adults added a 247 kcal, 12 g protein supplement before bed and compared it with water, while athlete trials commonly test a defined protein or carbohydrate dose around exercise. These designs cannot determine whether an isocaloric full dinner would have the same effect, whether the same food would work equally well earlier, or whether added calories and timing made separate contributions. ([Jayawardena et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/jhn.13334); [Casuso and Goossens, 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu17132070))

The practical question is therefore not simply "late or not late." It is what is eaten, how much, whether it is added or redistributed, what need it serves, and how close it falls to sleep, medication action, work, or exercise. ([Jayawardena et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/jhn.13334); [Obermayer et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.2337/dc22-1622); [Craven et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-020-00297-0); [Centofanti et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00125-024-06279-1))

## Cirrhosis: the clearest disease-specific exception

Cirrhosis has the most direct evidence supporting a late-evening snack. The intervention is intended to shorten the overnight period without energy intake in a condition associated with an earlier shift toward fasting metabolism. ([Guo et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1155/2018/9189062))

A 2018 systematic review and meta-analysis of 14 trials involving 478 people found that late-evening snacks increased morning respiratory quotient and carbohydrate oxidation and improved serum albumin and cholinesterase. The analysis did not find significant improvements in alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, or bilirubin. These are mainly metabolic and laboratory outcomes, not direct measures of survival, hospitalization, transplantation, or physical function. ([Guo et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1155/2018/9189062))

A separate 2019 meta-analysis of eight randomized trials involving 341 patients also reported improvements in several biochemical measures, but not in Child-Pugh score. The trials were generally small and short, and the review did not establish a benefit for patient-important clinical outcomes. ([Chen et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/jgh.14665))

This evidence supports treating a planned late-evening snack as a disease-specific nutrition strategy in cirrhosis. It does not support replacing that snack with an unrestricted late dinner, and it does not show that the same approach benefits people without cirrhosis. The snack's energy, protein, and carbohydrate content should fit the person's overall clinical nutrition plan. ([Guo et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1155/2018/9189062); [Chen et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/jgh.14665))

## Frailty, sarcopenia, and undernutrition: intake first, timing second

For frail or undernourished older adults, the better-supported objective is correcting inadequate energy and nutrient intake. Evidence that bedtime is a uniquely effective time to do this is much weaker. ([Thomson et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2666-7568%2822%2900171-4); [Jayawardena et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/jhn.13334))

A 2022 systematic review included 11 randomized trials and 822 frail older adults who were malnourished or at risk. Oral nutritional supplements may have increased energy intake, but most assessed outcomes were supported by very-low-certainty evidence, and the review found little evidence that supplements reduced malnutrition or its adverse clinical outcomes. Six of the 11 trials were fully or partly industry-funded, a concentration of sponsorship that further limits confidence in broad claims. ([Thomson et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2666-7568%2822%2900171-4))

In the 2024 bedtime trial, 50 institutionalized older adults were randomized and 42 completed 12 weeks. The group receiving the 247 kcal, 12 g protein supplement before bed gained more body weight and estimated lean mass than the water control. However, there was no equal supplement given earlier in the day, so the trial demonstrates a benefit from adding nutrition, not a unique benefit from bedtime timing. The trial was funded by Kalbe Lanka (Pvt) Ltd, which supplied the supplement; its favorable findings should be interpreted with that financial relationship in mind and require independent replication. ([Jayawardena et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/jhn.13334))

Timing also appears secondary when intake is already adequate. In a 2025 randomized trial of 30 older men completing 12 weeks of resistance training, 40 g of protein taken before sleep did not improve muscle thickness or strength compared with the same dose after exercise or resistance training alone. Participants already consumed at least 1.0 g/kg/day of protein at baseline, which limits extrapolation to undernourished or protein-deficient adults. ([Klemp et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/15502783.2025.2519511))

For this group, a late snack is reasonable when it is a practical way to close a documented energy or protein gap without displacing daytime food. Frailty or sarcopenia by itself does not prove that eating at bedtime is superior. The relevant outcomes are whether total intake, weight trajectory, strength, function, and tolerance improve. ([Thomson et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2666-7568%2822%2900171-4); [Klemp et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/15502783.2025.2519511))

## Pregnancy: insufficient evidence for a universal fasting rule

Pregnancy is often included in warnings about long fasting intervals, but the direct evidence is limited. Most outcome data concern Ramadan fasting, are observational, and do not isolate the effects of overnight fasting from changes in sleep, daytime intake, meal composition, culture, or fasting duration. ([Al-Taiar et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/ijgo.16127); [Giorno et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu17223546))

A 2025 umbrella review of 13 reviews found little evidence that Ramadan fasting increased preterm birth or low birthweight. It also found that the underlying literature was heterogeneous and that important rare outcomes, including stillbirth, miscarriage, and neonatal death, were not adequately covered by the included reviews. Absence of a clear association for common outcomes is therefore not proof that every fasting pattern is safe in every pregnancy. ([Al-Taiar et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/ijgo.16127))

A separate 2025 systematic review included 19 observational studies, six of which entered a birthweight meta-analysis. Fasting was associated with a small mean reduction in birthweight of about 94 g, with substantial heterogeneity, but not with a higher risk of low birthweight. Because none of the included studies was randomized, the result cannot establish that fasting caused the difference or define a safe fasting duration. The review reported no external funding. ([Giorno et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu17223546))

The evidence supports neither a blanket instruction to fast nor a blanket instruction to eat a late snack during pregnancy. A late snack may be useful when it helps meet an individualized nutrition or glucose-management plan, but pregnancy-specific symptoms, nutritional status, fetal growth, and any diabetes treatment change the decision. The Ramadan literature does not test all of those situations. ([Al-Taiar et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/ijgo.16127); [Giorno et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu17223546))

## Diabetes and glucose-lowering medication: snacks are not automatic

Diabetes creates two distinct questions: whether a bedtime snack improves overnight glycemia, and whether extending a fast is safe with the current medication regimen. The first does not have a universal positive answer. A 2022 systematic review of 16 intervention studies in type 2 diabetes or prediabetes found no consistent improvement in fasting or nocturnal glucose from a bedtime snack, especially when studies included a no-snack comparison. Evidence in people using insulin or sulfonylureas was particularly sparse. ([Roach et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/nqac245))

Routine snacks are not reliably protective in type 1 diabetes either. In a 2025 randomized crossover trial of 28 children using continuous glucose monitoring and multiple daily injections, carbohydrate-containing bedtime snacks did not reduce time below range and substantially worsened overnight time in range compared with no snack. The authors reported no duality of interest. This small pediatric study is not a rule for adults, but it directly argues against prescribing the same snack to everyone. ([Gokce et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41387-025-00392-9))

Longer fasting can raise hypoglycemia risk when glucose-lowering medication remains active. In a 12-week randomized trial of 41 adults with type 2 diabetes using metformin and/or hypoglycemia-causing medication, two very-low-calorie days per week approximately doubled the hypoglycemia rate on fasting days despite education and planned medication reduction. This was an extended intermittent-fasting intervention, not an ordinary overnight interval. ([Corley et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/dme.13595))

Structured fasting can also be feasible. In the 12-week INTERFAST-2 trial, 46 adults with insulin-treated type 2 diabetes were randomized to three fasting days per week or usual care. With continuous glucose monitoring, dietary counseling, and protocolized insulin changes, fasting reduced HbA1c, body weight, and insulin dose without increasing time below range or causing severe hypoglycemia. The study was publicly funded and reported no relevant conflicts of interest. Its safety result applies to a supervised protocol with medication adjustment, not to unmonitored fasting on an unchanged insulin regimen. ([Obermayer et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.2337/dc22-1622))

The practical conclusion is not that everyone using insulin or an insulin secretagogue needs food at bedtime. It is that fasting duration, medication dosing, glucose monitoring, recent exercise, and an individual's history of nocturnal hypoglycemia must be considered together. A routine snack can worsen overnight hyperglycemia, while extending a fast without an appropriate medication plan can increase hypoglycemia risk. ([Roach et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/nqac245); [Corley et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/dme.13595); [Obermayer et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.2337/dc22-1622))

## Evening-training athletes: recovery can override a rigid cutoff

Evening training changes the purpose of late eating. The relevant question is whether the athlete still needs energy, carbohydrate, protein, or fluid for recovery, especially when another demanding session follows soon. It is not whether everyone who exercises needs a meal immediately before sleep. ([Craven et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-020-00297-0); [Casuso and Goossens, 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu17132070))

A 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis of 29 trials involving 246 participants found that carbohydrate accelerated muscle-glycogen restoration during recovery periods of up to eight hours compared with water or noncaloric control. Adding protein did not further increase glycogen restoration when sufficient carbohydrate was supplied. The samples were overwhelmingly male, and protocols varied, which limits precise prescriptions. ([Craven et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-020-00297-0))

Protein timing is less decisive than popular "anabolic window" claims imply. A 2025 meta-analysis restricted to randomized, direct before-versus-after exercise comparisons found no difference in lean mass or chest-press strength by timing; evidence came from only six reports representing five studies. The review reported no external funding and no conflicts of interest. ([Casuso and Goossens, 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu17132070))

For an athlete with another glycogen-demanding session within about eight hours, delaying all recovery nutrition until morning may be a poor trade-off. For an athlete who ate recently, has met daily requirements, and has a long recovery window, an urgent large meal is not established as necessary. Neither meta-analysis directly compares an isocaloric small post-training snack with a full late dinner, so choosing between them should be based on the remaining nutrition requirement and tolerance rather than a claim that one is inherently healthier. ([Craven et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-020-00297-0); [Casuso and Goossens, 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu17132070))

## Shift workers: biological night is not simply displaced daytime

Shift workers need a strategy anchored to both work demands and biological sleep timing. In the 2025 laboratory trial, 55 healthy adults were assigned to fast, eat a 10-percent-of-energy snack, or eat a 30-percent-of-energy meal at 00:30 during simulated night work; 52 completed the protocol. After four night shifts and a recovery sleep, glucose tolerance worsened most after the full meal, less after the snack, and least after fasting. The study was publicly funded, and the authors declared no relationships or activities that could appear to influence the work. ([Centofanti et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00125-024-06279-1))

The result does not prove that night-shift workers should universally fast. Participants were young, healthy people without obesity who were studied for six days in a laboratory, not experienced shift workers followed for clinical outcomes. Insulin sensitivity worsened in all three groups, and avoiding food at night did not reverse the sleep disruption caused by the simulated schedule. ([Centofanti et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00125-024-06279-1))

The most current systematic review, published in 2026, found only four randomized trials of meal-timing interventions in shift-work settings: three tested night-fasting periods and one tested a self-selected 10-hour eating window. The review found some favorable postprandial and lipid findings but concluded that the small number of trials and generally weak methods limited robustness and generalizability. The authors declared no potential conflicts of interest. ([Wulandari et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261464874))

The best-supported provisional strategy is to place most intake in the biological daytime and avoid a substantial meal during the biological night when this is feasible and safe. A small snack is not metabolically identical to a full meal and may be a practical compromise when hunger, workload, pregnancy, recovery, or medication makes fasting unsuitable. Existing trials do not define one safe schedule for all occupations or all workers. ([Centofanti et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00125-024-06279-1); [Wulandari et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261464874))

## Quick reference

- **Cirrhosis:** A planned late-evening snack has the clearest condition-specific evidence, but benefits are mainly metabolic and biochemical; hard clinical outcomes remain unproven. ([Guo et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1155/2018/9189062); [Chen et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/jgh.14665))

- **Frailty or undernutrition:** Use a late snack if it helps close a documented energy or protein gap. Evidence does not establish bedtime as uniquely effective, and much of the supplement literature has very low certainty and substantial industry funding. ([Thomson et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2666-7568%2822%2900171-4); [Jayawardena et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/jhn.13334))

- **Sarcopenia with adequate intake:** Pre-sleep protein has not shown an advantage over other timing in the small available older-adult training trial. ([Klemp et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/15502783.2025.2519511))

- **Pregnancy:** Observational Ramadan evidence does not justify a universal fast or a universal late snack; nutritional status and clinical circumstances matter. ([Al-Taiar et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/ijgo.16127); [Giorno et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu17223546))

- **Diabetes or glucose-lowering medication:** Routine bedtime snacks are not supported. Any extended fast should be linked to the medication regimen, glucose data, and a safety plan. ([Roach et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/nqac245); [Corley et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/dme.13595); [Obermayer et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.2337/dc22-1622))

- **Evening-training athletes:** Recovery nutrition can take priority when daily requirements remain unmet or the next demanding session is close. Evidence does not establish that a full late dinner is necessary. ([Craven et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-020-00297-0); [Casuso and Goossens, 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu17132070))

- **Shift workers:** Avoiding a large biological-night meal has stronger short-term laboratory support than a total night fast has long-term clinical support. A small snack produced an intermediate glucose response in the best direct meal-size trial. ([Centofanti et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00125-024-06279-1); [Wulandari et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261464874))


## Practical interpretation

- Start with the reason for changing the schedule: cirrhosis-related fasting metabolism, inadequate intake, a documented glucose pattern, medication exposure, short-turnaround exercise recovery, or night work. The diagnostic label alone is not enough. ([Guo et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1155/2018/9189062); [Roach et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/nqac245); [Craven et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-020-00297-0))

- Define the intervention precisely. A small targeted snack, a full meal, and an extended fast produce different exposures and cannot be substituted for one another in interpreting the evidence. ([Centofanti et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00125-024-06279-1); [Jayawardena et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/jhn.13334))

- Determine whether the food is being added or redistributed. When a bedtime supplement is compared only with water, better weight gain cannot establish that bedtime is superior to an equal daytime dose. ([Jayawardena et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/jhn.13334))

- Link fasting decisions to medication decisions. The supervised insulin-adjustment protocol in INTERFAST-2 cannot be reproduced by simply skipping food while leaving the regimen unchanged. ([Obermayer et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.2337/dc22-1622); [Corley et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/dme.13595))

- Judge the pattern by the outcome it is meant to improve: total intake and weight in undernutrition, overnight glucose and hypoglycemia in diabetes, recovery before the next session in athletes, or feasibility and postprandial response in shift workers. ([Thomson et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2666-7568%2822%2900171-4); [Craven et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-020-00297-0); [Wulandari et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261464874))


Individualization is therefore the best-supported position, but it is not an invitation to treat all meal patterns as equivalent. It means matching a clearly defined timing change to a clearly defined clinical, nutritional, occupational, or performance need, then monitoring whether that need is actually met. ([Guo et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1155/2018/9189062); [Roach et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/nqac245); [Craven et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-020-00297-0); [Wulandari et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261464874))
