# ALT Thresholds: Are the “Normal” Limits Too High?

Many conventional alanine aminotransferase (ALT) upper limits are probably too high to function as a reassuring boundary between health and disease. They were often derived from reference populations that included people with metabolic dysfunction, excess body weight, or unrecognized steatotic liver disease. [Prati et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12093239/), [Valenti et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34520121/), [Choi et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38750867/), [Åberg et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41255223/)

That does not mean that 30 U/L for men and 19 U/L for women are universal biological cutoffs. Those values came from one influential study using a particular population and laboratory method. Selected recent rigorously screened cohorts using one-sided 95th-percentile approaches have produced upper limits ranging from approximately 22 to 42 U/L, depending on sex, population, assay, exclusion criteria, and statistical method. [Prati et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12093239/), [Valenti et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34520121/), [Choi et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38750867/), [Åberg et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41255223/)

The most defensible conclusion is therefore narrower: an ALT in the 30-40 U/L range should not automatically be dismissed merely because it falls below a laboratory’s reported upper limit. It is a signal to interpret in context, not proof of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), and not necessarily evidence that a mild post-exercise elevation originated primarily in the liver. [ACG](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27995906/), [AASLD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10735173/), [EASL-EASD-EASO](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11299976/), [Pettersson et al.](https://bpspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1365-2125.2007.03001.x), [Tirabassi et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29558239/)

## Verdict on the four competing claims

**1. Current ALT upper limits are too high: mostly supported, but the exact 30/19 proposal is not universal.**

Studies using carefully selected, metabolically healthy reference groups have often produced lower limits than some traditional laboratory ranges, although the result depends strongly on the percentile convention and assay. Results differ enough that 30/19 should not be treated as an assay-independent diagnostic boundary. [Prati et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12093239/), [Valenti et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34520121/), [Choi et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38750867/), [Åberg et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41255223/)

**2. Lower thresholds would increase false positives: supported as a sensitivity-specificity tradeoff, but the claimed net harm is not established.**

Lowering a cutoff necessarily identifies more people, including some without the target disease. The original 30/19 study demonstrated this sensitivity-specificity tradeoff directly. It did not measure anxiety, imaging use, referral volume, or downstream harms from routine implementation, so the net clinical burden of applying the threshold cannot be estimated from that study alone. [Prati et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12093239/)

**3. Clinical context should modify interpretation: strongly supported. Separate permanent “sedentary” and “athlete” ALT ranges are not.**

Metabolic risk and recent strenuous exercise affect the probability that a mildly elevated ALT represents liver disease. The better solution is contextual follow-up, not declaring a chronically higher ALT normal simply because someone exercises. The major guidelines reviewed do not provide a separate athlete-specific ALT upper limit. [ACG](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27995906/), [AASLD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10735173/), [EASL-EASD-EASO](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11299976/), [APASL](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40016576/), [Pettersson et al.](https://bpspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1365-2125.2007.03001.x), [Tirabassi et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29558239/)

**4. ALT should not be the primary method of detecting MASLD: substantially correct, although universal imaging is not the preferred alternative.**

ALT has insufficient sensitivity to exclude steatosis, steatohepatitis, or advanced fibrosis. Current guidance generally favors targeted case-finding for clinically significant fibrosis in people with metabolic risk or suspected MASLD, often beginning with a fibrosis score such as FIB-4 and using elastography or another second-line test when indicated, not ultrasound or FibroScan for every adult. [Ma et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31937252/), [Verma et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23763360/), [AASLD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10735173/), [EASL-EASD-EASO](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11299976/)

## What an ALT upper limit actually represents

A laboratory reference interval and a clinical decision limit are not necessarily the same thing. [Ozarda et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/)

A reference limit is usually derived statistically from a group classified as healthy. When the upper limit is defined as a one-sided ninety-fifth percentile, approximately 5 percent of that selected healthy group will exceed it by definition. Many laboratories instead use the upper boundary of a central 95 percent reference interval, which is the 97.5th percentile and leaves approximately 2.5 percent above it. The chosen percentile and statistical method can materially change the resulting limit. Neither convention directly identifies the ALT value at which fatty liver, fibrosis, or future liver-related complications begin. [Ozarda et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/), [Åberg et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41255223/)

A clinical decision limit is chosen according to a different question: at what value should clinicians investigate, repeat the test, calculate another score, or perform imaging? Such a limit can reasonably differ from a conventional reference limit because it is tied to clinical risk and action rather than only to the distribution in a reference population. [Ozarda et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/)

The laboratory method also matters. ALT values obtained with an International Federation of Clinical Chemistry-standardized assay are not necessarily interchangeable with values produced by older, nonstandardized methods. Valenti and colleagues specifically warned that applying cutoffs derived with one method to results produced by another can cause classification errors. [Valenti et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34520121/)

## Where the 30 U/L and 19 U/L limits came from

The best-known lower limits were proposed by Prati and colleagues in 2002. The investigators studied 6,835 first-time blood donors and selected the subgroup at lowest apparent risk of liver disease. ALT was independently related to body mass index and markers of abnormal carbohydrate and lipid metabolism, supporting the concern that conventional reference populations were metabolically contaminated. [Prati et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12093239/)

The study proposed upper limits of:

- **30 U/L for men**
- **19 U/L for women**

The authors then tested the revised limits in people with hepatitis C virus infection. Compared with the then-current cutoffs of 40 U/L in men and 30 U/L in women, the lower thresholds increased sensitivity for HCV viremia from 55.0 percent to 76.3 percent. Specificity fell from 97.4 percent to 88.5 percent. The additional cases detected tended to have minimal or mild histologic lesions. [Prati et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12093239/)

This was important evidence that traditional limits could miss HCV viremia accompanied by minimal or mild histologic lesions. But the validation disease was hepatitis C, not MASLD, and the study did not test whether lowering the reported laboratory limit would prevent cirrhosis, reduce mortality, or be cost-effective in a general screening population. [Prati et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12093239/)

The 30/19 values are therefore historically important clinical-warning thresholds, not diagnostic cutoffs that establish liver disease in every person above them. [Prati et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12093239/), [Valenti et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34520121/), [Choi et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38750867/), [Åberg et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41255223/)

## Modern “healthy” cohorts do not produce one universal cutoff

A 2021 study screened 21,296 apparently healthy blood donors and derived its principal upper reference limits from 9,195 donors who met the low-risk criteria. Using a modern standardized ALT method, it estimated upper reference limits of **42 U/L for men and 30 U/L for women**. These values were lower than the limits then recommended for that standardized method, but higher than 30/19. The revised values were associated with steatosis and fibrosis in validation groups, although accuracy for detecting these conditions remained limited. [Valenti et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34520121/)

A 2024 study examined 5,455 potential living-liver donors. Among 1,553 participants who had no histologic steatosis and met strict metabolic-health criteria, the ninety-fifth-percentile upper limits were **34 U/L for men and 22 U/L for women**. Among participants at or above those limits, 72.8 percent of men and 55.0 percent of women had steatosis. However, this was a predominantly young Asian donor population, so its precise values cannot automatically be transferred to every laboratory or demographic group. [Choi et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38750867/)

A 2025 Finnish population study also illustrated the effect of the statistical definition. In its healthiest subgroup, one-sided 95th-percentile ALT limits were **41 U/L for men and 27 U/L for women**, whereas 97.5th-percentile limits were **57 U/L and 35 U/L**, respectively. This does not establish the higher values as better clinical decision thresholds. It shows that the reported reference limit depends materially on the percentile and statistical method. [Åberg et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41255223/)

These studies support four conclusions:

1. Reference groups containing metabolic disease can inflate ALT limits. [Prati et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12093239/), [Valenti et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34520121/), [Choi et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38750867/), [Åberg et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41255223/)
2. Sex-specific limits are appropriate. [Ozarda et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/), [Prati et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12093239/), [Valenti et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34520121/), [Choi et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38750867/), [Åberg et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41255223/)
3. The exact result depends on the assay and the population selected as healthy. [Valenti et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34520121/), [Choi et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38750867/), [Åberg et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41255223/)
4. The statistical definition of the upper boundary also matters. [Åberg et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41255223/)

Thus, the evidence supports lowering some traditional limits, but not adopting 30/19 as an immutable worldwide standard. [Prati et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12093239/), [Valenti et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34520121/), [Choi et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38750867/), [Åberg et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41255223/)

## How much MASLD does a “normal” ALT miss?

Lowering the threshold improves sensitivity, but it cannot turn ALT into a reliable MASLD screening test. [Ma et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31937252/), [Verma et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23763360/)

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 11 studies containing 4,084 patients found that approximately **25 percent of people with NAFLD and 19 percent of people with NASH had ALT values classified as normal**. Heterogeneity was high, partly because the included studies used different populations, diagnostic methods, and definitions of normal ALT. [Ma et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31937252/)

In a biopsy-based cohort of 222 people with NAFLD, 56 of 222, approximately one quarter, had normal ALT. Among those with normal ALT, 37.5 percent nevertheless had NASH or advanced fibrosis. Conversely, 53 percent of those with elevated ALT had neither NASH nor advanced fibrosis. ALT’s area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve was only 0.62 for NASH and 0.46 for advanced fibrosis, leading the authors to conclude that there was no optimal ALT threshold for predicting either condition. [Verma et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23763360/)

ALT is a marker of hepatocellular stress or injury. It does not directly quantify liver fat, histologic inflammation, fibrosis, portal hypertension, or liver synthetic function. Some people with substantial steatosis or fibrosis have ALT values classified as normal, while others have mild ALT elevations for reasons unrelated to MASLD. [Ma et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31937252/), [Verma et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23763360/), [ACG](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27995906/), [AASLD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10735173/), [EASL-EASD-EASO](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11299976/)

Consequently, a lower ALT limit would detect additional people with metabolic liver disease, but a normal result, even under the lower limit, would still not exclude it. [Ma et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31937252/), [Verma et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23763360/), [AASLD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10735173/), [EASL-EASD-EASO](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11299976/)

## Exercise-induced ALT elevation

The exercise issue is real, but the strongest evidence reviewed here concerns strenuous, prolonged, or unfamiliar exercise, not ordinary habitual activity in every physically active person. [Pettersson et al.](https://bpspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1365-2125.2007.03001.x), [Tirabassi et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29558239/)

In one experimental study, 15 healthy men who were accustomed to moderate activity but not weightlifting were enrolled for an approximately one-hour weightlifting session. One withdrew after the exercise, leaving 14 participants in the statistical analysis. ALT, AST, lactate dehydrogenase, creatine kinase, and myoglobin increased and remained elevated for at least seven days. Bilirubin, gamma-glutamyl transferase, and alkaline phosphatase were almost unaltered overall, although one participant exceeded the local upper limit for bilirubin and one exceeded it for alkaline phosphatase. The parallel creatine kinase and myoglobin elevations supported skeletal muscle injury as a substantial contributor to the aminotransferase pattern. [Pettersson et al.](https://bpspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1365-2125.2007.03001.x)

This study was very small, included only men, and examined an unfamiliar muscle-damaging workout. It also has relevant institutional context: the authors were affiliated with AstraZeneca R&D in Lund or Mölndal, and the study was framed partly around avoiding misleading safety signals during drug development. [Pettersson et al.](https://bpspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1365-2125.2007.03001.x)

An observational study of a 161-km ultramarathon at altitude found even larger changes. Among 36 runners with paired samples, mean ALT increased from 23 to 117 U/L and mean AST from 23 to 485 U/L. ALT, AST, and lactate dehydrogenase correlated with creatine kinase, a pattern consistent with a strong muscle-injury contribution. The correlations do not prove that all of the aminotransferase increase originated in skeletal muscle or exclude concurrent hepatic stress. This was an extreme endurance event and cannot be extrapolated directly to a normal gym session. [Tirabassi et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29558239/)

The two studies therefore demonstrate that strenuous exercise can create transient “abnormal” ALT results. Because one examined an unfamiliar weightlifting session in 14 analyzed participants and the other a 161-km ultramarathon, neither provides a reliable estimate of the false-positive rate produced by a 30/19 threshold in habitual recreational athletes, trained strength athletes, or endurance athletes undergoing ordinary training. [Pettersson et al.](https://bpspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1365-2125.2007.03001.x), [Tirabassi et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29558239/)

An ALT of 35 U/L the day after a demanding resistance session could plausibly be exercise-related. But that interpretation becomes stronger when ALT is considered alongside the following findings and context. [Pettersson et al.](https://bpspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1365-2125.2007.03001.x), [ACG](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27995906/)

- AST and creatine kinase
- Gamma-glutamyl transferase and alkaline phosphatase
- Bilirubin
- Symptoms and medication or supplement exposure
- The timing and muscle-damaging nature of the workout
- Whether the elevation resolves after recovery

Persistent elevation should not be attributed to fitness without evaluating liver and non-liver causes. [ACG](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27995906/), [AASLD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10735173/)

## Would lower thresholds produce too many false positives?

The sensitivity-specificity tradeoff is unavoidable. In the original Prati validation, lowering the thresholds increased sensitivity by approximately 21 percentage points while reducing specificity by approximately 9 percentage points. [Prati et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12093239/)

However, the term **false positive** depends on the condition being tested. Someone above 30/19 who does not have MASLD may still have viral hepatitis, alcohol-related injury, medication-associated injury, autoimmune disease, iron overload, or recent muscle damage. An elevated ALT is not a positive diagnostic test for one specific disease; it is a nonspecific biochemical signal. [ACG](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27995906/), [Pettersson et al.](https://bpspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1365-2125.2007.03001.x)

The stronger criticism is therefore not that every additional flag would be false. It is that a lower flagging threshold, if linked automatically to imaging or specialist referral, could increase low-yield investigations. That is a plausible implementation risk rather than an outcome directly measured by the threshold studies. [Prati et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12093239/), [Valenti et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34520121/), [Choi et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38750867/), [Åberg et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41255223/)

The threshold studies cited here are observational. They describe ALT distributions, associations, and diagnostic sensitivity, but they do not test whether changing the laboratory flag improves patient-important outcomes or directly measure anxiety, incidental findings, referral volume, or complications from additional testing. [Prati et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12093239/), [Valenti et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34520121/), [Choi et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38750867/), [Åberg et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41255223/)

A staged response is more consistent with current guideline pathways than automatic escalation. Mild elevations can first be confirmed and interpreted against exercise, alcohol, medicines, metabolic risk, other liver tests, and previous results. More intensive fibrosis testing can then be concentrated in people with persistent abnormalities or meaningful cardiometabolic risk. This is an inference from the diagnostic and guideline evidence rather than a strategy proven in a randomized threshold trial. [ACG](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27995906/), [AASLD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10735173/), [EASL-EASD-EASO](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11299976/), [Pettersson et al.](https://bpspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1365-2125.2007.03001.x)

## Why separate athlete and sedentary thresholds are not the best solution

Clinical context should change the probability assigned to an ALT result, but it does not necessarily require two definitions of normal. [ACG](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27995906/), [AASLD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10735173/), [EASL-EASD-EASO](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11299976/), [Pettersson et al.](https://bpspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1365-2125.2007.03001.x)

Metabolic risk raises the pretest probability of MASLD. Thus, an overweight person with type 2 diabetes and an ALT of 28 U/L may warrant fibrosis risk assessment despite having an ALT below almost every proposed cutoff. [AASLD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10735173/), [EASL-EASD-EASO](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11299976/)

Recent strenuous exercise creates a different problem: it lowers confidence that a single mild aminotransferase elevation originated in the liver. That can justify checking creatine kinase and repeating the test after adequate recovery when it is clinically safe to do so. [Pettersson et al.](https://bpspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1365-2125.2007.03001.x), [ACG](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27995906/)

Neither situation is well served by a permanent higher “athlete ULN.” Such a limit could conceal persistent liver disease in an active person, while a lower limit for sedentary people could incorrectly imply that an enzyme value is intrinsically healthier in one lifestyle group than another. This is a clinical inference from the exercise evidence and the risk-based approach used in current liver guidelines. [ACG](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27995906/), [AASLD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10735173/), [EASL-EASD-EASO](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11299976/), [Pettersson et al.](https://bpspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1365-2125.2007.03001.x), [Tirabassi et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29558239/)

A more defensible framework, consistent with laboratory-methodology guidance, liver guidelines, and the exercise studies reviewed here, is: [Ozarda et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/), [ACG](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27995906/), [AASLD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10735173/), [EASL-EASD-EASO](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11299976/), [Pettersson et al.](https://bpspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1365-2125.2007.03001.x), [Tirabassi et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29558239/)

- **Sex- and assay-specific laboratory reference intervals**
- **A relatively low clinical threshold for attention**
- **Risk-specific follow-up rather than risk-specific definitions of normal**
- **Documentation of recent strenuous exercise as a pre-analytical factor**

The reviewed [ACG](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27995906/), [AASLD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10735173/), [EASL-EASD-EASO](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11299976/), and [APASL](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40016576/) guidance does not establish a separate ALT upper limit for athletes.

## ALT versus imaging and fibrosis assessment

The fourth claim is correct that ALT alone is inadequate, but “use imaging instead” is still too simple. [Ma et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31937252/), [Verma et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23763360/), [AASLD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10735173/), [EASL-EASD-EASO](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11299976/)

Conventional B-mode ultrasound can detect steatosis but has limited sensitivity for lesser degrees of steatosis, particularly in people with obesity. Vibration-controlled transient elastography can assess liver stiffness and, through the controlled attenuation parameter, provide information about steatosis. MRI-proton density fat fraction is more accurate for quantifying liver fat but is more expensive and less accessible. [AASLD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10735173/), [EASL-EASD-EASO](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11299976/)

More importantly, the clinically consequential feature is usually **fibrosis**, not merely the presence of liver fat. Fibrosis stage is more closely related to future liver-related outcomes than ALT or steatosis alone. Current European guidance therefore recommends that at-risk adults undergo a blood-based fibrosis assessment such as FIB-4, followed by elastography or another second-line test when the first test is indeterminate or elevated. FIB-4 is a fibrosis-risk stratification tool; it does not detect liver fat or establish a diagnosis of MASLD. These combined approaches have better diagnostic accuracy for fibrosis than standard ALT and AST testing. [AASLD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10735173/), [EASL-EASD-EASO](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11299976/)

The 2024 EASL-EASD-EASO guideline considers ALT above 33 U/L in men and 25 U/L in women elevated. It recommends case-finding for fibrosis in people with type 2 diabetes, abdominal obesity plus another metabolic risk factor, persistently abnormal liver tests, or radiological steatosis, not blanket screening of the entire population. [EASL-EASD-EASO](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11299976/)

AASLD guidance similarly cautions that aminotransferases are often normal in people with advanced steatohepatitis and should not be used alone to exclude significant disease. It advises against general-population screening while supporting fibrosis assessment in higher-risk groups, generally beginning with FIB-4 and proceeding to elastography or the enhanced liver fibrosis test when indicated. [AASLD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10735173/)

Thus, the alternative to relying on ALT is not necessarily “ultrasound everyone.” It is targeted identification of people at meaningful fibrosis risk, followed by sequential noninvasive testing. [AASLD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10735173/), [EASL-EASD-EASO](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11299976/)

## Cost-effectiveness

The economic studies reviewed here include evaluations of targeted diagnostic or screening pathways, including a model restricted to people with type 2 diabetes; they do not establish the cost-effectiveness of changing the ALT reference limit itself. [Asiyabar et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42769814/), [Choo et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36453216/)

A 2026 systematic review found only 11 eligible economic evaluations of NAFLD diagnostic or screening strategies. Although published in 2026, its literature search covered January 1990 through December 2023. The studies used different models, tests, populations, costs, and assumptions across European, Asian, and American settings. The authors concluded that affordability, accessibility, accuracy, feasibility, and local healthcare economics all need to be considered. The review did not establish that universally lowering the ALT limit is more or less cost-effective than imaging-based screening. [Asiyabar et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42769814/)

In a Singapore model restricted to people with type 2 diabetes, both vibration-controlled transient elastography and FIB-4 screening for advanced fibrosis were cost-effective relative to no screening. VCTE had an estimated incremental cost of US$24,727 per quality-adjusted life-year and FIB-4 US$36,800 per quality-adjusted life-year. The findings depended substantially on the assumed duration of screening benefit, treatment cost, and local access to elastography. [Choo et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36453216/)

This supports structured screening in selected high-risk populations. It does not demonstrate the cost-effectiveness of imaging all adults or of referring everyone with an ALT above 19 or 30 U/L. [Asiyabar et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42769814/), [Choo et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36453216/)

## Comparison of major guidelines

### American College of Gastroenterology, 2017

The ACG guideline describes a healthy ALT range of approximately **29-33 U/L for men and 19-25 U/L for women** and states that results above these values should be assessed. It also emphasizes that evaluation should consider viral, metabolic, alcohol-related, hereditary, autoimmune, medication-related, and other causes rather than assuming fatty liver. [ACG guideline](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27995906/)

### American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases, 2023

AASLD describes traditional laboratory limits as generally too high in the NAFLD setting. It notes that ALT or AST persistently or intermittently above approximately 30 U/L for 6-12 months may suggest chronic liver injury, even when the value remains below a laboratory’s stated upper limit. However, it does not recommend general-population NAFLD screening. It prioritizes fibrosis risk assessment in people with type 2 diabetes, complicated obesity, incidental steatosis, persistently abnormal enzymes, or other major risk factors. [AASLD guidance](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10735173/)

### EASL-EASD-EASO, 2024

The European guideline uses **greater than 33 U/L in men and greater than 25 U/L in women** as elevated. It recommends case-finding in cardiometabolically at-risk groups and a multistep fibrosis pathway beginning with FIB-4, followed by elastography or another second-line test. It explicitly states that combined noninvasive scores and imaging are more accurate for fibrosis detection than ALT or AST alone. [EASL-EASD-EASO guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11299976/)

### Asian Pacific Association for the Study of the Liver, 2025

The 2025 APASL guidance uses the term metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), a framework that overlaps with but is not identical to MASLD. It covers diagnosis, screening, risk assessment, and management. Like the US and European approaches, it treats ALT as one part of a broader assessment rather than a sufficient screening or exclusion test. The reviewed guidance does not define a separate exercise-adjusted upper limit. [APASL guideline](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40016576/)

The precise ALT numbers differ, but the direction of travel is consistent: clinicians should not rely uncritically on historically high laboratory limits, and fibrosis risk should not be ruled out by a normal ALT. [ACG](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27995906/), [AASLD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10735173/), [EASL-EASD-EASO](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11299976/), [APASL](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40016576/)

## What the evidence still does not answer

Several important evidence gaps remain.

**Contemporary reference distributions:** The available reference-limit studies used different populations, exclusion criteria, assays, and statistical definitions. Larger studies are needed in rigorously characterized, healthy-weight, metabolically healthy, imaging-negative adults using standardized assays. These studies should control for alcohol, medications, ethnicity, age, and recent exercise. [Prati et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12093239/), [Valenti et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34520121/), [Choi et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38750867/), [Åberg et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41255223/)

**Direct MASLD detection comparisons:** Among the studies reviewed here, few directly compare 30/19, 34/22, 42/30, and conventional laboratory limits against the same reference standard, such as MRI-PDFF, elastography, and biopsy, in a representative population. [Prati et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12093239/), [Valenti et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34520121/), [Choi et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38750867/), [Åberg et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41255223/), [Ma et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31937252/), [Verma et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23763360/)

**Athletic false-positive rates:** The exercise studies reviewed here do not provide a prospective estimate of how frequently ordinary strength training, endurance training, or competition pushes habitual athletes above the proposed lower limits. Future studies should stratify by sex, training status, exercise modality, creatine kinase response, and time since exercise. [Pettersson et al.](https://bpspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1365-2125.2007.03001.x), [Tirabassi et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29558239/)

**Patient-important outcomes:** None of the cited threshold studies is a pragmatic trial testing whether reporting lower ALT limits reduces progression to advanced fibrosis, cirrhosis, transplantation, or mortality. [Prati et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12093239/), [Valenti et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34520121/), [Choi et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38750867/), [Åberg et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41255223/)

**Downstream harms:** The threshold studies reviewed do not adequately measure referral volume, repeat-testing burden, anxiety, incidental imaging findings, or unnecessary procedures. [Prati et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12093239/), [Valenti et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34520121/), [Choi et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38750867/), [Åberg et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41255223/)

**Direct economic comparison:** The reviewed cost-effectiveness studies generally evaluate high-risk fibrosis pathways. They do not directly compare a lower laboratory ALT flag, targeted noninvasive fibrosis testing, and universal imaging within the same health system. [Asiyabar et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42769814/), [Choo et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36453216/)

## Quick reference values

- **Prati et al., 2002:** 30 U/L for men and 19 U/L for women in a selected low-risk donor population; primarily validated against HCV viremia. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12093239/)
- **Valenti et al., 2021:** 42 U/L for men and 30 U/L for women using a standardized IFCC method, derived from a low-risk subgroup of 9,195 donors. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34520121/)
- **Choi et al., 2024:** 34 U/L for men and 22 U/L for women in metabolically and histologically normal potential Asian liver donors. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38750867/)
- **Åberg et al., 2025:** 41 U/L for men and 27 U/L for women at the 95th percentile, and 57 U/L and 35 U/L at the 97.5th percentile, in the healthiest subgroup. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41255223/)
- **ACG clinical range:** approximately 29-33 U/L for men and 19-25 U/L for women. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27995906/)
- **EASL 2024 definition of elevation:** greater than 33 U/L for men and greater than 25 U/L for women. [PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11299976/)

These values are not fully interchangeable because the populations, analytical methods, percentile conventions, and intended uses differ. [Prati et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12093239/), [Valenti et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34520121/), [Choi et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38750867/), [Åberg et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41255223/), [ACG](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27995906/), [EASL-EASD-EASO](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11299976/)

## Practical interpretation

A sedentary, overweight man with an ALT of 40 U/L has not been diagnosed with fatty liver by that result. But the value should not be dismissed merely because the local laboratory reports an upper limit of 50 or 55 U/L. Persistence, metabolic risk, alcohol exposure, medications and supplements, viral hepatitis risk, and noninvasive fibrosis assessment may all be relevant. [ACG](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27995906/), [AASLD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10735173/), [EASL-EASD-EASO](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11299976/)

An active person with an ALT of 35 U/L shortly after unfamiliar or strenuous resistance exercise may have a transient muscle-related contribution. AST, creatine kinase, bilirubin, gamma-glutamyl transferase, and alkaline phosphatase can help characterize the pattern. An evidence-informed approach for a mild asymptomatic elevation may include repeating the test after adequate recovery from heavy muscle-damaging exercise. The cited experimental study did not validate a universal washout interval; it showed that aminotransferase and muscle-injury abnormalities can persist for at least seven days after the studied weightlifting session. [Pettersson et al.](https://bpspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1365-2125.2007.03001.x), [ACG](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27995906/)

A normal ALT does not reliably exclude MASLD, MASH, or advanced fibrosis. Metabolic risk and appropriate fibrosis assessment remain important even when ALT is below 30 U/L. [Ma et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31937252/), [Verma et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23763360/), [AASLD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10735173/), [EASL-EASD-EASO](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11299976/)

The repeat-after-recovery approach applies only to mild elevations when clinically appropriate. Marked elevations, jaundice, abnormal bilirubin, impaired clotting, significant symptoms, or other concerning findings should not be attributed to exercise without prompt clinical evaluation. [ACG](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27995906/)

## Funding and conflict-of-interest notes

The Prati study is indexed as having received non-US-government research support. Its PubMed record does not provide a complete account of all possible financial relationships. [Prati et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12093239/)

The Valenti paper reports multiple public, institutional, and industry-related funding sources, and several authors disclosed relationships with diagnostic or pharmaceutical companies. These disclosures do not invalidate the findings, but they are relevant to interpretation. [Valenti et al. full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8557310/)

The Choi study reported support from Korean government and academic programs and from the Gilead Sciences Research Scholar Program. The authors reported no conflicts of interest. [Choi et al. full text](https://www.cghjournal.org/article/S1542-3565%2824%2900439-7/fulltext)

The small weightlifting study was conducted by authors affiliated with AstraZeneca R&D in Lund or Mölndal and was motivated partly by interpretation of safety tests in clinical drug development. Its findings should be interpreted with that institutional context and its small, male-only sample in mind. [Pettersson et al.](https://bpspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1365-2125.2007.03001.x)

The ultramarathon investigators reported no potential conflicts of interest; the study was partly funded by an American College of Sports Medicine Clinical Sports Medicine Endowment Grant. [Tirabassi et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29558239/)

The 2026 cost-effectiveness systematic review reported no conflicts of interest. [Asiyabar et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42769814/)

## Bottom line

Many conventional ALT upper limits are too high **if they are interpreted as “nothing to investigate below this number.”** A persistent ALT of 30-40 U/L can carry meaningful information, particularly in someone with obesity, diabetes, dyslipidemia, or other metabolic risk. [Prati et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12093239/), [Valenti et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34520121/), [Choi et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38750867/), [Åberg et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41255223/), [ACG](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27995906/), [AASLD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10735173/)

But the evidence does not justify treating 30 U/L for men and 19 U/L for women as universal diagnostic cutoffs. Assay differences, sex, population selection, statistical convention, metabolic health, and recent strenuous exercise all affect interpretation. [Prati et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12093239/), [Valenti et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34520121/), [Choi et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38750867/), [Åberg et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41255223/), [Pettersson et al.](https://bpspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1365-2125.2007.03001.x), [Tirabassi et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29558239/)

The strongest overall strategy is not a choice between a high ALT limit and universal imaging. It is a combination of the following approaches. [Ozarda et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/), [ACG](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27995906/), [AASLD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10735173/), [EASL-EASD-EASO](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11299976/), [Pettersson et al.](https://bpspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1365-2125.2007.03001.x), [Tirabassi et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29558239/)

- Standardized, sex-specific laboratory limits. [Ozarda et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/), [Valenti et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34520121/)
- A relatively low threshold for clinical attention. [ACG](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27995906/), [AASLD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10735173/), [EASL-EASD-EASO](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11299976/)
- Confirmation and contextual interpretation of mild elevations. [ACG](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27995906/), [AASLD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10735173/)
- Recognition of exercise as a possible transient influence. [Pettersson et al.](https://bpspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1365-2125.2007.03001.x), [Tirabassi et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29558239/)
- Risk-based fibrosis assessment using FIB-4 and, when indicated, elastography or another second-line test. [AASLD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10735173/), [EASL-EASD-EASO](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11299976/)
- Recognition that normal ALT alone does not rule out clinically significant MASLD. [Ma et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31937252/), [Verma et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23763360/), [AASLD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10735173/), [EASL-EASD-EASO](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11299976/)
