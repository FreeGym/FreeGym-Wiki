# Normal vs Optimal: Should You Aim Higher?

The phrase “normal versus optimal” combines two different concepts. A **laboratory reference interval** describes results seen in a selected reference population, usually the central 95 percent. A **clinical decision limit** is a threshold linked to diagnosis, risk, or a treatment decision. Neither is automatically an “optimal target.” ([Ozarda et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/); [Timbrell, 2024](https://www.frontierspartnerships.org/journals/british-journal-of-biomedical-science/articles/10.3389/bjbs.2024.12339/full))

A narrower target can be legitimate, but it is most strongly justified when evidence shows that acting on it improves patient-important outcomes sufficiently to justify treatment burden, adverse effects, and cost. Across the examples reviewed here, the evidence is marker-specific: outcome-linked targets exist for some markers and populations, while the cited evidence is weaker or does not establish a treatment benefit for others. ([Fleming and Powers, 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22711298/); [UKPDS 33, 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9742976/); [ACCORD Study Group, 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18539917/); [Houston et al., 2018](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/8/4/e019240); [Feller et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6233842/); [Shah et al., 2024](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/109/8/1961/7686350); [Timbrell, 2024](https://www.frontierspartnerships.org/journals/british-journal-of-biomedical-science/articles/10.3389/bjbs.2024.12339/full))

## What a reference interval means - and what it does not

Reference intervals are generally constructed to include the middle 95 percent of results in an apparently healthy reference population. This means that approximately 5 percent of healthy reference participants will fall outside the interval by definition. The interval may also vary with age, sex, laboratory method, population selection, and other preanalytical or biological factors. ([Ozarda, 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4783089/); [Ozarda et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/))

Being inside the interval therefore does not prove that a person has no disease, and being outside it does not by itself establish disease. Reference intervals are descriptive. Clinical decision limits, by contrast, are thresholds based on diagnostic performance, disease risk, treatment evidence, or expert consensus. HbA1c, fasting glucose, lipids, and tumor markers are commonly interpreted using such decision limits rather than simply asking whether they fall within the central population distribution. ([Ozarda et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/))

“Optimal” should not mean “the middle of the lab range” or “the narrower range favored by a particular clinic.” When it is used to recommend an intervention, the proposed range should be evaluated as a treatment target rather than assumed from its position within a reference interval. ([Ozarda et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/); [Fleming and Powers, 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22711298/))

## What an “optimal range” would need to prove

A defensible treatment target requires more than an observational association between a biomarker and health. It requires evidence that:

- the marker is measured reliably and interpreted in the correct population;

- risk changes meaningfully across the proposed threshold;

- an intervention can move the marker;

- a treatment strategy aimed at moving it produces better symptoms, function, disease outcomes, or survival, not merely a better laboratory result;

- and the benefit outweighs adverse effects, treatment burden, false-positive follow-up, and cost. ([Fleming and Powers, 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22711298/))


Biomarkers frequently predict outcomes without functioning as valid surrogate endpoints. An intervention may improve a biomarker while having no benefit, or even causing net harm, because it affects other biological pathways not captured by the marker. Surrogate validity is therefore specific to the marker, intervention, disease, population, and outcome being studied. ([Fleming and Powers, 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22711298/))

A central evidentiary weakness arises when a proposed functional “optimal range” proceeds from **association -> proposed threshold -> treatment**, without showing **treatment to that threshold -> better patient outcomes**. ([Fleming and Powers, 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22711298/))

## Where tighter targets have real outcome evidence

### HbA1c in diabetes

HbA1c is an example of a biomarker for which more intensive control can improve outcomes, but also an example showing that a more aggressive treatment strategy is not always better. ([UKPDS 33, 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9742976/); [ACCORD Study Group, 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18539917/))

In UKPDS, 3,867 people with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes were randomized to an intensive or conventional glucose-control strategy. Over approximately 10 years, median HbA1c was 7.0 percent with intensive treatment and 7.9 percent with conventional treatment. Intensive treatment reduced the aggregate relative risk of diabetes-related endpoints by 12 percent, driven largely by a 25 percent relative reduction in microvascular endpoints. It did not significantly reduce mortality during the randomized phase and caused more hypoglycemia and weight gain. ([UKPDS 33, 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9742976/))

ACCORD tested a still more aggressive strategy in 10,251 people with longer-standing type 2 diabetes and high cardiovascular risk, targeting HbA1c below 6.0 percent rather than 7.0 to 7.9 percent. The intensive strategy was stopped early because mortality was higher, despite achieving a lower HbA1c. This trial tested the overall treatment strategy, not the isolated causal effect of the achieved HbA1c value. ([ACCORD Study Group, 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18539917/))

Thus, HbA1c has genuine outcome-linked treatment targets in diabetes, but the trials reject the simple optimization rule that an aggressive strategy to drive HbA1c as low as possible is always healthier. The contrast between the study populations and treatment strategies in UKPDS and ACCORD shows that the balance of benefit and harm is population- and treatment-specific. ([UKPDS 33, 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9742976/); [ACCORD Study Group, 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18539917/))

The trials cited above enrolled people with type 2 diabetes and therefore do not establish that healthy people benefit from medication or supplements intended merely to move a normal HbA1c toward the bottom of the reference interval. ([UKPDS 33, 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9742976/); [ACCORD Study Group, 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18539917/))

### Blood pressure as a model of a properly tested tighter target

Although blood pressure is not a laboratory analyte, SPRINT illustrates what convincing “optimization” evidence looks like. It randomized 9,361 higher-cardiovascular-risk adults without diabetes or prior stroke to systolic targets below 120 or 140 mm Hg. The intensive target reduced major cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality, while increasing adverse events including hypotension, syncope, electrolyte abnormalities, and acute kidney injury. Its numerical target should be interpreted within SPRINT's standardized measurement and treatment protocol. ([SPRINT Research Group, 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26551272/))

A subsequent economic analysis incorporated the observed benefits, adverse events, medication use, and monitoring costs and found that the intensive strategy was likely to be cost-effective in the SPRINT-eligible population. ([Bress et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28834469/))

This is different from deriving an “optimal” number from the healthiest-looking portion of an observational curve. SPRINT directly compared two predefined targets and measured major clinical outcomes and harms; the subsequent analysis evaluated economic value in the population studied. ([SPRINT Research Group, 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26551272/); [Bress et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28834469/))

## Ferritin 35: context-dependent, not automatically “fine” or “depleted”

Ferritin is one of the stronger examples for the argument that a result can be inside a laboratory interval yet still be clinically relevant. However, the cited treatment evidence concerns particular iron-deficient, nonanemic populations with fatigue, not a universal optimal ferritin range. ([Houston et al., 2018](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/8/4/e019240); [Vaucher et al., 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3414597/); [Krayenbuehl et al., 2011](https://ashpublications.org/blood/article/118/12/3222/28818/Intravenous-iron-for-the-treatment-of-fatigue-in))

Ferritin is also an acute-phase reactant. WHO guidance recommends accounting for inflammation when ferritin is used to assess iron status and notes that ferritin is commonly assessed alongside hemoglobin and additional iron indices. ([World Health Organization, 2020](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240000124))

A systematic review identified 18 randomized trials involving 1,170 iron-deficient but nonanemic adults overall. In the four trials contributing to the pooled fatigue analysis, which included 714 participants, iron supplementation produced a modest reduction in self-reported fatigue. Across the eligible evidence, it did not produce a significant overall improvement in objective measures of physical capacity such as maximal oxygen consumption or timed exercise performance. ([Houston et al., 2018](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/8/4/e019240))

In one trial, 198 menstruating women with fatigue, hemoglobin above 12 g/dL, and ferritin below 50 μg/L received oral iron or placebo for 12 weeks. Mean fatigue scores declined by 47.7 percent with iron and 28.8 percent with placebo, a statistically significant between-group difference. Quality of life, depression, and anxiety did not significantly differ. A person with ferritin of 35 would have met the ferritin criterion, but the trial did not establish 50 as an optimal treatment endpoint or show that everyone below 50 benefited. ([Vaucher et al., 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3414597/))

**Funding and conflict-of-interest context:** the Vaucher trial was sponsored by Pierre Fabre Médicament, and the sponsor's clinical-pharmacy department generated the allocation sequence and participated in study operations. This does not by itself invalidate the result, but independent replication and close appraisal of the methods are important before using it to establish a universal threshold. ([Vaucher et al., 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3414597/))

A placebo-controlled trial of intravenous iron enrolled 90 fatigued, nonanemic premenopausal women with ferritin at or below 50 μg/L. The overall primary fatigue comparison narrowly missed statistical significance. A larger benefit appeared in the subgroup with ferritin at or below 15 μg/L, while no significant fatigue reduction was seen above that level in the subgroup analysis. Drug-related adverse effects occurred in 21 percent of iron-treated participants and 7 percent of controls. ([Krayenbuehl et al., 2011](https://ashpublications.org/blood/article/118/12/3222/28818/Intravenous-iron-for-the-treatment-of-fatigue-in))

**Funding and conflict-of-interest context:** this intravenous trial was funded by Vifor Pharma, which was involved in trial design and data collection; one investigator was a company consultant. This disclosure is relevant to appraisal but does not, by itself, determine whether the findings are valid. ([Krayenbuehl et al., 2011](https://ashpublications.org/blood/article/118/12/3222/28818/Intravenous-iron-for-the-treatment-of-fatigue-in))

### Practical interpretation of ferritin 35

Ferritin of 35 is not automatically proof of clinically important iron depletion, but neither can it be dismissed solely because it falls inside a broad laboratory interval. Ferritin interpretation depends on the clinical context and on factors such as inflammation and other measures of iron status. ([World Health Organization, 2020](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240000124); [Houston et al., 2018](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/8/4/e019240))

In a menstruating person with otherwise unexplained fatigue and compatible iron studies, the value falls within populations in which oral iron produced a modest symptom benefit. The cited trials do not establish that supplementation in an asymptomatic person to reach a proposed “optimal” ferritin of 50, 70, or 100 improves long-term health. They measured short-term fatigue and related outcomes, not prevention of cardiovascular disease, cognitive decline, cancer, or mortality. ([Houston et al., 2018](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/8/4/e019240); [Vaucher et al., 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3414597/); [Krayenbuehl et al., 2011](https://ashpublications.org/blood/article/118/12/3222/28818/Intravenous-iron-for-the-treatment-of-fatigue-in))

The evidence therefore supports **contextual clinical evaluation**, not a universal narrower target. ([World Health Organization, 2020](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240000124); [Houston et al., 2018](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/8/4/e019240); [Vaucher et al., 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3414597/); [Krayenbuehl et al., 2011](https://ashpublications.org/blood/article/118/12/3222/28818/Intravenous-iron-for-the-treatment-of-fatigue-in))

## TSH 3.8: lowering the number has not been shown to improve outcomes

A TSH of 3.8 mIU/L is within the 0.50 to 4.49 mIU/L euthyroid comparison range used in the cited individual-participant analysis of 55,287 people. Coronary heart disease event risk was not significantly higher at TSH 4.5 to 6.9 or 7.0 to 9.9 mIU/L and was significantly higher at 10 to 19.9 mIU/L. Coronary heart disease mortality was not significantly higher at 4.5 to 6.9 mIU/L, but was significantly higher at 7.0 to 9.9 and 10 to 19.9 mIU/L. Total mortality was not significantly increased. This was observational evidence, so it establishes risk associations rather than treatment benefit. ([Rodondi et al., 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3923470/))

More importantly, randomized treatment evidence has generally not shown that lowering mildly elevated TSH improves how adults with subclinical hypothyroidism feel. A meta-analysis of 21 randomized trials involving 2,192 adults found that thyroid hormone lowered TSH into the reference range. In the trials reporting the relevant outcomes, it did not improve general quality of life or thyroid-related symptoms overall. These findings apply to populations resembling those enrolled, predominantly people with mild subclinical hypothyroidism, and should not be generalized to overt thyroid disease or pregnancy. ([Feller et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6233842/))

Randomized evidence in older adults is also consistent with no demonstrated cardiovascular benefit. A prespecified pooled analysis of the TRUST and IEMO80+ trials included 842 adults aged 65 or older with subclinical hypothyroidism. Over a median follow-up of 17 months, levothyroxine lowered TSH but did not significantly change cardiovascular events, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, or all-cause mortality. The confidence intervals were compatible with modest benefit or harm, so the finding is no demonstrated benefit rather than proof of equivalence. ([Zijlstra et al., 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/endocrinology/articles/10.3389/fendo.2021.674841/full))

For a typical nonpregnant adult, the cited randomized treatment evidence does not establish a benefit from medication solely to move TSH from 3.8 to a functional target such as 1 or 2. The meta-analysis combined trials that used varying definitions of subclinical hypothyroidism, and the pooled cardiovascular analysis enrolled older adults with TSH from 4.6 to 19.9 mIU/L; neither provides a randomized comparison specifically at a baseline TSH of 3.8 against a target of 1 or 2. The conclusion is an absence of demonstrated benefit in the cited evidence, not proof that no individual could benefit. Pregnancy and overt thyroid disease are distinct clinical situations and cannot be resolved by extrapolating these trials. ([Feller et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6233842/); [Zijlstra et al., 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/endocrinology/articles/10.3389/fendo.2021.674841/full))

## Vitamin D 25 ng/mL: no validated higher prevention target

Vitamin D is a prominent example of observational associations generating enthusiasm that was not consistently confirmed in randomized trials. ([Shah et al., 2024](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/109/8/1961/7686350); [Manson et al., 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415629/))

A 2024 systematic review supporting the Endocrine Society guideline included 151 studies. In generally healthy adults aged 19 to 74, vitamin D supplementation had no significant effect on the selected disease-prevention outcomes overall. The review found no randomized trials evaluating the benefits and harms of screening people according to their serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D concentration. It also found that high-dose intermittent supplementation may increase falls relative to lower daily dosing. ([Shah et al., 2024](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/109/8/1961/7686350))

In VITAL, 25,871 adults were randomized to vitamin D3 2,000 IU daily or placebo and followed for a median of 5.3 years. Supplementation did not significantly reduce invasive cancer or major cardiovascular events. Participants were not selected for severe deficiency, so this does not answer every clinical deficiency question, but it directly undermines the claim that broadly raising vitamin D prevents these major diseases in generally healthy adults. ([Manson et al., 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415629/))

The 2024 Endocrine Society guideline consequently advised against routine vitamin D testing and against empiric supplementation above standard dietary reference intakes for disease prevention in healthy adults younger than 75. Crucially, the evidence review could not identify an outcome-specific serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D concentration that should be targeted for general disease prevention. This prevention guidance does not replace testing or treatment for an established clinical indication. ([Demay et al., 2024](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/vitamin-d-for-prevention-of-disease))

A vitamin D level of 25 ng/mL therefore cannot be labeled universally “optimal” or universally “deficient” without specifying the clinical purpose. In generally healthy adults without a separate indication for treatment, the cited evidence does not establish that pushing 25 ng/mL into a functional range such as 40 to 60 ng/mL provides broad protection against cancer, cardiovascular disease, or other major outcomes. ([Shah et al., 2024](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/109/8/1961/7686350); [Demay et al., 2024](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/vitamin-d-for-prevention-of-disease); [Manson et al., 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415629/))

## Many routine chemistry values do not have trial-defined optimal subranges

For most measurements on routine chemistry panels, reference intervals help identify results that may warrant interpretation or further evaluation. They are descriptive intervals, not instructions to move every value toward the midpoint. ([Ozarda et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/); [Timbrell, 2024](https://www.frontierspartnerships.org/journals/british-journal-of-biomedical-science/articles/10.3389/bjbs.2024.12339/full))

The cited reference-interval reviews do not establish that deliberately moving values such as sodium, albumin, liver enzymes, creatinine, or routine blood-cell indices from one normal sub-band into a narrower practitioner-defined band improves outcomes in healthy adults. For these markers, the reviews emphasize interpretation in relation to biological variation, measurement conditions, population characteristics, and relevant clinical decision limits rather than treating distance from a proposed “optimal” midpoint as a treatment target. ([Ozarda et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/); [Timbrell, 2024](https://www.frontierspartnerships.org/journals/british-journal-of-biomedical-science/articles/10.3389/bjbs.2024.12339/full))

This is a limitation of the cited evidence, not proof that no relevant study exists or that every proposed subrange is biologically meaningless. It means that these sources do not demonstrate a patient-important benefit from intervening to reach such subranges. ([Ozarda et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/); [Timbrell, 2024](https://www.frontierspartnerships.org/journals/british-journal-of-biomedical-science/articles/10.3389/bjbs.2024.12339/full))

## Functional medicine versus conventional care

The comparative evidence summarized here consists of an observational cohort, a small randomized pilot trial, and a broader review of integrative healthcare. ([Beidelschies et al., 2019](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2753520); [Eldib et al., 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13054257/); [Leach et al., 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30721565/))

A 2019 Cleveland Clinic retrospective cohort compared 1,595 functional-medicine patients with 5,657 primary-care patients. Functional-medicine patients had greater improvement in a patient-reported physical-health measure at six months. In the overall analysis, the between-group difference was not significant at 12 months; a much smaller subset with measurements at both time points retained a significant association. Because patients selected their care setting, follow-up was incomplete, visits and attention differed, and residual confounding was likely, the study cannot establish that the functional-medicine intervention caused the improvement. ([Beidelschies et al., 2019](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2753520))

**Conflict-of-interest context:** one author reported serving as a paid educational consultant and instructor for the Institute for Functional Medicine; other investigators disclosed outside industry relationships. These disclosures do not invalidate the study, but they are relevant when interpreting an observational comparison from the institution delivering the intervention. ([Beidelschies et al., 2019](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2753520))

In 2026, investigators published an open-label randomized pilot trial comparing functional medicine plus conventional endocrinology care with conventional endocrinology care alone in insulin-treated type 2 diabetes. Seventy-one participants were randomized, and 47 had 12-month outcome data. At 12 months, 15.8 percent in the functional-medicine group and 11.1 percent in usual care met the primary outcome of insulin discontinuation without worsened HbA1c, a nonsignificant difference. HbA1c, weight, BMI, insulin dose, hypoglycemia, medication burden, and quality-of-life measures also did not significantly differ between groups. ([Eldib et al., 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13054257/))

The trial was under-enrolled, used a per-protocol analysis that excluded participants without follow-up measurements, had substantial attrition, was unblinded, and tested a heterogeneous individualized intervention. It therefore cannot exclude a smaller benefit or determine whether an individual component might work. The trial did not demonstrate an advantage from adding the tested functional-medicine package, but its size and limitations make it inconclusive rather than an equivalence test. ([Eldib et al., 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13054257/))

The trial was funded by the Cleveland Clinic Foundation, and the authors declared no known competing financial interests. ([Eldib et al., 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13054257/))

A broader systematic review of integrative healthcare for chronic disease found only eight controlled studies. Every study had at least three design features rated as carrying high or uncertain risk of bias, and physiological, psychological, functional, and quality-of-life findings were inconsistent. Only one trial suggested possible cost-effectiveness, which was insufficient for firm conclusions. Because integrative healthcare is broader than functional medicine, this evidence is indirect. ([Leach et al., 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30721565/))

The cited literature therefore does not establish long-term superiority of functional medicine over conventional care. Equally, one small null trial does not prove that every component of the tested package is ineffective, because the trial was under-enrolled and evaluated a heterogeneous intervention. Individual components require their own evidence. ([Beidelschies et al., 2019](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2753520); [Eldib et al., 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13054257/); [Leach et al., 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30721565/))

## Cost-effectiveness of biomarker optimization

Cost-effectiveness cannot be inferred merely from detecting risk earlier. It requires evidence that testing changes management, that management improves health, and that the resulting health gain is evaluated against the combined cost of testing, follow-up, treatment, adverse effects, and diagnostic cascades. ([Bress et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28834469/); [Leach et al., 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30721565/))

SPRINT provides a positive marker-specific example: a randomized target comparison demonstrated fewer cardiovascular events and deaths, and an economic model then found that the additional treatment and monitoring were likely to represent reasonable value in eligible patients. ([SPRINT Research Group, 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26551272/); [Bress et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28834469/))

The evidence cited here does not establish a comparable economic case for generalized “optimization panels.” The systematic review of integrative care found only a single suggestive economic result in a body of studies with substantial risk of bias. A separate observational functional-medicine cost study compared shared appointments with individual appointments **within functional medicine**; it did not compare functional medicine with conventional care and therefore cannot establish the cost-effectiveness of the overall model. ([Leach et al., 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30721565/); [Beidelschies et al., 2021](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/11/4/e048294))

## Medicalization and the risk of chasing numbers

Repeated broad testing increases the probability of flagged findings. Under an assumption of 20 statistically independent tests using central 95-percent reference intervals, the mathematical probability of at least one result falling outside its interval is approximately 64 percent. The 95-percent convention is described in the reference-interval literature, and the probability follows from the calculation below. ([Ozarda, 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4783089/); [Ozarda et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/))

`1 - 0.95^20 ≈ 0.642`

The calculation assumes independence and is therefore an illustration rather than an estimate for a particular laboratory panel. Narrower intervals would, by definition, classify a larger share of the same reference population as outside the proposed range. ([Ozarda, 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4783089/); [Timbrell, 2024](https://www.frontierspartnerships.org/journals/british-journal-of-biomedical-science/articles/10.3389/bjbs.2024.12339/full))

Evidence from general health checks provides indirect support for this concern. A review found that checkups increased recognition and treatment of chronic disease, risk-factor control, preventive-service use, and some patient-reported outcomes, but were not generally associated with reductions in mortality or cardiovascular events. General health checks are not equivalent to functional-medicine optimization programs, so this evidence cannot determine their net effects; it shows that detecting and treating more abnormalities does not automatically produce better major outcomes. ([Liss et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34100866/))

The studies summarized here do not establish whether optimization clinics cause sustained anxiety. Better documented concerns are the marker-specific burdens visible in trials: iron-related adverse effects, hypoglycemia from aggressive glucose lowering, adverse events from intensive blood-pressure treatment, and possible harm from high intermittent vitamin D dosing. ([Beidelschies et al., 2019](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2753520); [Eldib et al., 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13054257/); [Krayenbuehl et al., 2011](https://ashpublications.org/blood/article/118/12/3222/28818/Intravenous-iron-for-the-treatment-of-fatigue-in); [ACCORD Study Group, 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18539917/); [SPRINT Research Group, 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26551272/); [Shah et al., 2024](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/109/8/1961/7686350))

## Quick reference

- **Reference interval:** Describes the distribution in a selected reference population; it is not necessarily a treatment target. ([Ozarda et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/))

- **Ferritin 35 μg/L:** May be relevant in a symptomatic, iron-deficient, nonanemic menstruating person, but does not establish a universal need to supplement or a proven target above 50. ([Houston et al., 2018](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/8/4/e019240))

- **TSH 3.8 mIU/L:** The cited randomized evidence does not provide a dedicated comparison of treatment beginning at TSH 3.8 against a narrower functional target such as 1 or 2, or establish a patient-important benefit from doing so in a typical nonpregnant adult. ([Feller et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6233842/); [Zijlstra et al., 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/endocrinology/articles/10.3389/fendo.2021.674841/full))

- **Vitamin D 25 ng/mL:** The cited randomized evidence does not establish that raising it to 40-60 ng/mL improves major outcomes in generally healthy adults without another treatment indication. ([Shah et al., 2024](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/109/8/1961/7686350); [Demay et al., 2024](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/vitamin-d-for-prevention-of-disease))

- **HbA1c:** A genuine outcome-linked treatment marker in diabetes, but intensive-control trials show that the best target is population- and treatment-specific rather than “as low as possible.” ([UKPDS 33, 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9742976/); [ACCORD Study Group, 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18539917/))

- **Many routine chemistry values:** The cited reference-interval literature does not establish a patient-important benefit from treating every in-range result toward the middle or toward a commercially defined optimal subrange. ([Ozarda et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/); [Timbrell, 2024](https://www.frontierspartnerships.org/journals/british-journal-of-biomedical-science/articles/10.3389/bjbs.2024.12339/full))


## Which competing claim is best supported?

1. **“Optimal ranges prevent disease before it starts.”**

    Conditionally true only in the broader sense that a specific target has been tested prospectively and improves outcomes in a defined population. HbA1c targets in diabetes can reduce complications, and systolic blood-pressure targets in selected high-risk adults can reduce future cardiovascular events. These trials do not validate generalized biomarker optimization in healthy people. ([UKPDS 33, 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9742976/); [SPRINT Research Group, 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26551272/))

2. **“Many marketed functional optimal ranges lack rigorous outcome data.”**

    Supported as a methodological concern, but not quantified by the evidence assembled here. Observational association alone does not validate a treatment target, and the ferritin evidence shows how treatment effects can be limited to particular populations and outcomes. The cited evidence does not establish what proportion of marketed ranges lack rigorous support. ([Fleming and Powers, 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22711298/); [Houston et al., 2018](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/8/4/e019240))

3. **“The answer is marker-specific.”**

    This is the best-supported position in the evidence reviewed here. The cited studies differ substantially in population, outcome, effect size, and treatment risk across HbA1c, ferritin, TSH, vitamin D, and routine chemistry values. ([UKPDS 33, 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9742976/); [ACCORD Study Group, 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18539917/); [Houston et al., 2018](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/8/4/e019240); [Feller et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6233842/); [Shah et al., 2024](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/109/8/1961/7686350); [Timbrell, 2024](https://www.frontierspartnerships.org/journals/british-journal-of-biomedical-science/articles/10.3389/bjbs.2024.12339/full))

4. **“Optimization medicalizes healthy people.”**

    This is a credible concern, supported indirectly by screening research and by the mathematical increase in flagged results as more tests or narrower intervals are used. The studies cited here do not establish whether functional-medicine care causes sustained anxiety or quantify long-term diagnostic cascades, adverse events, expenditure, or quality-adjusted life-years. ([Liss et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34100866/); [Beidelschies et al., 2019](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2753520); [Eldib et al., 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13054257/); [Timbrell, 2024](https://www.frontierspartnerships.org/journals/british-journal-of-biomedical-science/articles/10.3389/bjbs.2024.12339/full))


## Practical interpretation

Before acting on an “in-range but suboptimal” result, ask:

- Is the quoted boundary a population reference interval, a diagnostic decision limit, or a randomized treatment target?

- Did the supporting studies enroll people with the same symptoms, age, sex, disease status, and baseline value?

- Did treatment improve something patients could feel or experience, or only change the laboratory number?

- Was the proposed endpoint itself randomized, or was it selected after observing an association?

- What adverse effects, repeat testing, cost, and stopping rule accompany the intervention?


The defensible goal is not to accept every in-range result uncritically, nor to optimize every number. It is to intervene when a marker-specific body of evidence shows that doing so is more likely to improve health than to create treatment burden and harm. ([Fleming and Powers, 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22711298/); [UKPDS 33, 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9742976/); [ACCORD Study Group, 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18539917/); [SPRINT Research Group, 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26551272/))
