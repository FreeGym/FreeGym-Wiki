# What Does “Normal Range” Actually Mean?

The “normal range” printed beside a blood test is more accurately called a **reference interval**. It describes how results are distributed in a defined reference population when measured under specified conditions with a particular laboratory method. ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/ "https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/"))

For a conventional two-sided reference interval, the lower limit is usually the 2.5th percentile and the upper limit is the 97.5th percentile. In other words, it covers the central 95 percent of the reference population - not “the 95th percentile.” By construction, approximately 5 percent of individuals who meet the study’s reference-subject criteria will fall outside the interval. This does not mean those individuals are diseased, nor does falling inside the interval prove health. ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/ "https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/"))

A reference interval is therefore not a guarantee of health, a diagnosis, or an optimal target. But the opposite claim - that reference ranges are normally calculated from everyone who happened to be tested, including large numbers of sick people - is also inaccurate. The standard direct method uses selected reference individuals. Routine patient data are used in some indirect methods, but statistical procedures are applied to estimate the non-pathological component of the distribution rather than simply taking the middle 95 percent of all results. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29672266/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29672266/"))

## What a reference interval is and is not

A reference interval is a **population comparator**. It answers a question resembling: ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/ "https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/"))

> Where does this result sit relative to results obtained from a specified reference group using this laboratory method?

It does not directly answer: ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/"))

> Does this person have a disease, and would treating the result improve their health?

That second question may require a **clinical decision limit**: a threshold selected because it is associated with disease probability, future risk, or evidence that intervention is beneficial. Glucose, HbA1c, lipids, and some tumor markers are examples in which clinical interpretation may depend more on decision limits than on the central distribution of an apparently healthy population. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/"))

This distinction explains how someone can have disease while every result remains inside its reference interval. It also explains why an isolated result outside the interval does not establish disease. The clinical meaning depends on the test, the degree of abnormality, pretest probability, specimen conditions, related results, previous measurements, and the broader clinical context. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/"); [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/ "https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36537071/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36537071/"))

## How reference intervals are actually established

### The direct method

Under the direct approach, investigators define the intended reference population, specify inclusion and exclusion criteria, recruit reference individuals, collect specimens under controlled conditions, and estimate the relevant percentiles. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29672266/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29672266/"); [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/ "https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/"))

Depending on the analyte, selection criteria may address age, sex, pregnancy, fasting, medication use, alcohol intake, exercise, obesity, known disease, or other factors capable of changing the measurement. The interval is also tied to the analytical method, because different assays can produce systematically different results from the same specimen. ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/ "https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/"))

A laboratory does not necessarily recruit its own reference sample for every analyte. It may adopt an interval from an assay manufacturer, published study, professional consensus document, or another laboratory and then verify that the interval is suitable for its own method and population. In practice, the rigor of that verification varies. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17516737/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17516737/"))

### The indirect method

Indirect methods begin with results already stored in laboratory databases. These data contain people with and without disease, so statistical methods are used to estimate the distribution representing the non-pathological majority. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29672266/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29672266/"))

This can make it practical to derive age- and sex-specific intervals from very large datasets. It also creates vulnerabilities: the estimate may be biased when pathological results are common, when diseased and non-diseased distributions overlap extensively, or when the assumptions of the statistical model do not fit the analyte. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29672266/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29672266/"))

A 2022 Labcorp-authored study, for example, used approximately 72 million routine patient results to develop nationwide intervals for 266 analytes and roughly 2,700 age categories. The study used a modified indirect statistical method; it did not simply define the middle 95 percent of all submitted results as healthy. Because the work was written by Labcorp-affiliated authors and assessed the company’s own approach, it is useful as an operational example but should not be treated as independent validation. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34626611/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34626611/"))

## Where the unhealthy-population criticism is right

Calling a group “apparently healthy” does not ensure that every participant is metabolically healthy or free of early disease. The resulting interval can change according to how strictly health is defined. ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/ "https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38750867/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38750867/"))

A 2007 U.S. College of American Pathologists study involving 163 laboratories found considerable variation in where common reference intervals came from. Only about half reported internal healthy-adult validation for the analytes examined, while many relied on manufacturer or other external sources. Some intervals differed enough that the same numerical result could be below one laboratory’s limit and above another’s. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17516737/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17516737/"))

Alanine aminotransferase, or ALT, illustrates why reference-population selection matters. In a 2024 study of Korean living-liver-donor candidates, investigators identified 1,553 people without hepatic steatosis or metabolic abnormalities from 5,455 screened candidates. The estimated upper limits were 34 U/L for men and 22 U/L for women. People above those limits had substantially higher frequencies of steatosis and metabolic syndrome. These values cannot automatically be generalized to every ethnicity, setting, or assay, but the study shows that strict metabolic and histological screening can materially change the resulting limits. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38750867/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38750867/"))

The strongest version of the criticism is therefore not that laboratories universally use an unfiltered sick population. It is that the definition of “healthy,” the exclusions applied, and the statistical method can all change where the limits are placed. ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/ "https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29672266/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29672266/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17516737/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17516737/"))

## Why reference ranges differ across laboratories

Reference intervals can differ because of:

- assay calibration and standardization ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37478022/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37478022/"));

- instruments, reagents, and analytical methods ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28647526/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28647526/"));

- specimen collection and handling ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/ "https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29672266/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29672266/"));

- the population used to establish the interval ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/ "https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38750867/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38750867/"));

- age, sex, or other partitioning ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26044506/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26044506/"));

- the statistical method used to estimate the limits ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29672266/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29672266/")).


In a Canadian national study, 37 laboratories supplied the reference intervals they used for seven analytes, while 40 laboratories measured six analytes in a common specimen. Reference intervals for ALT, alkaline phosphatase, and creatinine were particularly variable. For most analytes, variation in the reported intervals was greater than variation in the actual measurements, including among laboratories using similar instrumentation. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28647526/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28647526/"))

Ferritin provides a striking example of how this changes classification. Across 52 Dutch laboratories and more than one million ferritin measurements, the proportion of premenopausal women classified below the laboratory’s lower limit ranged from 11 to 53 percent. It ranged from 3 to 37 percent among postmenopausal women and from 2 to 19 percent among men. Those classification differences reflected both platform-dependent measurement differences and wide variation in laboratory reference intervals, including interval variation among laboratories using the same analytical platform. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39392623/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39392623/"))

A laboratory flag is therefore partly a statement about the chosen interval - not only about the patient’s biology. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28647526/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28647526/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39392623/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39392623/"))

## Do ranges automatically shift as populations become less healthy?

They can shift under some circumstances, but the reviewed evidence does not show that this is an automatic process. ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/ "https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29672266/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29672266/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38205847/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38205847/"))

An interval could move if a laboratory re-estimated it from routine patient data and the inferred non-pathological distribution changed. It could also move if a newer reference study used different eligibility criteria, or if a laboratory changed methods or adopted a different transferred or manufacturer-supplied interval. A downward shift in a population average would not necessarily “widen” the interval: the interval could shift, narrow, widen, change only at one limit, or remain fixed. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29672266/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29672266/"); [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/ "https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38205847/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38205847/"))

Longitudinal thyroid data provide a useful counterexample to the idea of inevitable drift. Investigators examined reference information from 13 Dutch laboratories between 2008 and 2022. Indirectly estimated TSH intervals were generally stable across manufacturers. Free T4 intervals were also stable on three analytical platforms, although one platform showed a modest change in its upper limit. The study also found that some manufacturer-supplied limits did not align well with the laboratory data. ([Amsterdam UMC](https://pure.amsterdamumc.nl/en/publications/should-we-depend-on-reference-intervals-from-manufacturer-package/ "https://pure.amsterdamumc.nl/en/publications/should-we-depend-on-reference-intervals-from-manufacturer-package/"))

The proposition that worsening population health systematically causes all laboratory intervals to accommodate disease is therefore **plausible under specific derivation methods but not established as a general rule**. The studies reviewed here do not provide direct longitudinal evidence that population-level metabolic deterioration automatically produces reference-interval drift. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38205847/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38205847/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29672266/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29672266/"))

## Age-, sex-, and population-specific intervals

Age and sex can have large effects on laboratory values. In the Canadian Health Measures Survey, approximately 12,000 participants aged 3 to 79 were used to establish intervals for 24 biochemical markers. Most analytes required some form of age or sex partitioning. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26044506/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26044506/"))

More specific intervals can reduce misclassification, but they do not solve every problem. The underlying assay must still be sufficiently standardized, specimen conditions must be controlled, and each subgroup needs enough suitable observations to estimate its limits reliably. ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/ "https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26044506/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26044506/"))

A Canadian harmonization project using data from nine laboratories and five major manufacturers found that common intervals could be supported for several analytes. Harmonization was more difficult for others and was not considered appropriate for free T4 because method-dependent differences remained substantial. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37478022/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37478022/"))

Age-, sex-, and method-specific intervals are therefore useful when the analyte genuinely varies across those dimensions. They are not a substitute for clinical decision thresholds or for validation against meaningful health outcomes. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37478022/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37478022/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/"))

## How changing the interval changes biochemical classification

Changing a reference limit changes how many results are classified as outside the interval. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39283820/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39283820/"))

A 2024 study analyzed approximately 7.6 million TSH test requests and 2.2 million free-T4 test requests from 13 Dutch laboratories. TSH upper limits increased at older ages. In the Roche dataset, applying age-specific limits reduced the proportion of requests meeting the study’s biochemical definition of subclinical hypothyroidism among women aged 50 to 60 from 13.1 to 8.6 percent. Among women aged 90 to 100, it fell from 22.7 to 8.1 percent. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39283820/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39283820/"))

This demonstrates substantial **biochemical reclassification**, but reclassification alone does not prove that the new interval improves patient outcomes. Establishing which threshold is preferable requires evidence relating the threshold to clinically meaningful outcomes, diagnostic performance, treatment benefits, and harms - not merely a better statistical fit to the observed distribution. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/"))

## Population range versus personal baseline

Population intervals combine variation between different people with variation occurring within one person. For some analytes, between-person differences are much larger than the changes normally seen within an individual. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36537071/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36537071/"))

This means a person can experience a change that is statistically unusual relative to expected analytical and within-person variation while remaining inside the population interval. Conversely, a stable result just outside the interval may reflect that person’s individual set point rather than a new change. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36537071/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36537071/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34773728/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34773728/"))

Laboratory medicine uses concepts such as the **reference change value**, which estimates how large a difference between serial results must be before it is unlikely to reflect analytical and ordinary within-person variation alone. Exceeding a reference change value does not by itself establish disease or clinical importance. The **index of individuality** describes how useful a population interval is for detecting changes in an individual. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36537071/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36537071/"))

In a small longitudinal study, 43 healthy people were tested annually for six years. About half of the 24 analytes studied had a low index of individuality, suggesting that population intervals had limited sensitivity for recognizing person-specific changes in those markers. The study was small and sampled only once per year, but it illustrates why longitudinal trends may add information that a single population interval cannot provide. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34773728/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34773728/"))

## Testosterone as a case study

Several observational datasets are consistent with a secular decline in male testosterone, although the claim that testosterone has fallen by exactly 25 percent is too precise to apply across populations, age groups, assays, and time periods. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40748419/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40748419/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17062768/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17062768/"))

A 2025 systematic review and aggregate-data meta-regression included 1,256 papers, 1,504 study groups, and more than one million men. The review searched publications through July 10, 2024 and used reported blood-collection years in its temporal analyses. Testosterone declined over calendar time after adjustment for study-level variables including age, body mass index, assay method, and sample size. However, heterogeneity, incomplete covariate data, selection bias, changing assays, and the use of aggregate rather than individual-level data make the magnitude and generalizability of the trend uncertain. A mass-spectrometry-only analysis did not confirm the decline, but that analysis contained very few pre-2000 groups, limiting its ability to evaluate the complete time period. The review declared no competing interests. ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40618-025-02671-9 "https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40618-025-02671-9"))

An earlier longitudinal analysis of 1,532 Boston-area men, contributing 2,769 observations between 1987 and 2004, also found a substantial age-independent population decline. Together, these datasets are consistent with a decline, while differences in sampling, assay technology, obesity, medication exposure, and study population make a single universal percentage difficult to defend. ([OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/1/196/2598434 "https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/1/196/2598434"))

### A secular decline does not prove reference-range drift

A 2006 survey of 25 laboratories found striking variation in total-testosterone intervals. Lower limits ranged from 130 to 450 ng/dL and upper limits from 486 to 1,593 ng/dL. Only four laboratories used age-adjusted ranges. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17100942/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17100942/"))

A later harmonization study standardized testosterone measurements across four U.S. and European cohorts. Its harmonized range of 264 to 916 ng/dL for healthy, non-obese men aged 19 to 39 was derived from 1,185 participants in the two cohorts that contained sufficient numbers of young men. The purpose was to correct assay and calibration differences - not to redefine normality around the average testosterone of all contemporary men. ([OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/102/4/1161/2884621 "https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/102/4/1161/2884621"))

Variation remains. A 2025 survey of 124 laboratories in 27 European countries found that fewer than half provided adequate guidance about morning or fasting collection, only approximately one quarter used mass spectrometry, and reported intervals varied considerably. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40068942/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40068942/"))

These studies establish cross-laboratory inconsistency and provide evidence consistent with a secular testosterone decline. They do **not** establish that laboratories systematically lowered or widened testosterone intervals as population testosterone fell. The stronger historical claim - that a particular value would have been flagged in 1990 but is now considered normal because ranges accommodated declining health - requires longitudinal records of actual laboratory intervals that the cited studies do not provide. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17100942/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17100942/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28324103/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28324103/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40068942/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40068942/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40748419/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40748419/"))

## Which competing claim is best supported?

### 1. “Reference ranges are statistically sound and clinically useful”

**Mostly true, with qualifications.**

Reference intervals are useful for placing results in population context and identifying statistically unusual values. The central 95 percent convention is well established, but it is a convention rather than proof that those limits are the best boundaries for every clinical purpose. A result outside the interval deserves interpretation, not an automatic conclusion that treatment is needed. ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/ "https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/"))

### 2. “Reference ranges are dangerously misleading because they come from unhealthy populations”

**Partly true, but the mechanism is overstated.**

Reference-population selection can be imperfect, and occult disease or broad inclusion criteria can influence the limits. Indirect methods begin with mixed patient data and can also be biased. But the standard direct approach does not normally consist of pooling everyone tested at a laboratory. False reassurance can arise when a reference interval is incorrectly treated as a diagnostic rule-out threshold, not because every reference interval is inherently derived from an unhealthy population. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29672266/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29672266/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/"))

### 3. “The main problem is how ranges are used”

**This is the best-supported claim, with one correction.**

Reference intervals are not designed solely for screening; they are interpretive tools used during diagnosis, assessment, and monitoring. But they should not be treated as diagnostic conclusions or individualized health targets. Construction quality still matters, so the problem is both **how an interval was established** and **how clinicians and patients interpret it**. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/"))

### 4. “Age-, sex-, and population-specific ranges would solve most of the problem”

**Partly true, but too optimistic.**

Appropriate partitioning can substantially improve classification for some analytes. It does not correct assay disagreement or preanalytical variation, and it does not remove limitations arising from reference-subject selection, within-person biological variation, or the absence of outcome-based thresholds. In some cases, harmonization across methods is not currently feasible. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26044506/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26044506/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37478022/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37478022/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36537071/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36537071/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17516737/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17516737/"))

## Quick reference

- A typical two-sided reference interval runs from the 2.5th to the 97.5th percentile. ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/ "https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/"))

- Approximately 5 percent of individuals who meet the reference-subject criteria fall outside it by construction. ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/ "https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/"))

- The direct method uses selected reference individuals. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29672266/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29672266/"))

- The indirect method uses routine patient data plus statistical modeling. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29672266/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29672266/"))

- A reference interval describes a population distribution. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/"))

- A clinical decision limit is intended to support a disease- or risk-related decision. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/"))

- “Within range” does not prove health. ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/ "https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/"))

- “Outside range” does not prove disease. ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/ "https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/"))

- A personal trend can be informative even when every result remains inside the population interval. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36537071/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36537071/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34773728/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34773728/"))


## Practical interpretation

- **Identify what the line represents.** Determine whether the report is showing a statistical reference interval, a clinical decision threshold, a therapeutic target, or an assay-specific alert level. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/"); [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/ "https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/"))

- **Consider the size and pattern of the deviation.** A result only slightly outside an interval should not be treated as categorically different from a result just inside it. A reference limit is an estimate rather than a biological discontinuity, so analytical uncertainty and the broader pattern of results should be considered. ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/ "https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37036018/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37036018/"))

- **Check collection conditions.** Time of day, fasting, exercise, recent illness, posture, medications, and specimen handling can matter, depending on the test. ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/ "https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29672266/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29672266/"))

- **Compare serial results carefully.** Where possible, compare measurements performed by the same laboratory and analytical method. A change between laboratories can reflect method or interval differences rather than a biological change. Where relevant, a reference change value can help assess whether a difference is unusual relative to analytical and within-person variation, but it does not establish clinical importance. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28647526/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28647526/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37478022/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37478022/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36537071/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36537071/"))

- **Use the correct demographic interval.** Age-, sex-, and pregnancy-specific intervals can be important when properly validated. ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/ "https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26044506/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26044506/"))

- **Do not replace one unsupported cutoff with another.** The limitations of laboratory reference intervals do not automatically validate narrower “optimal” ranges promoted without outcome evidence. An alternative range is more clinically meaningful only when it has stronger validation against relevant risks, diagnostic performance, treatment benefits, and harms. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/"))


## Evidence gaps

This is a narrative evidence review rather than a registered systematic review, so failure to identify a study should not be interpreted as proof that no such study exists. The evidence reviewed here supports several important limitations, but it does not support every popular criticism of reference ranges.

First, the sources reviewed here do not provide complete, analyte-by-analyte information on the reference-population composition used by every major commercial laboratory. They include a [large Labcorp method paper](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34626611/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34626611/") and [surveys of routine laboratory practice](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17516737/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17516737/"), but not an independent audit covering every major laboratory and analyte.

Second, the cited reclassification studies show how new intervals alter the number of results flagged or diagnostically classified, but they do not by themselves establish improvements in symptoms, disease prevention, treatment burden, or survival. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39283820/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39283820/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30047297/"))

Third, the studies reviewed here do not establish that deteriorating population health automatically causes reference intervals to drift. Whether an interval changes depends on how it is derived, whether it is recalculated, how reference subjects are selected, and whether routine patient results are used. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38205847/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38205847/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29672266/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29672266/"); [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/ "https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932992/"))

Finally, the reviewed testosterone evidence is consistent with a secular decline and substantial cross-laboratory variation, but it does not demonstrate systematic historical lowering or widening of testosterone reference intervals in response to that decline. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40748419/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40748419/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17100942/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17100942/"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40068942/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40068942/"))

## Funding and conflict-of-interest considerations

Financially relevant relationships should be identified without treating industry involvement as an automatic reason for exclusion.

The nationwide indirect-interval study by Fleming and colleagues was authored by current or former Labcorp personnel and evaluated a Labcorp dataset and methodology. It should be treated as a useful description of large-scale commercial-laboratory practice, not independent confirmation that the resulting intervals improve clinical outcomes. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34626611/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34626611/"))

The review of indirect methods by Jones and colleagues included authors affiliated with Sonic Healthcare and Labcorp. Those affiliations are relevant because the review concerns methods used by high-volume clinical laboratories, although they do not by themselves invalidate its methodological analysis. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29672266/ "https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29672266/"))

The 2017 testosterone harmonization study received substantial public and academic support, but several authors disclosed pharmaceutical grants, advisory work, consulting relationships, or other financial interests. The study’s assay-standardization findings should be considered alongside those disclosures. ([OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/102/4/1161/2884621 "https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/102/4/1161/2884621/"))

The 2025 systematic review of temporal testosterone trends declared no competing interests. ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40618-025-02671-9 "https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40618-025-02671-9"))
