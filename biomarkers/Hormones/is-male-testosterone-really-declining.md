# Is Male Testosterone Really Declining?

Average testosterone appears to have declined over calendar time in several male populations, beyond what would be expected from aging alone. However, the evidence does not support a universal decline of roughly 1 percent every year, nor does it show that men everywhere now have clinically deficient testosterone. ([Massachusetts Male Aging Study](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/1/196/2598434), [2025 systematic review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40618-025-02671-9), [NHANES comparison](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4137971/), [Danish population surveys](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/12/4696/2597312), [Endocrine Society clinical practice guideline](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/103/5/1715/4939465))

Some longitudinal cohorts and a large 2025 systematic review found declines that remained after adjustment for age and, where available, BMI. In the review, those adjustments used study-group mean values rather than individual-level data. Other strong evidence points in the opposite direction: nationally representative US data found no overall decline, a Danish study found that adjustment for BMI removed the decline in total testosterone, and the mass-spectrometry subgroup of the 2025 review did not reproduce the trend. The most defensible conclusion is therefore that a genuine but heterogeneous secular decline probably exists in some populations, while its average magnitude, universality, and causes remain uncertain. ([Massachusetts Male Aging Study](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/1/196/2598434), [2025 systematic review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40618-025-02671-9), [NHANES comparison](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4137971/), [Danish population surveys](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/12/4696/2597312))

## What a secular decline actually means

An age-related decline is a change within an individual as he grows older. A secular decline is different: men of the same age have lower average testosterone when measured in a later historical period or birth cohort. ([Age-period-cohort methods](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4221491/))

This distinction is important because many testosterone-trend studies are not true lifelong longitudinal studies. They compare different samples recruited at different times, combine study-level averages, or analyze patients whose clinicians ordered testosterone testing. Those designs can detect a calendar-time association, but they cannot automatically show that an otherwise identical man today has lower testosterone than his father did. ([2025 systematic review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40618-025-02671-9), [Israeli health-care database study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7063751/), [NHANES comparison](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4137971/))

Age, calendar period, and birth cohort are also mathematically linked: birth cohort equals calendar period minus age. Most of these designs therefore cannot fully distinguish a period effect affecting men at the time of measurement from a birth-cohort effect reflecting earlier-life conditions without additional assumptions. ([Age-period-cohort methods](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4221491/))

Total testosterone, free testosterone, bioavailable testosterone, and sex hormone-binding globulin, or SHBG, also need to be considered separately. Free testosterone is the unbound fraction, while bioavailable testosterone generally includes free plus albumin-bound testosterone. Most testosterone circulates bound to SHBG or albumin. A reduction in SHBG can lower measured total testosterone without producing a proportional reduction in free testosterone. In the Danish study, free testosterone was calculated rather than directly measured, and its lack of an age-independent trend shows why a decline in total testosterone alone is not sufficient to establish an equivalent decline in the unbound fraction. ([Danish population surveys](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/12/4696/2597312), [Endocrine Society clinical practice guideline](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/103/5/1715/4939465))

## Evidence that the decline is real

A major US source is the Massachusetts Male Aging Study. It included 1,532 randomly selected men from the greater Boston area and 2,769 observations across three measurement waves ending in 2004. The investigators reported a substantial age-independent decline in total and calculated bioavailable testosterone that was not explained by the observed changes in obesity, smoking, health, or other measured lifestyle variables. ([OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/1/196/2598434))

A separate longitudinal analysis of 991 US Air Force veterans followed through six examination cycles over 20 years also found that weight gain intensified testosterone loss. However, among men who maintained or lost weight, the paper reported a 19 percent decline over 20 years, given as 117 ng/dL in the abstract and results and 107 ng/dL in the discussion. This argues against obesity being a complete explanation within that particular cohort. The analysis was limited to men who completed all six cycles, creating a healthy-survivor selection risk, and the authors noted possible assay artifacts in two examination cycles. Military-veteran samples are also not necessarily representative of the general population. ([PLOS One](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0076178))

A large Israeli health-care database subsequently identified an age-independent decline among 102,334 men receiving their first recorded total-testosterone test between 2006 and March 2019. The decline was present in most age groups. The authors judged it unlikely to be explained by the observed changes in BMI, but the available data did not exclude individual-level confounding by adiposity, metabolic health, sleep apnea, medications, or illness. Its major limitation is selection: this was a clinically tested population, so changes in who was tested, why testing was ordered, and which medical conditions were represented could contribute to the trend. ([PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7063751/))

## The 2025 systematic review

The 2025 systematic review included 1,256 papers, 1,504 study groups, and 1,064,891 participants. Meta-regression found that study-group mean total testosterone was lower in studies collecting samples later across approximately 55 years. The association persisted after adjustment for study-level mean age and, in the subset with available data, mean BMI. It also remained statistically significant when the analysis was restricted to study groups with a mean BMI below 25 kg/m². Luteinizing hormone also declined in the authors' analyses, which they interpreted as being more compatible with altered hypothalamic-pituitary regulation than with isolated testicular failure. That parallel LH finding is compatible with altered central regulation, but it does not by itself establish a physiological "resetting." ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40618-025-02671-9))

The very large cumulative sample size reduces random error, but it does not remove the design's important limits. This was a meta-regression of published **study-group averages**, not an individual-participant longitudinal analysis or a pooled set of serially representative population surveys. The groups came from a changing mix of prospective clinical trials, case-control studies, and cross-sectional studies conducted in different countries, laboratories, and populations. BMI was reported in only 746 study groups, and variables such as body composition, sleep apnea, medication use, smoking exposure, physical activity, insulin resistance, chronic illness, and sampling time were not consistently available. These limitations leave the result vulnerable to ecological bias, changing study composition, selection bias, assay heterogeneity, and incomplete covariate control. Because some papers contributed more than one study group, estimates may also be correlated unless that dependence is explicitly modeled; the report does not clearly describe a paper-level clustering correction. The review included study groups classified as healthy and eugonadal, but a population mean cannot show how the lower tail of the distribution changed. The review did not measure symptoms or establish an equivalent rise in confirmed clinical hypogonadism. ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40618-025-02671-9))

There was also an important assay finding. Declines appeared in studies using immunometric assays, ELISA, and radioimmunoassay, but not in the subgroup using mass spectrometry. Only five mass-spectrometry papers, comprising eight study groups, predated 2000, so the null finding could reflect an insufficient historical window rather than the absence of a decline. Still, it prevents assay artifact from being dismissed. ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40618-025-02671-9))

## The most recent analysis is still preliminary

A July 2026 conference-supplement abstract pooled 12 trend studies, containing 40 data points from seven countries and 102,334 participants. It estimated annual declines of 0.26 nmol/L in total testosterone, 4.13 pmol/L in free testosterone, and 0.16 nmol/L in SHBG. A post hoc analysis estimated a larger total-testosterone decline in studies with a median sampling year after 2000 than in those before 2000. ([OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/41/Supplement_1/deag083.202/8727608))

This is relevant because it reports a trend in free testosterone as well as total testosterone. However, it is currently an abbreviated conference report rather than a full-length article with fully inspectable study selection, assay methods, information on whether free testosterone was measured or calculated, subgroup analyses, heterogeneity estimates, risk-of-bias assessments, funding disclosures, and study-level results. The reported total participant count is also identical to the 102,334 participants in the Israeli database study described above, but the abstract provides no study table from which to determine how that count was assembled or how strongly one large dataset influenced the pooled estimates. Its data came primarily from high-income countries, and the authors acknowledged that BMI and other population differences could contribute. It should therefore be treated as new, hypothesis-generating supporting evidence, not as the final resolution of the question. ([OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/41/Supplement_1/deag083.202/8727608))

## Evidence against a universal decline

A nationally representative US counterexample comes from NHANES samples. Investigators compared 1,413 men examined in 1988-1991 with 902 examined in 1999-2004, restricting the analysis to morning blood samples and adjusting for age, race or ethnicity, BMI, waist circumference, alcohol intake, and smoking. They found little change in total testosterone, calculated free testosterone, or SHBG and concluded that there was no evidence of an overall decline in the US general population over that interval. ([PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4137971/))

The Danish study analyzed 5,350 serum samples from four population surveys conducted between 1982 and 2001. Later cohorts had lower total testosterone and altered SHBG, but there was no significant age-independent trend in calculated free testosterone. Adjustment for the concurrent increase in BMI removed the total-testosterone trend. The investigators proposed that the primary change was in SHBG, followed by a lower total-testosterone concentration that preserved calculated free testosterone. ([OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/12/4696/2597312))

These findings do not prove that other cohorts are wrong. They show that the trend varies according to population, period, assay, hormone measure, and adjustment strategy. The familiar "1 percent per year" figure should consequently be treated as a result from particular cohorts, not as a biological constant that applies to every country, age group, or generation. ([Massachusetts Male Aging Study](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/1/196/2598434), [2025 systematic review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40618-025-02671-9), [NHANES comparison](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4137971/), [Danish population surveys](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/12/4696/2597312))

## How much does obesity explain?

Obesity and metabolic dysfunction have a well-documented, partly reversible relationship with lower testosterone, supported by longitudinal and weight-loss intervention evidence. ([Weight-loss meta-analysis](https://academic.oup.com/ejendo/article-abstract/168/6/829/6659681), [Endocrine Society clinical practice guideline](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/103/5/1715/4939465))

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 24 weight-loss studies found that both low-calorie diets and bariatric surgery were associated with increases in total and unbound testosterone. The average total-testosterone increase was 2.87 nmol/L following dietary weight loss and 8.73 nmol/L after bariatric surgery. The amount of weight lost was the strongest predictor of the testosterone increase. ([OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/ejendo/article-abstract/168/6/829/6659681))

This strongly supports excess adiposity and related metabolic dysfunction as reversible contributors to lower testosterone. Because many included studies were uncontrolled before-and-after comparisons and bariatric surgery changes multiple physiological pathways, the meta-analysis does not by itself isolate fat loss as the sole causal mechanism. It also does **not** establish what proportion of the population-level historical trend is caused by obesity. The Massachusetts and Air Force cohorts retained declines after adjustment or stratification by weight, while the Danish trend disappeared after BMI adjustment. The evidence therefore supports a large metabolic contribution whose size probably differs across populations, not a single universal answer. ([Weight-loss meta-analysis](https://academic.oup.com/ejendo/article-abstract/168/6/829/6659681), [Massachusetts Male Aging Study](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/1/196/2598434), [PLOS One](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0076178), [Danish population surveys](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/12/4696/2597312))

BMI adjustment is also an incomplete test of the metabolic hypothesis. BMI does not directly measure body composition or metabolic health, and study-level adjustment for mean BMI cannot reproduce individual-level relationships between adiposity and hormones. A residual trend in a BMI-adjusted model therefore does not prove that metabolic factors played no role. ([2025 systematic review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40618-025-02671-9), [Endocrine Society clinical practice guideline](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/103/5/1715/4939465))

## Smoking, sleep, and other lifestyle confounders

A meta-analysis of 22 observational studies involving 13,317 men found that current smokers had, on average, 1.53 nmol/L higher total testosterone than nonsmokers. The studies were heterogeneous and observational, so this does not prove that nicotine itself causally increases testosterone or that smoking is hormonally beneficial. It does mean that smoking prevalence is a potential confounder in historical comparisons. The size and direction of any contribution to the secular trend have not been established. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26763163/))

Sleep is biologically plausible but less convincing as an explanation for a decades-long population trend. A meta-analysis of 18 studies involving 252 men found no statistically significant pooled effect from short-term partial sleep deprivation, whereas total sleep deprivation lasting at least 24 hours reduced testosterone. Those experiments establish an acute effect of severe sleep loss; they do not quantify how changing chronic sleep patterns have affected population testosterone since the 1970s. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34801825/))

Physical inactivity, psychological stress, chronic illness, medication use, and sleep-disordered breathing are also plausible contributors. The central problem is that historical studies rarely measured all of them consistently. Statements that lifestyle explains "most" of the decline go beyond the currently available attribution evidence, even though weight change, health, and metabolic factors can affect testosterone within individuals. ([2025 systematic review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40618-025-02671-9), [Air Force longitudinal cohort](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0076178), [Weight-loss meta-analysis](https://academic.oup.com/ejendo/article-abstract/168/6/829/6659681), [Endocrine Society clinical practice guideline](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/103/5/1715/4939465))

## How much could be a measurement artifact?

Testosterone measurements can differ substantially across laboratories and assay platforms. In a direct comparison of six immunoassays against liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry, one platform underestimated testosterone by an average of approximately 90 ng/dL while another overestimated it by approximately 99 ng/dL. Although most assays distinguished broadly eugonadal from hypogonadal men when laboratory-specific ranges were used, substantial platform-dependent bias was present. ([OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/89/2/534/2840750))

A secular comparison can therefore become biased when early and late samples are analyzed by different platforms, laboratories, calibration systems, or reference populations. Differences in blood-collection time, participant health, and the clinical indications for testing can introduce further variation. ([Laboratory-method comparison](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/89/2/534/2840750), [Harmonized testosterone reference ranges](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28324103/), [Endocrine Society clinical practice guideline](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/103/5/1715/4939465), [Israeli health-care database study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7063751/))

Measurement artifact is unlikely to explain every finding, especially declines observed within a cohort or under a common study protocol. But the null result in the mass-spectrometry subgroup of the 2025 review, the null NHANES comparison, and the different behavior of total and calculated free testosterone in Denmark all show that methodology can materially alter the conclusion. ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40618-025-02671-9), [PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4137971/), [OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/12/4696/2597312))

The strongest future test would be to retrieve archived serum from representative cohorts spanning several decades and remeasure every sample in the same laboratory, using a standardized isotope-dilution mass-spectrometry method. Samples from older and newer cohorts should be randomized across analytical batches, technicians should be blinded to collection year, and storage duration and freeze-thaw history should be addressed explicitly.

## Endocrine disruptors: plausible contributors, not proven drivers

### Phthalates

A 2024 systematic review included 37 studies from eight countries and 28,911 participants. In the pooled male evidence, higher concentrations of metabolites of di(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate, or DEHP, were associated with higher SHBG and lower total testosterone, free androgen index, and FSH. However, the findings varied by population. Significant reductions in free testosterone and androgen indices were more apparent among infertile men, while the meta-analysis of fertile reproductive-age men did not find significant associations with total testosterone, SHBG, LH, FSH, or estradiol. Most included studies were cross-sectional and frequently relied on a single urine sample for a rapidly cleared chemical. ([PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11574633/))

### Bisphenol A

A 2024 systematic review of 18 epidemiological studies found that BPA exposure was most frequently associated with higher SHBG and estradiol and lower biologically active androgen measures. Total-testosterone findings were inconsistent, with some studies reporting higher rather than lower total testosterone. Because the hormone studies were heterogeneous, their results were reviewed qualitatively instead of being combined into one pooled testosterone estimate. ([PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11054375/))

### PFAS

A meta-analysis of 12 studies involving 7,506 participants found inverse associations between testosterone and two PFAS compounds, PFNA and PFOA. The pooled coefficients were relatively small, and the underlying literature was observational and inconsistent across individual PFAS compounds. These results identify PFAS as candidate exposures associated with testosterone variation, but they do not establish that PFAS exposure caused the historical testosterone trend. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38262091/))

### Microplastics

Microplastics were detected in all 23 human testicular samples examined in one 2024 tissue study. That small, selected tissue series demonstrated exposure and tissue presence; it could not estimate population prevalence and did not measure testosterone or demonstrate hormonal harm in humans. The study did not provide longitudinal human exposure-hormone data, so it cannot establish that microplastics contribute to secular testosterone change. ([Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/toxsci/article/200/2/235/7673133))

Overall, the studies reviewed here support biological plausibility and several exposure-hormone associations. They do not establish that phthalates, BPA, PFAS, microplastics, or their mixtures explain a substantial attributable fraction of the secular decline. Exposure studies would need repeated biomarker measurements, prospective hormone data, mixture analysis, and formal mediation of calendar-time trends to support that conclusion. ([DEHP systematic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11574633/), [BPA systematic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11054375/), [PFAS meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38262091/), [Microplastics tissue study](https://academic.oup.com/toxsci/article/200/2/235/7673133))

## Are the functional consequences real?

True androgen deficiency has real physiological effects. Experimental suppression and graded replacement of male gonadal steroids show that androgens influence lean mass, muscle size, strength, and sexual function, while estradiol also contributes substantially to body-fat regulation and sexual function. ([New England Journal of Medicine](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1206168))

Clinical treatment effects are nevertheless outcome-specific rather than universal. In the Testosterone Trials, 790 symptomatic men aged 65 or older with repeatedly low testosterone were randomized to testosterone or placebo for one year. Testosterone produced a moderate improvement in sexual function and small improvements in mood. It did not significantly improve the primary vitality endpoint. The primary walking-distance endpoint was not significant within the Physical Function Trial, although a secondary analysis across all trial participants favored testosterone. The trial was too small and short to establish long-term treatment risks. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26886521/))

In a 211-participant substudy, testosterone increased volumetric bone density and estimated bone strength, especially in spinal trabecular bone. That substudy did not test fracture outcomes. A later randomized fracture subtrial involving 5,204 middle-aged and older men with hypogonadism and preexisting or high cardiovascular risk found no fracture-prevention benefit. After a median follow-up of 3.19 years, clinical fractures occurred in 3.50 percent of the testosterone group and 2.46 percent of the placebo group, corresponding to a hazard ratio of 1.43 with a 95 percent confidence interval of 1.04 to 1.97. This does not establish that testosterone universally increases fracture risk. Taken together, these separate studies show that improved bone-density surrogates do not by themselves establish fewer clinical fractures. ([JAMA Internal Medicine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5433755/), [New England Journal of Medicine](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2308836))

These trials show that confirmed low testosterone can matter clinically. They do not prove that a modest downward movement in the population mean has produced a corresponding epidemic of low libido, fatigue, muscle loss, or osteoporosis. The 2025 systematic review included study groups classified as healthy and eugonadal and reported an overall study-group mean total testosterone of 18.5 nmol/L. A population mean cannot show how the lower tail of a distribution changed, and the trend studies did not measure whether the prevalence of repeatedly low testosterone plus compatible symptoms increased because of the hormonal shift. ([Testosterone Trials](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26886521/), [JAMA Internal Medicine bone substudy](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5433755/), [TRAVERSE fracture subtrial](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2308836), [2025 systematic review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40618-025-02671-9))

Cause also matters clinically. Obesity, some sleep disorders, chronic illness, medications, primary testicular disease, and pituitary disease can produce different combinations of symptoms and hormone results. They do not imply identical evaluation or management. A population-level trend cannot diagnose an individual or establish that testosterone replacement is the appropriate response. ([Endocrine Society clinical practice guideline](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/103/5/1715/4939465))

## Verdict on the competing claims

1. **"The decline is real and not fully explained by obesity." - Moderately supported.** ([Massachusetts Male Aging Study](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/1/196/2598434), [2025 systematic review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40618-025-02671-9))

    Multiple cohorts and the 2025 systematic review found residual declines after age and BMI adjustment, including among study groups with BMI below 25. However, the review used aggregate study-level adjustment, BMI information was incomplete, some representative data were null, and the mass-spectrometry subgroup did not confirm the decline. ([Massachusetts Male Aging Study](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/1/196/2598434), [Air Force longitudinal cohort](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0076178), [2025 systematic review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40618-025-02671-9), [NHANES comparison](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4137971/), [Danish population surveys](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/12/4696/2597312))

2. **"The decline is primarily metabolic." - Plausible but not demonstrated.** ([Weight-loss meta-analysis](https://academic.oup.com/ejendo/article-abstract/168/6/829/6659681), [Danish population surveys](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/12/4696/2597312))

    Obesity and metabolic dysfunction are strongly associated with lower testosterone, weight loss commonly raises it, and BMI explained the Danish total-testosterone trend. But other cohorts retained substantial declines among weight-stable or BMI-adjusted men. The cited evidence does not reliably calculate whether obesity, sleep, inactivity, stress, and related metabolic factors collectively explain "most" of the secular change. ([Weight-loss meta-analysis](https://academic.oup.com/ejendo/article-abstract/168/6/829/6659681), [Danish population surveys](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/12/4696/2597312), [Massachusetts Male Aging Study](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/1/196/2598434), [Air Force longitudinal cohort](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0076178), [2025 systematic review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40618-025-02671-9))

3. **"The decline is partly a measurement artifact." - Likely.** ([Laboratory-method comparison](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/89/2/534/2840750), [2025 systematic review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40618-025-02671-9))

    Testosterone assays can differ materially, trends vary according to the hormone measure used, and the recent mass-spectrometry subgroup was null. Measurement differences probably contribute to the inconsistency, although they do not adequately explain every within-cohort result. ([Laboratory-method comparison](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/89/2/534/2840750), [Harmonized testosterone reference ranges](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28324103/), [2025 systematic review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40618-025-02671-9), [NHANES comparison](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4137971/), [Danish population surveys](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/12/4696/2597312))

4. **"Regardless of cause, the functional consequences are real." - Only partly correct.** ([Testosterone Trials](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26886521/), [TRAVERSE fracture subtrial](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2308836))

    Clinically important androgen deficiency has real consequences, and treatment can improve selected outcomes in appropriately studied men. But a lower population mean is not the same as an epidemic of symptomatic hypogonadism, and testosterone treatment has not improved every outcome or prevented fractures in the populations tested. The cause remains important because metabolic suppression, medication effects, and structural endocrine disease require different evaluation and management. ([Testosterone Trials](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26886521/), [TRAVERSE fracture subtrial](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2308836), [Endocrine Society clinical practice guideline](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/103/5/1715/4939465))


## Evidence that would resolve the question

The most informative future research would include:

- Reanalysis of archived serum from multiple decades using one standardized mass-spectrometry laboratory, with samples randomized across batches and technicians blinded to sample year.

- Repeated morning measurements in the same individuals and representative birth cohorts, rather than comparisons of study-group averages or clinically tested patients.

- Individual-level adjustment for waist circumference and body composition, insulin resistance, objectively measured sleep and sleep apnea, cotinine-confirmed smoking, physical activity, alcohol, medications, acute illness, and chronic comorbidities.

- Studies in populations whose obesity and smoking prevalence remained relatively stable, providing a stronger natural control for metabolic explanations.

- Repeated measurements of phthalates, bisphenols, PFAS, pesticides, air pollutants, and plastic particles, analyzed as mixtures rather than one chemical at a time.

- Mediation analyses estimating how much of the calendar-time effect is statistically attributable to metabolic health, lifestyle, environmental exposure, assay change, and participant selection, with their causal assumptions stated explicitly.

- Concurrent measurement of total testosterone, free testosterone measured by a validated equilibrium-dialysis method or calculated with a validated equation, SHBG, LH, FSH, estradiol, semen parameters, symptoms, body composition, bone outcomes, and sexual function.


## Quick reference

- **Is there evidence of an age-independent decline?** Yes, in several populations. ([Massachusetts Male Aging Study](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/1/196/2598434), [Air Force longitudinal cohort](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0076178), [Israeli health-care database study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7063751/), [2025 systematic review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40618-025-02671-9))

- **Is a universal 1-percent annual decline established?** No. ([Massachusetts Male Aging Study](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/1/196/2598434), [NHANES comparison](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4137971/), [Danish population surveys](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/12/4696/2597312), [2025 systematic review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40618-025-02671-9))

- **Does obesity lower testosterone?** Excess adiposity and metabolic dysfunction commonly lower it, and weight loss commonly raises it. ([Weight-loss meta-analysis](https://academic.oup.com/ejendo/article-abstract/168/6/829/6659681), [Endocrine Society clinical practice guideline](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/103/5/1715/4939465))

- **Does obesity explain the entire secular trend?** No convincing evidence establishes that. ([Massachusetts Male Aging Study](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/1/196/2598434), [Air Force longitudinal cohort](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0076178), [Danish population surveys](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/12/4696/2597312), [2025 systematic review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40618-025-02671-9))

- **Could assay changes explain part of the trend?** Yes. ([Laboratory-method comparison](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/89/2/534/2840750), [Harmonized testosterone reference ranges](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28324103/), [2025 systematic review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40618-025-02671-9))

- **Are endocrine disruptors associated with altered male hormones?** Some are, but population-trend attribution is unproven. ([DEHP systematic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11574633/), [BPA systematic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11054375/), [PFAS meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38262091/))

- **Do microplastics explain declining testosterone?** The cited human tissue study does not demonstrate that. ([Microplastics tissue study](https://academic.oup.com/toxsci/article/200/2/235/7673133))

- **Has an epidemic of symptomatic hypogonadism been established?** No. The trend studies did not establish one. ([2025 systematic review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40618-025-02671-9), [Endocrine Society clinical practice guideline](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/103/5/1715/4939465))

- **Best overall conclusion:** Evidence supports a real but heterogeneous decline in some populations, while the relative contributions of biological change, metabolic health, measurement, and participant selection remain unquantified. ([Massachusetts Male Aging Study](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/1/196/2598434), [2025 systematic review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40618-025-02671-9), [NHANES comparison](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4137971/), [Danish population surveys](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/12/4696/2597312))


## Practical interpretation

The phrase **"testosterone crisis"** overstates what the evidence can currently support. It combines three separate questions: whether average concentrations have changed, why they changed, and whether clinical disease increased, as though they had already received the same answer. ([2025 systematic review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40618-025-02671-9), [Endocrine Society clinical practice guideline](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/103/5/1715/4939465))

The first question has moderate supporting evidence. The second remains unresolved. The third has not been directly established by the trend studies. ([Massachusetts Male Aging Study](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/1/196/2598434), [2025 systematic review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40618-025-02671-9), [NHANES comparison](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4137971/), [Danish population surveys](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/12/4696/2597312))

Calling the phenomenon purely a measurement artifact would dismiss several meaningful cohort findings. Calling it a mysterious toxic collapse would overlook the strong, reversible effects of obesity and metabolic health. Calling it only an obesity problem would ignore residual trends, assay-standardized uncertainty, environmental plausibility, and cohorts in which weight did not explain the decline. ([Massachusetts Male Aging Study](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/1/196/2598434), [Air Force longitudinal cohort](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0076178), [Weight-loss meta-analysis](https://academic.oup.com/ejendo/article-abstract/168/6/829/6659681), [Laboratory-method comparison](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/89/2/534/2840750), [DEHP systematic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11574633/), [PFAS meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38262091/))

The most accurate framing is therefore **a possible population-level endocrine shift observed amid broader metabolic, environmental, behavioral, and methodological change, not a demonstrated single-cause male hormone crisis.** ([Massachusetts Male Aging Study](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/1/196/2598434), [2025 systematic review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40618-025-02671-9), [NHANES comparison](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4137971/), [Danish population surveys](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article-abstract/92/12/4696/2597312))

## Funding and conflict-of-interest considerations

The 2025 systematic review reported that its open-access publication charge was covered through a university agreement and declared no competing interests. This describes publication support, not necessarily funding of the underlying research. ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40618-025-02671-9))

The weight-loss meta-analysis included Farid Saad, whose listed affiliation was Bayer Schering Pharma. This is relevant disclosure information, but an affiliation alone does not invalidate the results; study design, analysis, and consistency with other evidence remain the primary considerations. ([OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/ejendo/article-abstract/168/6/829/6659681))

The Testosterone Trials were principally NIH-funded, but AbbVie provided grants or nonfinancial support, and several investigators disclosed relationships with manufacturers of testosterone or related products. The trial's randomized design remains valuable, but these relationships should be disclosed when treatment effects are discussed. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26886521/))

The 2024 fracture trial was a subtrial of TRAVERSE, was funded by AbbVie and others, and included AbbVie employees among its authors. Its randomized, double-blind design remains important, while the sponsor and author relationships should be considered in the usual assessment of transparency and risk of bias. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38231621/))

The 2024 DEHP meta-analysis reported public or academic funding and no competing financial interest, while the 2024 PFAS meta-analysis reported no competing financial interests. ([PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11574633/), [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38262091/))

The 2026 testosterone-trend report is presently available as a conference-supplement abstract; the accessible report does not provide the complete funding and conflict detail expected in a full publication. ([OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/41/Supplement_1/deag083.202/8727608))
