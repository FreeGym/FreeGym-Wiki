# Medications That Distort Glucose Testing

Glucose and HbA1c are generally robust markers, but medications can change what the results mean. Some drugs genuinely raise or lower glucose. Others alter red blood cell turnover and disconnect HbA1c from actual glucose exposure. A third group interferes analytically with particular glucose meters or continuous glucose monitoring systems. ([CTT Collaboration, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38554713/); [Hirst et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22275444/); [Albright et al., 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15259122/); [Heinemann, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34911382/))

These mechanisms should not be treated as interchangeable. Glucocorticoid-induced hyperglycemia may require treatment during the exposure, a falsely low HbA1c requires assessment with direct glucose data or an alternative glycemic marker, and suspected sensor interference requires confirmation with a compatible method. ([Aberer et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8157052/); [Danese et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25591856/); [Heinemann, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34911382/))

## Three different problems hidden under one label

**True changes in glucose physiology:** Glucocorticoids, antipsychotics, thiazide diuretics, statins, metformin, and incretin-based drugs can change actual glucose concentrations. In these cases, the measured result reflects physiology under the influence of treatment. ([Liu et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25402408/); [Smith et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40864439/); [Hall et al., 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32157653/); [CTT Collaboration, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38554713/); [Hirst et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22275444/); [Jalleh et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39418085/))

**Biological distortion of HbA1c:** Drugs that shorten red blood cell survival, cause hemolysis, or stimulate red blood cell production can make HbA1c lower than expected for the person's glucose exposure. Some medications, particularly dapsone, can also cause assay-specific analytical interference through methemoglobinemia. ([Unnikrishnan et al., 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3401751/); [Albright et al., 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15259122/))

**Analytical interference:** Certain medications or their metabolites can be detected by a particular meter or sensor as if they were glucose. This can create a falsely high or low reading even though the person's glucose has not changed. The effect is highly device-specific. ([Heinemann, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34911382/); [Tellez et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33314990/))

## Glucocorticoids: a major cause of drug-induced hyperglycemia

Systemic glucocorticoids are among the most clinically important medications affecting glucose. A meta-analysis of 13 studies in people without known diabetes found glucocorticoid-associated hyperglycemia in 32.3 percent and newly diagnosed diabetes in 18.6 percent. Twelve of the 13 included studies were retrospective or observational, so these figures should not be treated as universal probabilities for every dose, disease, or population. A more recent matched cohort study also found substantially more new-onset hyperglycemia among hospitalized adults exposed to systemic glucocorticoids. ([Liu et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25402408/); [Golubic et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40171899/))

The timing of testing matters. Intermediate- to high-dose glucocorticoid treatment can produce predominantly afternoon and evening hyperglycemia, particularly when a drug such as prednisone or prednisolone is taken in the morning. In a study of patients with rheumatic diseases, hyperglycemia appeared after lunch and dinner by the third day of treatment. Fasting glucose alone can therefore remain normal while clinically important post-meal hyperglycemia is already present. ([Cansu et al., 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30914158/))

HbA1c can rise during sustained glucocorticoid exposure, but it is poorly suited to detecting hyperglycemia that began only days or a few weeks earlier. HbA1c reflects cumulative glycemia over approximately the preceding 2 to 3 months, weighted toward more recent weeks. Shortly after glucocorticoids are started, HbA1c may largely reflect the period before treatment. A baseline HbA1c can help identify pre-existing dysglycemia, but direct glucose measurements timed to the expected steroid effect are more informative for early detection. ([Sherwani et al., 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4933534/); [Suh and Park, 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5503862/); [Cansu et al., 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30914158/))

Route also matters. Systemic glucocorticoids are well-established causes of hyperglycemia, but inhaled and locally injected treatment should not be assumed to be metabolically inert. A systematic review of seven small studies involving 72 people with diabetes found increased glucose after intra-articular steroid injections, commonly peaking 24 to 72 hours after injection. The evidence was limited by small samples and low study quality. ([Choudhry et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27500431/))

For inhaled corticosteroids in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, a 2023 meta-analysis found no statistically significant increase in hyperglycemia across randomized trials, with an odds ratio of 1.07. Observational data suggested greater diabetes risk with long-term high-dose exposure, including an odds ratio of 1.20 in a high-dose subgroup. High-dose inhaled therapy may therefore matter, but it should not be presented as metabolically equivalent to systemic glucocorticoid treatment. ([Kholis et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38035057/))

## Is steroid-induced diabetes reversible?

The distinction is not between "real" diabetes and "false" diabetes. Glucocorticoid-induced hyperglycemia is real while the exposure is present. The unresolved question is whether glucose returns below the diagnostic range after the dose is reduced or withdrawn. ([Aberer et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8157052/); [Shah et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9142341/))

Some patients normalize after glucocorticoid withdrawal. Others remain dysglycemic because glucocorticoids exposed a pre-existing vulnerability involving insulin resistance, limited beta-cell reserve, or previously unrecognized diabetes. Reviews of steroid-induced hyperglycemia therefore recommend continued reassessment after dose reduction or withdrawal rather than assuming either complete reversibility or permanent diabetes from the first abnormal result. ([Aberer et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8157052/); [Shah et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9142341/))

Starting a glucose-lowering medication during a six-week steroid course is not necessarily inappropriate. Temporary treatment may be appropriate when hyperglycemia is sufficiently severe or symptomatic. The diagnosis and treatment plan should be reassessed as the steroid dose changes, including consideration of de-escalation after withdrawal. ([Aberer et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8157052/); [Shah et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9142341/))

## Antipsychotics: substantial differences between drugs

A 2025 systematic review identified 163 blinded randomized trials of antipsychotic treatment, with 127 included in the meta-analysis. Compared with placebo, antipsychotics increased fasting glucose by an average of 0.72 mg/dL, HbA1c by 0.04 percentage points, and the odds of hyperglycemia by 29 percent. These average shifts were small. Evidence certainty was low for hyperglycemia and very low for fasting glucose and HbA1c, and the fasting-glucose analysis showed evidence of publication-bias risk. The hyperglycemia signal remained after considering treatment duration, dose, diagnosis, and the medication's tendency to cause weight gain. The findings suggest that antipsychotic-related glucose dysregulation is not fully explained by weight-gain propensity, although changes in weight and adiposity still contribute. ([Smith et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40864439/))

The pooled average conceals important differences between drugs. A publicly funded network meta-analysis of 100 randomized trials involving 25,952 patients found that olanzapine and clozapine had the least favorable overall metabolic profiles. Changes in glucose relative to placebo ranged from a reduction of 0.29 mmol/L with lurasidone to an increase of 1.05 mmol/L with clozapine. Aripiprazole, brexpiprazole, cariprazine, lurasidone, and ziprasidone were among the more metabolically benign options in that acute-treatment evidence base. ([Pillinger et al., 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31860457/))

Quetiapine is not metabolically neutral. The cited reviews establish meaningful between-drug differences, but they do not define a consistent quetiapine-specific dose threshold at which glucose effects begin. Drug choice and baseline metabolic risk remain important, while longer-term and dose-specific effects are less certain. ([Smith et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40864439/); [Pillinger et al., 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31860457/))

Baseline and follow-up metabolic monitoring is still inconsistently performed. A systematic review covering 290,534 patients found that baseline glucose was measured in only 44.3 percent. The review reported lipid and HbA1c monitoring in fewer than 25 percent, although individual triglyceride and cholesterol measurements were recorded more often. Monitoring improved after implementation of guidelines but remained incomplete. ([Mitchell et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21846426/))

Several authors of the 2025 antipsychotic meta-analysis disclosed financial relationships with manufacturers of psychiatric medications. Its results should be interpreted with those relationships in mind. However, the overall conclusion that metabolic effects differ meaningfully between drugs is also supported by the independently conducted 2020 network meta-analysis, which was funded by the UK Medical Research Council, Wellcome Trust, and National Institute for Health Research. ([Smith et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40864439/); [Pillinger et al., 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31860457/))

## Thiazide diuretics: a small average glucose increase

A meta-analysis of 95 randomized trials involving 76,608 participants found that thiazide and thiazide-like diuretics raised fasting plasma glucose by an average of 0.20 mmol/L, equivalent to approximately 3.6 mg/dL. The authors concluded that the average effect was small and unlikely to be clinically important for most patients. ([Hall et al., 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32157653/))

Potassium depletion is a proposed contributor because lower potassium may impair insulin secretion. However, the meta-analysis did not find that combining a thiazide with a potassium-correcting medication materially changed the pooled fasting-glucose effect. Potassium may contribute in some individuals, but it should not be presented as the conclusively established explanation for the entire thiazide effect. ([Hall et al., 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32157653/))

The reported effect is therefore a genuine but generally modest change in fasting glucose. ([Hall et al., 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32157653/))

## Beta-blockers: symptom modification is not the same as test distortion

The main concerns described in the cited beta-blocker studies are physiological: some beta-blockers can modestly worsen glucose handling, and adrenergic signs of hypoglycemia may change. ([Kerr et al., 1990](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1974143/); [Bakris et al., 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15536109/))

The common statement that beta-blockers completely "mask hypoglycemia" is too broad. In a randomized crossover study of 12 healthy volunteers, propranolol, atenolol, and metoprolol modified heart-rate, blood-pressure, and tremor responses during moderate experimental hypoglycemia. Sweating was increased, and overall symptoms and awareness were not significantly reduced. The study was small, old, and conducted in healthy volunteers, so it does not eliminate concern in insulin-treated diabetes, but it shows that hypoglycemia awareness is not simply switched off by every beta-blocker. ([Kerr et al., 1990](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1974143/))

Metabolic effects also differ between agents. In a randomized trial in people with type 2 diabetes and hypertension, metoprolol worsened HbA1c while carvedilol did not, and carvedilol produced a more favorable change in insulin resistance. The trial was funded by GlaxoSmithKline, which marketed carvedilol, so the findings should be interpreted with that sponsorship in mind. They support heterogeneity within the beta-blocker class, not the conclusion that carvedilol is metabolically superior in every clinical setting. ([Bakris et al., 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15536109/))

## Statins: a small real glycemic shift, not a false HbA1c

Large-scale randomized evidence indicates that statins cause a small, dose-dependent upward shift in actual glycemia. An individual-participant-data meta-analysis included 19 blinded statin-versus-placebo trials with 123,940 participants and four trials comparing more- with less-intensive statin treatment. ([CTT Collaboration, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38554713/))

Low- or moderate-intensity statin therapy increased new-onset diabetes by 10 percent relative to placebo, with annual rates of 1.3 versus 1.2 percent. High-intensity therapy produced a 36 percent relative increase in the relevant trials. Among participants without diabetes, average HbA1c increased by 0.06 percentage points with low- or moderate-intensity therapy and by 0.08 percentage points with high-intensity therapy. Approximately 62 percent of new diagnoses occurred among participants already in the highest quarter of the baseline glycemia distribution. ([CTT Collaboration, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38554713/))

The statin-associated HbA1c rise should therefore not be dismissed as laboratory noise. It represents a small genuine deterioration in glycemia for some patients, particularly those already close to a diagnostic threshold. At the same time, the cardiovascular benefit observed in statin trials already incorporates this adverse glycemic effect. For patients with an appropriate cardiovascular indication, the average risk-benefit balance still strongly favors statin treatment. ([CTT Collaboration, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38554713/))

Because most excess diagnoses occurred among participants already near the diagnostic threshold, a small increase after starting a statin should be interpreted in relation to the patient's baseline HbA1c, absolute glucose values, statin intensity, and overall trajectory. ([CTT Collaboration, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38554713/))

## Metformin: the test is measuring treated physiology

Metformin lowers actual glucose rather than distorting its measurement. A meta-analysis of 35 randomized trials estimated an HbA1c reduction of 1.12 percentage points when metformin was used as monotherapy, 0.95 percentage points when added to another oral medication, and 0.60 percentage points when added to insulin. The meta-analysis also found a dose-related effect and reported different average reductions across monotherapy, add-on oral therapy, and add-on insulin settings. ([Hirst et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22275444/))

Metformin reduces hepatic glucose production substantially through suppression of gluconeogenesis, with additional effects on insulin sensitivity and other metabolic pathways. ([Hundal et al., 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11118008/); [Rena et al., 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5552828/))

A fasting glucose, HbA1c, or oral glucose tolerance test performed while someone is taking metformin describes glucose regulation under treatment. It cannot be interpreted as that person's untreated baseline physiology. This is appropriate when assessing treatment response, but it complicates attempts to determine whether the person would still meet diagnostic criteria without the medication. ([Hirst et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22275444/); [Rena et al., 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5552828/))

## GLP-1 receptor agonists and tirzepatide

Semaglutide and liraglutide are GLP-1 receptor agonists. Tirzepatide should not be classified as a pure GLP-1 receptor agonist because it activates both GIP and GLP-1 receptors. These medications lower actual glucose through several mechanisms, including glucose-dependent effects on insulin and glucagon, weight loss, reduced energy intake, and changes in gastrointestinal motility. ([Jalleh et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39418085/); [Urva et al., 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32519795/))

GLP-1 receptor agonists and tirzepatide can slow gastric emptying, particularly early in treatment or after dose escalation. This can reduce early post-meal glucose excursions and delay the glucose peak. The gastric-emptying effect is not uniform. It differs between shorter- and longer-acting drugs, may diminish during sustained treatment through tachyphylaxis, and is smaller in people whose gastric emptying was already slow before treatment. ([Jalleh et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39418085/))

In a randomized crossover trial of 15 people with type 2 diabetes, oral semaglutide lowered fasting and postprandial glucose and delayed gastric emptying during the first post-meal hour. The study was funded by Novo Nordisk, and several authors were company employees and shareholders. Its findings are consistent with broader physiological evidence, but the funding relationship should be considered. ([Dahl et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33710717/))

A mechanistic tirzepatide study found delayed gastric emptying after a single dose, with attenuation after repeated administration and some residual delay during dose escalation in participants with type 2 diabetes. The study was funded by Eli Lilly, and most authors were company employees and shareholders. ([Urva et al., 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32519795/))

An OGTT or post-meal glucose assessment conducted during treatment therefore describes treated glucose physiology. The measured pattern should be interpreted with the exact medication, dose, treatment duration, and timing of the last dose documented. ([Jalleh et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39418085/); [Dahl et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33710717/); [Urva et al., 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32519795/))

## Drugs that can make HbA1c falsely low

Dapsone is one of the clearest medication-related causes of a falsely low HbA1c. It can lower HbA1c through shortened red blood cell survival or hemolysis, which reduces the time available for hemoglobin glycation. It can also cause assay-specific analytical interference through methemoglobinemia. Dapsone is therefore a mixed biological and analytical confounder. A case report found high plasma and home glucose readings together with high fructosamine but a low HbA1c, with the HbA1c reduction related to the dapsone dose. ([Albright et al., 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15259122/); [Unnikrishnan et al., 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3401751/))

A later withdrawal-and-rechallenge report strengthened the causal evidence: HbA1c fell while dapsone was used despite stable glucose, rose after dapsone was discontinued, and fell again when treatment was restarted. The evidence remains based largely on case reports rather than large trials, but the repeated within-person pattern is compelling. ([Lai et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22370290/))

Ribavirin, some antiretroviral regimens, and other drugs associated with hemolysis or altered red blood cell indices can also make HbA1c lower than expected. In a study of 100 adults with HIV and diabetes or fasting hyperglycemia, HbA1c underestimated glucose by an amount corresponding to approximately 29 mg/dL compared with matched controls. Nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor use and greater mean corpuscular volume were important correlates, particularly abacavir use. This finding should not be generalized to every antiretroviral drug or every person living with HIV. ([Kim et al., 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19502538/); [Unnikrishnan et al., 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3401751/))

In people with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease, intravenous iron or erythropoiesis-stimulating treatment can lower HbA1c without a corresponding change in measured glucose. In a small prospective study, HbA1c fell after either treatment even though mean glucose measured through repeated testing and CGM did not materially change. ([Konya et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23758176/))

When HbA1c is unexpectedly low relative to laboratory glucose, finger-stick values, or CGM data, medication-related red blood cell effects should be considered. Concurrent glucose measurements and, where appropriate, fructosamine or glycated albumin can help determine whether the HbA1c is biologically discordant. These alternative markers also have limitations, particularly when albumin concentration or turnover is abnormal, and should be interpreted in the relevant clinical context. ([Albright et al., 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15259122/); [Unnikrishnan et al., 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3401751/); [Konya et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23758176/); [Danese et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25591856/))

## Drugs that interfere with CGM readings

Hydroxyurea can cause falsely elevated readings on particular CGM platforms. In an inpatient study involving older Dexcom G4 and G6 systems, the largest discrepancy between sensor and reference glucose occurred 0.5 to 2 hours after hydroxyurea administration, with clinically important positive bias persisting for several hours. This was analytical interference, not true hyperglycemia. ([Tellez et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33314990/))

Acetaminophen interfered with some older CGM generations, while changes in sensor membranes reduced its effect in some newer systems. High-dose ascorbic acid can cause false-high readings on certain other platforms. These effects cannot be generalized across all CGMs because sensor chemistry and interference protection differ between manufacturers and product generations. Published studies are sparse, and some available validation data are manufacturer-sponsored or remain unpublished. ([Heinemann, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34911382/))

The exact device, generation, dose, market, and current labeling therefore matter as much as the medication name. A statement such as "acetaminophen interferes with CGM" is incomplete unless the sensor platform is specified. ([Heinemann, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34911382/))

## Drugs and solutions that interfere with blood glucose meters

Icodextrin is metabolized to maltose. Certain glucose meters using older glucose dehydrogenase-pyrroloquinoline quinone or glucose dye oxidoreductase chemistries can misread maltose and related sugars as glucose, producing a falsely high result. This can lead to unnecessary insulin administration and severe hypoglycemia. ([Schleis, 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17723085/))

The 2007 review describing this problem was authored by an employee of Octapharma's medical-affairs department, which is a relevant industry affiliation. Separate analytical testing has nevertheless confirmed substantial positive meter bias from maltose and interactions with other factors such as hematocrit. ([Schleis, 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17723085/); [Lyon et al., 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21129345/))

Meters differ by assay chemistry in their susceptibility. A result that conflicts sharply with symptoms, laboratory plasma glucose, or a second compatible method should be confirmed before it is used to administer insulin or correct presumed hypoglycemia. ([Schleis, 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17723085/); [Lyon et al., 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21129345/))

## The "reversible versus real" distinction

Medication-associated dysglycemia is better divided into three questions. ([Aberer et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8157052/); [Albright et al., 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15259122/); [Tellez et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33314990/))

- **Is the glucose abnormality genuine or analytically false?** A steroid-related glucose elevation is genuine. A hydroxyurea-related false-high CGM result may not be. ([Liu et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25402408/); [Tellez et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33314990/))

- **Does the patient need treatment during the exposure?** Genuine, clinically important hyperglycemia may require temporary treatment even if the precipitating medication will later be withdrawn. ([Aberer et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8157052/))

- **Does dysglycemia persist after the medication changes?** Persistence suggests that the drug may have uncovered an underlying susceptibility or that diabetes was already developing independently. ([Aberer et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8157052/); [Shah et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9142341/))


This framework avoids two opposite errors: dismissing clinically important hyperglycemia because it is "only from a medication," and assigning a permanent diabetes diagnosis or indefinite treatment plan without reassessing the patient after a reversible exposure has ended. ([Aberer et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8157052/); [Shah et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9142341/); [Tellez et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33314990/); [Albright et al., 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15259122/))

## Limits and open debates

Glucose can normalize after glucocorticoid withdrawal, but persistent dysglycemia can also occur when treatment has unmasked pre-existing susceptibility or previously unrecognized diabetes. Continued reassessment after dose reduction or withdrawal is therefore recommended. ([Aberer et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8157052/); [Shah et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9142341/))

Most randomized antipsychotic trials are relatively short, while clinically important weight gain and diabetes can develop over much longer treatment periods. Short-term metabolic changes and long-term diabetes incidence answer related but different questions. ([Pillinger et al., 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31860457/); [Smith et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40864439/))

GLP-1 receptor agonists and tirzepatide clearly alter gastric emptying and post-meal glucose physiology. The magnitude and duration of these effects differ between drugs and individuals and can change during sustained treatment. ([Jalleh et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39418085/); [Urva et al., 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32519795/))

CGM and meter interference changes as sensor chemistry and product generations change. Evidence obtained using an older device should not automatically be applied to its successor. The published evidence is also less complete than the manufacturer-held interference data. ([Heinemann, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34911382/))

- Systemic glucocorticoids: hyperglycemia in 32.3 percent and diabetes in 18.6 percent in a meta-analysis of 13 predominantly observational studies. ([Liu et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25402408/))

- Antipsychotics overall: hyperglycemia odds ratio 1.29, fasting glucose increase 0.72 mg/dL, and HbA1c increase 0.04 percentage points versus placebo. Evidence certainty was low for hyperglycemia and very low for fasting glucose and HbA1c. ([Smith et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40864439/))

- Thiazide diuretics: average fasting glucose increase 0.20 mmol/L, approximately 3.6 mg/dL. ([Hall et al., 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32157653/))

- Low- or moderate-intensity statins: new-onset diabetes rate ratio 1.10; mean HbA1c increase 0.06 percentage points in participants without diabetes. ([CTT Collaboration, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38554713/))

- High-intensity statins: new-onset diabetes rate ratio 1.36; mean HbA1c increase 0.08 percentage points in participants without diabetes. ([CTT Collaboration, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38554713/))

- Metformin monotherapy: average HbA1c reduction 1.12 percentage points versus placebo. ([Hirst et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22275444/))

- Dapsone: can produce a falsely low HbA1c through shortened red blood cell survival or hemolysis and can also cause assay-specific analytical interference through methemoglobinemia. ## Practical interpretation

- Record the exact medication, dose, route, start date, time of the most recent dose, dose-escalation history, and planned taper before interpreting glucose testing. ([Aberer et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8157052/); [Heinemann, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34911382/))

- Match glucose testing to the expected drug pattern. After morning prednisone, afternoon or post-meal testing can be more informative than fasting glucose alone. ([Cansu et al., 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30914158/))

- Treat HbA1c cautiously when the medication was started recently. A short exposure can substantially change daily glucose before it materially changes HbA1c. ([Suh and Park, 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5503862/))

- Determine whether the result represents untreated physiology, treated physiology, biological HbA1c distortion, or analytical device interference. ([CTT Collaboration, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38554713/); [Hirst et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22275444/); [Albright et al., 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15259122/); [Heinemann, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34911382/))

- Compare HbA1c with direct glucose measurements whenever the two appear clinically inconsistent. Consider red blood cell turnover, hemolysis, iron or erythropoietic treatment, and medications such as dapsone. ([Albright et al., 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15259122/); [Konya et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23758176/))

- For CGM or meter readings that conflict with symptoms or laboratory glucose, check the exact device's interference profile and confirm the result with a compatible method before making a high-risk treatment decision. ([Heinemann, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34911382/); [Tellez et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33314990/))

- Reassess glucose after a temporary glucose-altering medication is reduced or stopped. A temporary treatment plan should include an explicit plan for retesting and possible de-escalation. ([Aberer et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8157052/); [Shah et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9142341/))

- Before changing a necessary medication because of glucose or HbA1c, confirm whether the abnormality is reproducible and whether it reflects true physiology, biological HbA1c distortion, or analytical interference. The medication's indication and the risks of changing treatment remain part of the decision. ([Aberer et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8157052/); [Smith et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40864439/); [CTT Collaboration, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38554713/); [Heinemann, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34911382/))
