# The Oral Glucose Tolerance Test: Forgotten Gold Standard?

The oral glucose tolerance test is best described as the clinical reference test for **post-challenge dysglycemia**, not as a universal gold standard for metabolic health. It detects impaired glucose tolerance because impaired glucose tolerance is defined by an abnormal 2-hour glucose response to a standardized glucose load. That makes the OGTT uniquely capable of identifying a phenotype that fasting glucose and HbA1c may miss, but it also makes claims of its superior sensitivity partly definitional. ([Diabetes Journals](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S27/163926/2-Diagnosis-and-Classification-of-Diabetes "Diagnosis and Classification of Diabetes: Standards of Care in Diabetes-2026"); [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7585270/ "The Oral Glucose Tolerance Test: 100 Years Later"))

Its central weakness is substantial within-person variability. A person close to the 140 or 200 mg/dL threshold may cross from one diagnostic category to another when retested. These considerations favor targeted OGTT use, with formal confirmation of diabetes-range results in asymptomatic people and repeat testing of consequential borderline findings under standardized conditions, rather than universal replacement of fasting glucose and HbA1c. ([JAMA Network](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/412871 "Short-term Variability in Measures of Glycemia and Implications for the Classification of Diabetes"); [Diabetes Journals](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S27/163926/2-Diagnosis-and-Classification-of-Diabetes "Diagnosis and Classification of Diabetes: Standards of Care in Diabetes-2026"))

## How the conventional OGTT is performed

For diagnosing diabetes in nonpregnant adults, the conventional test uses 75 g of anhydrous glucose after a fast of at least 8 hours with no caloric intake, with fasting and 2-hour plasma glucose measurements. A routine 1-hour measurement is not required under the conventional American Diabetes Association diagnostic framework. One-hour measurements are used in some pregnancy protocols and have recently been proposed for earlier detection of dysglycemia in high-risk nonpregnant adults. ([Diabetes Journals](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S27/163926/2-Diagnosis-and-Classification-of-Diabetes "Diagnosis and Classification of Diabetes: Standards of Care in Diabetes-2026"); [Diabetes Journals](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S321/163918/15-Management-of-Diabetes-in-Pregnancy-Standards "Management of Diabetes in Pregnancy: Standards of Care in Diabetes-2026"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38458916/ "International Diabetes Federation Position Statement on the 1-hour post-load plasma glucose"))

Under current American Diabetes Association criteria, the 2-hour result is categorized as follows: ([Diabetes Journals](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S27/163926/2-Diagnosis-and-Classification-of-Diabetes "Diagnosis and Classification of Diabetes: Standards of Care in Diabetes-2026"))

- Normal 2-hour glucose: below 140 mg/dL. ([Diabetes Journals](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S27/163926/2-Diagnosis-and-Classification-of-Diabetes "Diagnosis and Classification of Diabetes: Standards of Care in Diabetes-2026"))

- Impaired glucose tolerance: 140 to 199 mg/dL. ([Diabetes Journals](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S27/163926/2-Diagnosis-and-Classification-of-Diabetes "Diagnosis and Classification of Diabetes: Standards of Care in Diabetes-2026"))

- Diabetes: 200 mg/dL or higher. ([Diabetes Journals](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S27/163926/2-Diagnosis-and-Classification-of-Diabetes "Diagnosis and Classification of Diabetes: Standards of Care in Diabetes-2026"))


An HbA1c of 5.7 to 6.4 percent or fasting glucose of 100 to 125 mg/dL defines other forms of prediabetes. In an asymptomatic person without unequivocal hyperglycemia, a diabetes-range result should be confirmed by a second abnormal result from the same test or a different diagnostic test. ([Diabetes Journals](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S27/163926/2-Diagnosis-and-Classification-of-Diabetes "Diagnosis and Classification of Diabetes: Standards of Care in Diabetes-2026"))

## What the OGTT measures that fasting glucose and HbA1c do not

Fasting glucose measures glucose regulation in a basal, fasting state. HbA1c estimates average glycemic exposure over approximately the preceding 2 to 3 months, although it is also influenced by red-cell lifespan and other nonglycemic factors. The OGTT asks a different question: how effectively can the body dispose of a standardized glucose load over time? Current diagnostic guidance explicitly recognizes that fasting glucose, HbA1c, and 2-hour glucose measure different aspects of glucose metabolism and consequently identify partly different groups of people. ([Diabetes Journals](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S27/163926/2-Diagnosis-and-Classification-of-Diabetes "Diagnosis and Classification of Diabetes: Standards of Care in Diabetes-2026"))

The 2-hour response integrates several processes, including intestinal glucose absorption, incretin and insulin secretion, suppression of hepatic glucose output, and peripheral glucose uptake. It is therefore more informative than a fasting measurement about post-challenge glucose regulation. But calling it the “most physiologically informative glucose test available” is too broad: research methods such as glucose clamps provide more specific measurements of insulin sensitivity. The OGTT is a standardized glucose challenge, not a simulation of a typical mixed meal. ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7585270/ "The Oral Glucose Tolerance Test: 100 Years Later"))

## How often does it find abnormalities when fasting glucose and HbA1c are normal?

The claim that 30 to 40 percent of people with **both** normal fasting glucose and normal HbA1c have impaired glucose tolerance is not supported as a general-population estimate. ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7408667/ "Limited Agreement between Classifications of Diabetes and Prediabetes Resulting from the OGTT, Hemoglobin A1c, and Fasting Glucose Tests in 7,412 U.S. Adults"))

In an analysis of 7,412 randomly selected U.S. adults from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, approximately 93 percent of people classified as normal by both fasting glucose and HbA1c were also classified as normal by the OGTT. Thus, about 7 percent were reclassified by the OGTT as having either prediabetes or diabetes. That 7 percent was not restricted specifically to isolated impaired glucose tolerance, so it should not be quoted as the precise prevalence of IGT. ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7408667/ "Limited Agreement between Classifications of Diabetes and Prediabetes Resulting from the OGTT, Hemoglobin A1c, and Fasting Glucose Tests in 7,412 U.S. Adults"))

Discordance was considerably larger in other diagnostic categories. Among people classified as prediabetic by both fasting glucose and HbA1c, only about one-third were also classified as prediabetic by the OGTT. Conversely, among those with diabetes-range OGTT results, concordance with HbA1c and fasting glucose was only 34 and 44 percent, respectively. These findings show that the tests select different populations, but because the study treated a single OGTT as the reference, they do not establish that every disagreement represented a true error by fasting glucose or HbA1c. ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7408667/ "Limited Agreement between Classifications of Diabetes and Prediabetes Resulting from the OGTT, Hemoglobin A1c, and Fasting Glucose Tests in 7,412 U.S. Adults"))

Much higher figures arise when “normal” refers to only one marker. In the LIFE-Adult study, 35.6 percent of participants with a normal HbA1c had either impaired fasting glucose **or** impaired glucose tolerance, and another 1.8 percent had diabetes according to fasting or 2-hour glucose. This does not mean that 35.6 percent had isolated IGT despite both normal HbA1c and fasting glucose. ([Wiley Online Library](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/edm2.30 "Prevalence of moderately increased albuminuria among individuals with normal HbA1c level but impaired glucose tolerance"))

A 2025 Taiwanese cohort provides more direct evidence about omitting the OGTT. Of 1,935 participants, 156, or about 8.1 percent, would have been placed in a lower glycemic category if diagnosis relied only on fasting glucose and HbA1c. This group included people downgraded from diabetes to prediabetes as well as people downgraded from prediabetes to normoglycemia, so it was not an isolated-IGT cohort. ([Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-03988-4 "Diagnosis of diabetes without oral glucose tolerance test is associated with increased mortality in older adults"))

The practical conclusion from these studies is that the apparent miss rate depends strongly on population selection, age, underlying risk and the definition of “normal.” A 30 to 40 percent figure can occur when normality is defined by HbA1c alone, but the studies reviewed here do not support that estimate for unselected adults who have both normal fasting glucose and normal HbA1c. ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7408667/ "Limited Agreement between Classifications of Diabetes and Prediabetes Resulting from the OGTT, Hemoglobin A1c, and Fasting Glucose Tests in 7,412 U.S. Adults"); [Wiley Online Library](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/edm2.30 "Prevalence of moderately increased albuminuria among individuals with normal HbA1c level but impaired glucose tolerance"); [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-03988-4 "Diagnosis of diabetes without oral glucose tolerance test is associated with increased mortality in older adults"))

## Reproducibility is the OGTT’s central weakness

One well-characterized reproducibility study is a nonrandom NHANES III repeat-examination substudy. Fasting glucose and HbA1c were measured twice in 685 fasting adults, while the repeated 2-hour OGTT analysis included 312 participants aged 40 to 74. The examinations occurred approximately two weeks apart. The within-person coefficient of variation was: ([JAMA Network](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/412871 "Short-term Variability in Measures of Glycemia and Implications for the Classification of Diabetes"))

- 16.7 percent for 2-hour glucose (95 percent CI 15.0 to 18.3). ([JAMA Network](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/412871 "Short-term Variability in Measures of Glycemia and Implications for the Classification of Diabetes"))

- 5.7 percent for fasting glucose (95 percent CI 5.3 to 6.1). ([JAMA Network](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/412871 "Short-term Variability in Measures of Glycemia and Implications for the Classification of Diabetes"))

- 3.6 percent for HbA1c (95 percent CI 3.2 to 4.0). ([JAMA Network](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/412871 "Short-term Variability in Measures of Glycemia and Implications for the Classification of Diabetes"))


Thus, the 2-hour measurement was approximately three times as variable as fasting glucose and more than four times as variable as HbA1c. These coefficients describe population-level short-term variability and should not be interpreted as simple individual error margins. ([JAMA Network](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/412871 "Short-term Variability in Measures of Glycemia and Implications for the Classification of Diabetes"))

Among participants with fasting glucose of 126 mg/dL or higher on the first measurement, 70.4 percent (95 percent CI 49.8 to 86.2) remained at or above that threshold when retested. At the 2-hour glucose threshold of 140 mg/dL, 72.0 percent (95 percent CI 62.1 to 80.5) remained at or above the threshold on repeat testing. This means that a substantial minority crossed a diagnostic boundary on repeat testing, particularly when the first result was close to the cut-point. ([JAMA Network](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/412871 "Short-term Variability in Measures of Glycemia and Implications for the Classification of Diabetes"))

Preparation also matters. Current guidance recommends a mixed eating pattern containing at least 150 g of carbohydrate per day for the three days preceding the test. Recent carbohydrate restriction can exaggerate the post-load response and produce a potentially false-positive result. Acute illness, unusual physical activity and medications that affect glucose can also make a single test less representative of usual physiology. ([Diabetes Journals](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S27/163926/2-Diagnosis-and-Classification-of-Diabetes "Diagnosis and Classification of Diabetes: Standards of Care in Diabetes-2026"); [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7585270/ "The Oral Glucose Tolerance Test: 100 Years Later"))

Poorer reproducibility does not make the OGTT meaningless. It means that the underlying post-challenge response is biologically variable and that converting a continuous measurement into categories at 140 and 200 mg/dL creates unstable classifications near those boundaries. A clearly abnormal result is more persuasive than a value barely above a threshold. In the absence of unequivocal hyperglycemia, diabetes-range results require formal confirmation; repeating a borderline IGT result under standardized conditions is prudent when the classification would materially affect a consequential decision. ([JAMA Network](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/412871 "Short-term Variability in Measures of Glycemia and Implications for the Classification of Diabetes"); [Diabetes Journals](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S27/163926/2-Diagnosis-and-Classification-of-Diabetes "Diagnosis and Classification of Diabetes: Standards of Care in Diabetes-2026"))

## Cardiovascular risk associated with impaired glucose tolerance

The prognostic evidence for 2-hour glucose is substantial. ([JAMA Network](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/647267 "Glucose Tolerance and Cardiovascular Mortality: Comparison of Fasting and 2-Hour Diagnostic Criteria"); [BMJ](https://www.bmj.com/content/370/bmj.m2297 "Association between prediabetes and risk of all-cause mortality and cardiovascular disease: updated meta-analysis"))

The DECODE collaboration pooled 10 European prospective cohorts containing 22,514 adults aged 30 to 89 years, with a median follow-up of 8.8 years among those without previously diagnosed diabetes. In a model containing both fasting and 2-hour glucose categories, impaired glucose tolerance was associated with: ([JAMA Network](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/647267 "Glucose Tolerance and Cardiovascular Mortality: Comparison of Fasting and 2-Hour Diagnostic Criteria"))

- Cardiovascular mortality: hazard ratio 1.32 (95 percent CI 1.12 to 1.56). ([JAMA Network](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/647267 "Glucose Tolerance and Cardiovascular Mortality: Comparison of Fasting and 2-Hour Diagnostic Criteria"))

- Coronary heart disease mortality: hazard ratio 1.27 (95 percent CI 1.01 to 1.58). ([JAMA Network](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/647267 "Glucose Tolerance and Cardiovascular Mortality: Comparison of Fasting and 2-Hour Diagnostic Criteria"))

- All-cause mortality: hazard ratio 1.37 (95 percent CI 1.25 to 1.51). ([JAMA Network](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/647267 "Glucose Tolerance and Cardiovascular Mortality: Comparison of Fasting and 2-Hour Diagnostic Criteria"))


By comparison, impaired fasting glucose had hazard ratios of 1.01 (95 percent CI 0.84 to 1.22) for cardiovascular mortality and 1.03 (95 percent CI 0.93 to 1.14) for all-cause mortality in the same model. In this study, impaired fasting glucose was defined as fasting plasma glucose of 110 to below 126 mg/dL, not the current American Diabetes Association range of 100 to 125 mg/dL. That difference limits direct comparison with the broader current ADA category. The investigators concluded that 2-hour glucose classification added mortality information beyond fasting glucose. ([JAMA Network](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/647267 "Glucose Tolerance and Cardiovascular Mortality: Comparison of Fasting and 2-Hour Diagnostic Criteria"); [Diabetes Journals](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S27/163926/2-Diagnosis-and-Classification-of-Diabetes "Diagnosis and Classification of Diabetes: Standards of Care in Diabetes-2026"))

A 2020 systematic review and meta-analysis encompassing 129 studies and more than 10 million participants similarly found that impaired glucose tolerance was associated with a relative risk of approximately 1.23 for composite cardiovascular disease and 1.25 for all-cause mortality compared with normoglycemia. These are moderate relative increases, not evidence that every person with a single abnormal OGTT is destined to develop cardiovascular disease. ([BMJ](https://www.bmj.com/content/370/bmj.m2297 "Association between prediabetes and risk of all-cause mortality and cardiovascular disease: updated meta-analysis"))

The DECODE findings are observational and cannot establish that post-load glucose itself causes cardiovascular disease independently of the wider metabolic phenotype accompanying IGT. The models adjusted for age, sex, study center, body mass index, blood pressure, total cholesterol and smoking, but residual confounding and differences in glucose measurement among cohorts remain possible. The findings do, however, show that the phenotype identified by the OGTT carries prognostic information that fasting glucose alone can miss. ([JAMA Network](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/647267 "Glucose Tolerance and Cardiovascular Mortality: Comparison of Fasting and 2-Hour Diagnostic Criteria"))

## Outcomes among people underdiagnosed without the OGTT

Within the evidence reviewed here, direct outcome data restricted specifically to people who are normal by both fasting glucose and HbA1c but have isolated IGT are limited; the 2025 Taiwanese cohort combined several forms of underclassification rather than isolating that subgroup. ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7408667/ "Limited Agreement between Classifications of Diabetes and Prediabetes Resulting from the OGTT, Hemoglobin A1c, and Fasting Glucose Tests in 7,412 U.S. Adults"); [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-03988-4 "Diagnosis of diabetes without oral glucose tolerance test is associated with increased mortality in older adults"))

In the 2025 Taiwanese cohort, all-cause mortality was 8.3 percent in participants whose glycemic category would have been underestimated without an OGTT, compared with 3.6 percent among correctly classified participants. The crude hazard ratio was 2.54 (95 percent CI 1.40 to 4.61). However, the misclassified group was older and had a worse cardiometabolic risk profile. After adjustment that included age and the full set of measured confounders, the overall hazard ratio was 1.71 (95 percent CI 0.90 to 3.25) and was not statistically significant. In participants aged 60 or older, the fully adjusted mortality hazard ratio remained 2.04 (p = 0.036). ([Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-03988-4 "Diagnosis of diabetes without oral glucose tolerance test is associated with increased mortality in older adults"))

This study is relevant because it identifies a higher-risk group that would be underclassified without the OGTT, but it has important limitations. It involved one Taiwanese volunteer cohort, assessed all-cause rather than specifically cardiovascular mortality, and combined several forms of diagnostic underclassification rather than isolating normal fasting glucose plus normal HbA1c plus IGT. It does not establish that omission of the OGTT itself caused the higher mortality. Its findings are more directly relevant to targeted OGTT use in older or metabolically high-risk adults than to universal screening. ([Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-03988-4 "Diagnosis of diabetes without oral glucose tolerance test is associated with increased mortality in older adults"))

## Does diagnosing and treating IGT improve outcomes?

In high-risk adults like those enrolled in the Diabetes Prevention Program, identifying IGT is actionable for preventing or delaying diabetes. In that trial, 3,234 high-risk, overweight or obese adults with elevated fasting glucose and impaired glucose tolerance were randomized to intensive lifestyle intervention, metformin or placebo. Over an average of 2.8 years, lifestyle intervention reduced diabetes incidence by 58 percent (95 percent CI 48 to 66) and metformin reduced it by 31 percent (95 percent CI 17 to 43) compared with placebo. ([New England Journal of Medicine](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa012512 "Reduction in the Incidence of Type 2 Diabetes with Lifestyle Intervention or Metformin"))

Whether this produces fewer major cardiovascular events is less certain. After a median follow-up of approximately 21 years, neither the original lifestyle intervention nor metformin significantly reduced major cardiovascular events in the Diabetes Prevention Program Outcomes Study. The hazard ratio was 1.14 (95 percent CI 0.87 to 1.50) for lifestyle intervention versus placebo and 1.03 (95 percent CI 0.78 to 1.37) for metformin versus placebo, despite long-term reductions in diabetes incidence. Provision of group lifestyle intervention to all participants, treatment crossover and widespread use of statins and antihypertensive agents may have diluted between-group differences. ([AHA Journals](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.121.056756 "Effects of Long-term Metformin and Lifestyle Interventions on Cardiovascular Events in the Diabetes Prevention Program and Its Outcome Study"))

The cluster-randomized Da Qing Diabetes Prevention Outcome Study produced a more favorable result. After 30 years, lifestyle intervention in Chinese adults with impaired glucose tolerance was associated with fewer cardiovascular events, with a hazard ratio of 0.74 (95 percent CI 0.59 to 0.92); fewer cardiovascular deaths, with a hazard ratio of 0.67 (95 percent CI 0.48 to 0.94); and fewer all-cause deaths, with a hazard ratio of 0.74 (95 percent CI 0.61 to 0.89). Diabetes onset was delayed by a median of 3.96 years (95 percent CI 1.25 to 6.67). ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31036503/ "Morbidity and mortality after lifestyle intervention for people with impaired glucose tolerance: 30-year results of the Da Qing Diabetes Prevention Outcome Study"))

Across trials of people with prediabetes or type 2 diabetes as a whole, the conclusion is more cautious. A 2022 meta-analysis of 11 randomized studies found no significant reduction in cardiovascular mortality, with a relative risk of 0.99 (95 percent CI 0.79 to 1.23), or all-cause mortality, with a relative risk of 0.93 (95 percent CI 0.85 to 1.03), from intensive lifestyle intervention compared with usual care. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36318674/ "Long-term Effect of Lifestyle Interventions on Cardiovascular and All-Cause Mortality in Subjects With Prediabetes and Type 2 Diabetes"))

A newer 2026 post-hoc analysis of the Diabetes Prevention Program and Da Qing cohorts found that participants who returned to normal glucose regulation had a substantially lower long-term risk of cardiovascular death or hospitalization for heart failure. In the U.S. cohort, remission after one year was associated with an adjusted hazard ratio of 0.41 (95 percent CI 0.20 to 0.84). Because remission was achieved rather than randomly assigned, this result is prognostic and does not by itself prove that remission caused the cardiovascular benefit. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41397402/ "Prediabetes remission and cardiovascular morbidity and mortality"))

Taken together, these studies support a distinction: in selected high-risk populations, finding IGT identifies people in whom diabetes can often be delayed, but the cited trials did not directly test universal OGTT screening as a population strategy for reducing cardiovascular events. ([New England Journal of Medicine](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa012512 "Reduction in the Incidence of Type 2 Diabetes with Lifestyle Intervention or Metformin"); [AHA Journals](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.121.056756 "Effects of Long-term Metformin and Lifestyle Interventions on Cardiovascular Events in the Diabetes Prevention Program and Its Outcome Study"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31036503/ "Morbidity and mortality after lifestyle intervention for people with impaired glucose tolerance: 30-year results of the Da Qing Diabetes Prevention Outcome Study"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36318674/ "Long-term Effect of Lifestyle Interventions on Cardiovascular and All-Cause Mortality in Subjects With Prediabetes and Type 2 Diabetes"))

## Who is most likely to benefit from an OGTT?

Routine screening with fasting glucose or HbA1c is reasonable because these tests are easier to administer and are more reproducible. The OGTT becomes more useful when the clinical value of detecting post-challenge dysglycemia is high enough to justify the inconvenience and variability. ([Diabetes Journals](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S27/163926/2-Diagnosis-and-Classification-of-Diabetes "Diagnosis and Classification of Diabetes: Standards of Care in Diabetes-2026"); [JAMA Network](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/412871 "Short-term Variability in Measures of Glycemia and Implications for the Classification of Diabetes"))

Targeted applications supported by current guidance or the evidence reviewed here include: ([Diabetes Journals](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S27/163926/2-Diagnosis-and-Classification-of-Diabetes "Diagnosis and Classification of Diabetes: Standards of Care in Diabetes-2026"); [Diabetes Journals](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S321/163918/15-Management-of-Diabetes-in-Pregnancy-Standards "Management of Diabetes in Pregnancy: Standards of Care in Diabetes-2026"); [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-03988-4 "Diagnosis of diabetes without oral glucose tolerance test is associated with increased mortality in older adults"))

- **Pregnancy and previous gestational diabetes.** Pregnancy has separate OGTT protocols and thresholds. After gestational diabetes, current guidance recommends a 75-g OGTT at 4 to 12 weeks postpartum because HbA1c is less sensitive in this period. ([Diabetes Journals](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S321/163918/15-Management-of-Diabetes-in-Pregnancy-Standards "Management of Diabetes in Pregnancy: Standards of Care in Diabetes-2026"))

- **High-risk people with normal or nearly normal routine markers.** Observational evidence suggests potential value in older adults and in people with substantial metabolic risk whose fasting glucose and HbA1c do not fully explain the clinical concern. ([Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-03988-4 "Diagnosis of diabetes without oral glucose tolerance test is associated with increased mortality in older adults"))

- **Discordant results.** Because fasting glucose, HbA1c and 2-hour glucose identify different populations, an OGTT can clarify cases in which the markers disagree or HbA1c may be unreliable. ([Diabetes Journals](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S27/163926/2-Diagnosis-and-Classification-of-Diabetes "Diagnosis and Classification of Diabetes: Standards of Care in Diabetes-2026"))

- **Situations in which post-meal dysglycemia is specifically suspected.** A standardized challenge can reveal reduced glucose tolerance even when basal glucose remains relatively preserved. ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7585270/ "The Oral Glucose Tolerance Test: 100 Years Later"))


A value just above a threshold should not be treated as an immutable biological label. Repeating the test under standardized conditions is particularly reasonable when the result would change treatment, pregnancy management or another consequential decision. Formal confirmation is required for diabetes-range results in the absence of unequivocal hyperglycemia; repeating borderline IGT is a prudent clinical step rather than a universal diagnostic rule. ([JAMA Network](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/412871 "Short-term Variability in Measures of Glycemia and Implications for the Classification of Diabetes"); [Diabetes Journals](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S27/163926/2-Diagnosis-and-Classification-of-Diabetes "Diagnosis and Classification of Diabetes: Standards of Care in Diabetes-2026"))

## Could continuous glucose monitoring replace the OGTT?

Continuous glucose monitoring and the OGTT provide different kinds of information. The OGTT measures the response to the same standardized challenge in every person. CGM records glucose during free-living eating, sleep and activity over several days, providing far more observations but under less standardized conditions. ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7585270/ "The Oral Glucose Tolerance Test: 100 Years Later"); [Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/19322968261426384 "Differences in Continuous Glucose Monitoring Metrics Between Prediabetes and Normoglycemia"))

A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis included 16 studies, with 10 studies and 1,657 participants in the quantitative synthesis. Compared with normoglycemia, prediabetes was associated with: ([Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/19322968261426384 "Differences in Continuous Glucose Monitoring Metrics Between Prediabetes and Normoglycemia"))

- Mean amplitude of glycemic excursions higher by approximately 9.5 mg/dL (95 percent CI 4.31 to 15.31). ([Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/19322968261426384 "Differences in Continuous Glucose Monitoring Metrics Between Prediabetes and Normoglycemia"))

- Time above 140 mg/dL higher by 5.68 percentage points (95 percent CI 1.04 to 10.32). ([Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/19322968261426384 "Differences in Continuous Glucose Monitoring Metrics Between Prediabetes and Normoglycemia"))

- Mean 24-hour glucose higher by 7.91 mg/dL (95 percent CI 6.27 to 9.55). ([Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/19322968261426384 "Differences in Continuous Glucose Monitoring Metrics Between Prediabetes and Normoglycemia"))


The paper reports the mean-amplitude point estimate as 9.41 mg/dL in its abstract and 9.81 mg/dL in the main results, with the same confidence interval; approximately 9.5 mg/dL avoids false precision. Between-study heterogeneity was substantial for mean amplitude of glycemic excursions (I² = 77 percent) and time above 140 mg/dL (I² = 90 percent), but low for mean 24-hour glucose (I² = 10 percent). The authors concluded that CGM can distinguish prediabetes from normoglycemia at the group level and may complement conventional testing, but they also stated that prospective studies are needed to determine whether the measurements predict progression to diabetes. ([Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/19322968261426384 "Differences in Continuous Glucose Monitoring Metrics Between Prediabetes and Normoglycemia"))

This is not yet a head-to-head demonstration that CGM diagnoses early dysglycemia better than the OGTT. The included studies generally compared CGM profiles between groups whose glycemic status had already been defined by conventional testing. Group-average differences do not automatically provide a validated diagnostic threshold for an individual patient. Current American Diabetes Association guidance explicitly states that evidence is insufficient to use CGM to screen for or diagnose prediabetes or diabetes. ([Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/19322968261426384 "Differences in Continuous Glucose Monitoring Metrics Between Prediabetes and Normoglycemia"); [Diabetes Journals](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S27/163926/2-Diagnosis-and-Classification-of-Diabetes "Diagnosis and Classification of Diabetes: Standards of Care in Diabetes-2026"))

A multi-day CGM recording produces more data than one OGTT, but “more data” is not the same as more validated diagnostic information. CGM captures free-living excursions that an OGTT is not designed to sample. Conversely, an OGTT measures reserve under a fixed challenge, whereas a person’s CGM profile is influenced by what and when that person eats and how active they are during the recording period. ([Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/19322968261426384 "Differences in Continuous Glucose Monitoring Metrics Between Prediabetes and Normoglycemia"); [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7585270/ "The Oral Glucose Tolerance Test: 100 Years Later"))

At present, CGM is best regarded as complementary. Replacing the OGTT would require standardized diagnostic metrics, validated cut-points, evidence of individual-level reproducibility and prospective evidence that CGM-based classification predicts diabetes and cardiovascular outcomes at least as well as laboratory testing. ([Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/19322968261426384 "Differences in Continuous Glucose Monitoring Metrics Between Prediabetes and Normoglycemia"); [Diabetes Journals](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S27/163926/2-Diagnosis-and-Classification-of-Diabetes "Diagnosis and Classification of Diabetes: Standards of Care in Diabetes-2026"))

## The emerging 1-hour OGTT

The International Diabetes Federation issued a 2024 position statement proposing a shorter 1-hour OGTT for high-risk adults. It recommended: ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38458916/ "International Diabetes Federation Position Statement on the 1-hour post-load plasma glucose"))

- One-hour glucose of 155 mg/dL or higher for intermediate hyperglycemia. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38458916/ "International Diabetes Federation Position Statement on the 1-hour post-load plasma glucose"))

- One-hour glucose of 209 mg/dL or higher for type 2 diabetes, with repeat testing to confirm the diagnosis. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38458916/ "International Diabetes Federation Position Statement on the 1-hour post-load plasma glucose"))


The proposal is based on cohort evidence suggesting that one-hour glucose may identify risk earlier than conventional fasting or 2-hour criteria. It is an expert position statement rather than evidence from a randomized screening trial, and its thresholds have not replaced the conventional 2-hour criteria in the current American Diabetes Association framework. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38458916/ "International Diabetes Federation Position Statement on the 1-hour post-load plasma glucose"); [Diabetes Journals](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S27/163926/2-Diagnosis-and-Classification-of-Diabetes "Diagnosis and Classification of Diabetes: Standards of Care in Diabetes-2026"))

This development complicates the idea that the OGTT has simply been abandoned. The test may instead be evolving toward a shorter, targeted protocol, although clinical adoption will depend on further validation and evidence that acting on the newly identified group improves outcomes. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38458916/ "International Diabetes Federation Position Statement on the 1-hour post-load plasma glucose"))

## Funding and evidence limitations

The large DECODE analysis disclosed that its lead author was supported by a fellowship from Novo Nordisk. This does not invalidate the results, but it is a relevant financial relationship and should be considered alongside the study’s observational design. ([JAMA Network](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/647267 "Glucose Tolerance and Cardiovascular Mortality: Comparison of Fasting and 2-Hour Diagnostic Criteria"))

The 2025 Taiwanese mortality study reported funding from Taiwan’s National Science and Technology Council and university hospitals, and its authors declared no competing interests. ([Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-03988-4 "Diagnosis of diabetes without oral glucose tolerance test is associated with increased mortality in older adults"))

Much of the evidence reviewed here consists of cohort associations, diagnostic cross-classification studies and diabetes-prevention trials in selected high-risk populations. These sources support the prognostic relevance of post-challenge dysglycemia, but they do not directly establish that universal OGTT screening is superior to targeted testing in clinical outcomes, cost-effectiveness or patient burden. ([JAMA Network](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/647267 "Glucose Tolerance and Cardiovascular Mortality: Comparison of Fasting and 2-Hour Diagnostic Criteria"); [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7408667/ "Limited Agreement between Classifications of Diabetes and Prediabetes Resulting from the OGTT, Hemoglobin A1c, and Fasting Glucose Tests in 7,412 U.S. Adults"); [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-03988-4 "Diagnosis of diabetes without oral glucose tolerance test is associated with increased mortality in older adults"); [New England Journal of Medicine](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa012512 "Reduction in the Incidence of Type 2 Diabetes with Lifestyle Intervention or Metformin"); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31036503/ "Morbidity and mortality after lifestyle intervention for people with impaired glucose tolerance: 30-year results of the Da Qing Diabetes Prevention Outcome Study"))

## Quick reference values

- Conventional nonpregnant 75-g OGTT: 2-hour glucose below 140 mg/dL is normal, 140 to 199 mg/dL is IGT, and 200 mg/dL or higher is diabetes, requiring confirmation by a second abnormal result in an asymptomatic person without unequivocal hyperglycemia. ([Diabetes Journals](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S27/163926/2-Diagnosis-and-Classification-of-Diabetes "Diagnosis and Classification of Diabetes: Standards of Care in Diabetes-2026"))

- Normal fasting glucose **and** normal HbA1c in a U.S. general-population study: approximately 93 percent also normal by OGTT; approximately 7 percent reclassified as prediabetes or diabetes. ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7408667/ "Limited Agreement between Classifications of Diabetes and Prediabetes Resulting from the OGTT, Hemoglobin A1c, and Fasting Glucose Tests in 7,412 U.S. Adults"))

- Normal HbA1c alone in the LIFE-Adult cohort: 35.6 percent had IFG or IGT and 1.8 percent had diabetes by fasting or 2-hour glucose. ([Wiley Online Library](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/edm2.30 "Prevalence of moderately increased albuminuria among individuals with normal HbA1c level but impaired glucose tolerance"))

- Within-person coefficient of variation in the NHANES repeat-examination substudy: 2-hour glucose 16.7 percent in 312 participants; fasting glucose 5.7 percent and HbA1c 3.6 percent in 685 participants. ([JAMA Network](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/412871 "Short-term Variability in Measures of Glycemia and Implications for the Classification of Diabetes"))

- IGT in the DECODE analysis: cardiovascular mortality hazard ratio 1.32 (95 percent CI 1.12 to 1.56) and all-cause mortality hazard ratio 1.37 (95 percent CI 1.25 to 1.51) after accounting for fasting-glucose category. ([JAMA Network](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/647267 "Glucose Tolerance and Cardiovascular Mortality: Comparison of Fasting and 2-Hour Diagnostic Criteria"))

- CGM meta-analysis: prediabetes was associated with a 7.91 mg/dL higher mean 24-hour glucose and approximately 9.5 mg/dL higher mean amplitude of excursions, but diagnostic replacement has not been established. ([Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/19322968261426384 "Differences in Continuous Glucose Monitoring Metrics Between Prediabetes and Normoglycemia"))


## Practical interpretation

- **Claim 1 is partly correct but overstated.** The OGTT is uniquely informative about response to a standardized glucose challenge and detects a prognostically important phenotype. It is not the most physiologically informative metabolic test in every sense, and the claim that it misses 30 to 40 percent of all people normal by both fasting glucose and HbA1c is not supported in general-population data. ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7585270/ "The Oral Glucose Tolerance Test: 100 Years Later"); [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7408667/ "Limited Agreement between Classifications of Diabetes and Prediabetes Resulting from the OGTT, Hemoglobin A1c, and Fasting Glucose Tests in 7,412 U.S. Adults"); [JAMA Network](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/647267 "Glucose Tolerance and Cardiovascular Mortality: Comparison of Fasting and 2-Hour Diagnostic Criteria"))

- **Claim 2 identifies a real limitation but goes too far.** A population-level within-person coefficient of variation of 16.7 percent makes a single borderline OGTT classification unstable. It does not erase the test’s population-level prognostic value or its ability to detect post-challenge dysglycemia. ([JAMA Network](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/412871 "Short-term Variability in Measures of Glycemia and Implications for the Classification of Diabetes"); [JAMA Network](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/647267 "Glucose Tolerance and Cardiovascular Mortality: Comparison of Fasting and 2-Hour Diagnostic Criteria"); [BMJ](https://www.bmj.com/content/370/bmj.m2297 "Association between prediabetes and risk of all-cause mortality and cardiovascular disease: updated meta-analysis"))

- **Claim 3 is supported by current guidance and the evidence reviewed here.** The balance of diagnostic yield, burden and reproducibility favors pregnancy-related testing, high-risk or older adults, discordant results and circumstances in which routine markers may be insufficient. ([Diabetes Journals](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S321/163918/15-Management-of-Diabetes-in-Pregnancy-Standards "Management of Diabetes in Pregnancy: Standards of Care in Diabetes-2026"); [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-03988-4 "Diagnosis of diabetes without oral glucose tolerance test is associated with increased mortality in older adults"); [Diabetes Journals](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S27/163926/2-Diagnosis-and-Classification-of-Diabetes "Diagnosis and Classification of Diabetes: Standards of Care in Diabetes-2026"); [JAMA Network](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/412871 "Short-term Variability in Measures of Glycemia and Implications for the Classification of Diabetes"))

- **Claim 4 is plausible but not currently established.** CGM captures more free-living information and can distinguish prediabetes from normoglycemia at the group level, but validated diagnostic thresholds and evidence of superior outcome prediction are still lacking. ([Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/19322968261426384 "Differences in Continuous Glucose Monitoring Metrics Between Prediabetes and Normoglycemia"); [Diabetes Journals](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S27/163926/2-Diagnosis-and-Classification-of-Diabetes "Diagnosis and Classification of Diabetes: Standards of Care in Diabetes-2026"))


The OGTT is therefore neither a forgotten universal gold standard nor an obsolete historical test. It remains the reference method for identifying impaired glucose tolerance, but its most evidence-based future is likely **selective, standardized and confirmatory use**, potentially supplemented by CGM but not yet replaced by it. ([Diabetes Journals](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S27/163926/2-Diagnosis-and-Classification-of-Diabetes "Diagnosis and Classification of Diabetes: Standards of Care in Diabetes-2026"); [JAMA Network](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/412871 "Short-term Variability in Measures of Glycemia and Implications for the Classification of Diabetes"); [Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/19322968261426384 "Differences in Continuous Glucose Monitoring Metrics Between Prediabetes and Normoglycemia"))
