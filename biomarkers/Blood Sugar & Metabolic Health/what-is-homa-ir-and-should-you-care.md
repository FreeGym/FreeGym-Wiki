# What Is HOMA-IR and Should You Care?

HOMA-IR is a fasting estimate of insulin resistance calculated from glucose and insulin. By combining the two measurements, it can show when fasting insulin is relatively high for the concurrent glucose value, a pattern compatible with compensatory insulin secretion within the HOMA model. It is not a direct measurement of insulin sensitivity, does not describe the body's full response to a meal, and has no universally accepted diagnostic cutoff. ([Matthews et al., 1985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3899825/); [Wallace et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.2337/diacare.27.6.1487); [Lee et al., 2016](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0163424); [Gayoso-Diz et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24131857/))

The best-supported interpretation is that HOMA-IR is a useful adjunct - not a diagnosis. In a 2026 comparative cohort, fasting-insulin indexes performed better than indexes that did not measure insulin, but dynamic oral-glucose-tolerance-test indexes performed better overall, and clamp-based measurement is more direct when available. ([Vazquez et al., 2026](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/111/8/2251/8512919); [Sacks et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/clinchem/article/69/8/808/7226244))

## What HOMA-IR is and is not

The Homeostasis Model Assessment was introduced in 1985 as a mathematical model of the fasting feedback relationship between glucose production, insulin secretion, and insulin action. The original investigators compared model-derived insulin resistance with the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp, establishing HOMA as a scalable surrogate rather than a replacement for direct physiological measurement. ([Matthews et al., 1985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3899825/))

The commonly used equation is technically **HOMA1-IR**. ([Wallace et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.2337/diacare.27.6.1487))

**HOMA1-IR = (fasting glucose in mg/dL × fasting insulin in mIU/L) / 405** ([Wallace et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.2337/diacare.27.6.1487))

When glucose is reported in mmol/L, the denominator is 22.5. HOMA2 is a later nonlinear computer model that makes additional assumptions about the glucose-insulin relationship; HOMA1 and HOMA2 results and cutoffs should not be treated as interchangeable. ([Wallace et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.2337/diacare.27.6.1487))

For example:

- Glucose 90 mg/dL and insulin 4 mIU/L: HOMA-IR = 0.89.

- Glucose 90 mg/dL and insulin 18 mIU/L: HOMA-IR = 4.00.


The fasting glucose is identical, but the second person has substantially more circulating insulin. Under the HOMA model, that pattern produces a higher HOMA-IR and is compatible with greater insulin resistance and compensatory insulin secretion. That is the main information HOMA-IR adds: it makes the fasting glucose-insulin relationship visible rather than interpreting either measurement in isolation. ([Matthews et al., 1985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3899825/); [Wallace et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.2337/diacare.27.6.1487))

A higher value is compatible with greater insulin resistance, but HOMA-IR is an **index**, not a concentration or direct physiological quantity. Its interpretation depends on the insulin assay, population, age, metabolic state, and version of the model used. ([Wallace et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.2337/diacare.27.6.1487); [Rohlfing et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40802520/); [Gayoso-Diz et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24131857/); [da Silva et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36468919/))

## How well does HOMA-IR correlate with the glucose clamp?

The hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp estimates insulin sensitivity by raising insulin and measuring how much glucose must be infused to maintain a steady blood-glucose concentration. It remains the reference research method, but it is invasive, labor-intensive, and impractical for routine population testing. ([Vazquez et al., 2026](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/111/8/2251/8512919))

In the original 1985 validation, HOMA-derived insulin resistance correlated strongly with clamp measurements, with a reported Spearman correlation of 0.88. However, this early result came from a relatively limited validation setting and did not mean the two methods were interchangeable in individual patients. ([Matthews et al., 1985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3899825/))

A later meta-analysis covering 22 correlation estimates found a pooled correlation of approximately **-0.60** between log-transformed HOMA-IR and clamp-measured insulin sensitivity. The negative direction is expected: HOMA-IR increases as clamp-measured sensitivity decreases. This represents a useful but only moderate correlation, not evidence that the methods agree or are interchangeable. ([Otten et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24891021/))

A 2026 study of 2,260 Indigenous American adults, including 286 participants with clamp measurements, reported an age- and sex-adjusted Spearman correlation of **0.644** between log-transformed HOMA-IR and the clamp-derived M value after reversing the HOMA-IR direction for presentation. An oral-glucose-tolerance-test index, the Matsuda index, performed somewhat better, with a correlation of 0.691. HOMA-IR and QUICKI were tied for the strongest correlation among indexes based on fasting insulin, while the Matsuda index provided better discrimination. ([Vazquez et al., 2026](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/111/8/2251/8512919))

The same study illustrates the difference between group-level correlation and individual classification. When clamp-defined insulin resistance was operationalized as the lowest quartile of clamp M and HOMA-IR was likewise classified by quartiles, the HOMA-IR classification had 49.3% sensitivity and 83.3% specificity. In other words, it missed roughly half of the people classified as insulin resistant by the direct method. This was a quartile-concordance analysis, not validation of a universal clinical HOMA-IR cutoff. ([Vazquez et al., 2026](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/111/8/2251/8512919))

HOMA-IR therefore tracks insulin resistance reasonably well across groups, but an individual value cannot be assumed to reveal a person's "true" clamp-measured insulin sensitivity. ([Otten et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24891021/); [Vazquez et al., 2026](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/111/8/2251/8512919))

## What HOMA-IR actually measures

HOMA-IR is based entirely on the fasting state. In the original model, fasting glucose and insulin reflect a feedback relationship involving hepatic glucose output, insulin secretion, and insulin action. This is why HOMA-IR is often interpreted as predominantly reflecting basal hepatic insulin resistance, although that description is not liver-specific. ([Matthews et al., 1985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3899825/); [Wallace et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.2337/diacare.27.6.1487); [Abdul-Ghani et al., 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17192339/))

HOMA-IR is **weighted toward basal hepatic physiology**, but it is not a liver-specific test. Fasting insulin also reflects pancreatic compensation, and the equation cannot cleanly separate hepatic resistance from skeletal-muscle resistance or other contributors. ([Wallace et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.2337/diacare.27.6.1487); [Abdul-Ghani et al., 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17192339/))

This matters because skeletal muscle is a major site of glucose disposal after eating or during an oral glucose-tolerance test. Dynamic indexes derived from an OGTT capture the glucose and insulin response over time and can correlate more closely with muscle or whole-body insulin sensitivity than a fasting calculation. ([Abdul-Ghani et al., 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17192339/); [Vazquez et al., 2026](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/111/8/2251/8512919))

Consequently:

- A normal HOMA-IR does not rule out impaired glucose tolerance or abnormal post-meal glucose handling. ([Abdul-Ghani et al., 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17192339/); [Vazquez et al., 2026](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/111/8/2251/8512919))

- An elevated HOMA-IR does not prove that every component of meal-related glucose disposal is impaired. ([Abdul-Ghani et al., 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17192339/))

- HOMA-IR cannot identify which tissue or mechanism is responsible for an abnormal result. ([Wallace et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.2337/diacare.27.6.1487); [Abdul-Ghani et al., 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17192339/))


"Fasting, predominantly hepatic surrogate" is more accurate than "a complete measure of insulin resistance." ([Wallace et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.2337/diacare.27.6.1487); [Abdul-Ghani et al., 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17192339/))

## Why there is no universal HOMA-IR cutoff

Values such as **1.0-1.5** and **2.0-2.5** should not be treated as internationally standardized clinical categories. Published thresholds differ because studies use different insulin assays, populations, ages, reference methods, and outcomes. ([Lee et al., 2016](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0163424); [Gayoso-Diz et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24131857/); [da Silva et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36468919/); [Rohlfing et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40802520/))

In a Hong Kong Chinese cohort, cross-sectional baseline ROC analyses yielded HOMA-IR cutoffs of 1.37 for prevalent dysglycemia and 1.97 for prevalent type 2 diabetes, with sensitivities and specificities of 65.6% and 71.3%, and 65.5% and 82.9%, respectively. In a separate prospective component, the baseline 75th and 90th percentiles among 872 participants who remained normoglycemic over 15 years were 1.44 and 2.03. These were population-specific reference values, not prospective prediction thresholds or universal diagnostic boundaries. ([Lee et al., 2016](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0163424))

In a Spanish adult population, proposed thresholds ranged from approximately 1.85 to 3.46 depending on sex, age, whether the threshold was defined by a population percentile, and whether metabolic-syndrome features were used as the reference. ([Gayoso-Diz et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24131857/))

In 80 adolescents assessed against a hyperglycemic clamp, reported thresholds were greater than 3.22 during puberty and greater than 2.91 after puberty. Pubertal physiology therefore changes the interpretation substantially. ([da Silva et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36468919/))

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis covering 87 studies and 235,148 participants found substantial diversity in the cutoffs used to define insulin resistance, with extreme overall heterogeneity of **I² = 99%**. This reinforces that prevalence estimates depend partly on the selected threshold and on other differences among studies. ([Ballena-Caicedo et al., 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/endocrinology/articles/10.3389/fendo.2025.1646258/full))

Race and sex can also alter the relationship between fasting indexes and directly measured insulin sensitivity. In a study of 240 nondiabetic European American and African American participants, the performance of fasting and OGTT-derived indexes differed across demographic subgroups. In African American men, fasting insulin and HOMA-IR were not significantly correlated with clamp glucose disposal, whereas some OGTT-derived indexes were. This single study supports caution but does not define performance across all ancestries. ([Pisprasert et al., 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3609485/))

The practical conclusion is not that every cutoff is meaningless. Across the cited studies, proposed adult thresholds span values below 1.5 to above 3. These are **contextual, study-derived ranges**, not diagnostic zones equivalent to the established glucose or HbA1c thresholds used for diabetes. ([Lee et al., 2016](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0163424); [Gayoso-Diz et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24131857/); [Sacks et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/clinchem/article/69/8/808/7226244))

## Does HOMA-IR predict diabetes and cardiovascular outcomes?

HOMA-IR has prospective associations with several outcomes at the population level. ([González-González et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36181637/))

A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis included 38 cohort studies and 215,878 participants. Compared with lower HOMA categories, higher HOMA-IR was associated with the following outcomes. ([González-González et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36181637/))

- Incident type 2 diabetes: hazard ratio 1.87. ([González-González et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36181637/))

- Nonfatal major cardiovascular events: hazard ratio 1.46. ([González-González et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36181637/))

- Incident hypertension: hazard ratio 1.35. ([González-González et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36181637/))


The review did not find a clear association with fatal major cardiovascular events or cancer mortality, and evidence for several other outcomes was insufficient. The included studies also used different definitions of "high" HOMA-IR, so the pooled associations do not establish one actionable cutoff. ([González-González et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36181637/))

In the 2026 Indigenous American cohort, each standard-deviation increase in log-transformed HOMA-IR was associated with an adjusted hazard ratio of **1.97** (95% confidence interval 1.77-2.19) for future type 2 diabetes over follow-up of up to 14.5 years. OGTT-derived indexes were nevertheless somewhat stronger predictors. ([Vazquez et al., 2026](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/111/8/2251/8512919))

These observational findings support HOMA-IR as a **population-level risk marker**, but they do not establish the following conclusions. ([González-González et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36181637/); [Vazquez et al., 2026](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/111/8/2251/8512919))

- One HOMA-IR threshold diagnoses insulin resistance in every person. ([Lee et al., 2016](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0163424); [Gayoso-Diz et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24131857/); [Sacks et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/clinchem/article/69/8/808/7226244))

- HOMA-IR necessarily improves clinical decisions beyond established risk factors. ([Sacks et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/clinchem/article/69/8/808/7226244))

- Lowering HOMA-IR itself, independently of glucose, adiposity, blood pressure, lipids, or fitness, improves health outcomes. The cited outcome evidence is observational rather than a test of HOMA-IR-targeted treatment. ([González-González et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36181637/); [Sacks et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/clinchem/article/69/8/808/7226244))


A 2023 laboratory guideline therefore did not recommend routine insulin or proinsulin measurement for most people with diabetes, diabetes risk, or cardiovascular risk. It emphasized assessing clinically established consequences and risk factors rather than diagnosing a condition solely from an insulin-based index. ([Sacks et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/clinchem/article/69/8/808/7226244))

## Insulin-assay variability is a major limitation

Glucose methods used for diagnosis are comparatively standardized. Serum-insulin methods are not fully standardized. ([Sacks et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/clinchem/article/69/8/808/7226244); [Rohlfing et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40802520/))

A 2025 comparison tested 40 serum samples using 12 commercial insulin assays from nine manufacturers and compared them with an isotope-dilution mass-spectrometry method. Individual assays showed excellent internal repeatability, but results differed substantially between assay platforms; only one assay met all of the study's agreement criteria. ([Rohlfing et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40802520/))

This has an important implication: two laboratories can report different fasting-insulin concentrations from the same underlying sample, producing different HOMA-IR values even when the glucose measurement is unchanged. ([Rohlfing et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40802520/))

The problem is longstanding. Laboratory guidelines continue to note that commercial insulin methods are discordant and that full insulin standardization has not been achieved. ([Sacks et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/clinchem/article/69/8/808/7226244))

This is why a cutoff derived with one insulin assay cannot automatically be imported into a laboratory using another assay. It also explains why comparing a current HOMA-IR with an old result from an unknown laboratory may be misleading. ([Rohlfing et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40802520/); [Sacks et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/clinchem/article/69/8/808/7226244))

## Biological variability matters even at the same laboratory

Using the same laboratory reduces one source of disagreement, but it does not eliminate normal within-person fluctuation. ([Rohlfing et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40802520/); [Carobene et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38987271/))

The European Biological Variation Study repeatedly sampled 90 adults without diabetes over ten weeks. The within-person coefficient of variation for HOMA-IR was **26.7%**, largely because fasting insulin itself varied substantially. ([Carobene et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38987271/))

This does not mean every change below 27% is meaningless or that every larger change is real. A coefficient of variation describes biological variability; it is not a clinical decision boundary. It does mean that a modest difference between two isolated measurements should not automatically be interpreted as improved or worsened insulin sensitivity. ([Carobene et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38987271/))

The original HOMA work also reported substantial imprecision in model-derived insulin resistance, and subsequent repeatability research has found materially greater variability for HOMA-IR than for fasting glucose alone. ([Matthews et al., 1985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3899825/); [Carobene et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38987271/))

As a practical implication, a borderline or unexpected result is more credible when it persists across repeated measurements obtained under comparable conditions. ([Carobene et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38987271/))

## HOMA-IR in physically active and sedentary populations

At a population level, greater physical activity was associated with lower HOMA-IR in an observational cohort. Because the study was observational, it supports an association rather than proving that increased activity caused the difference. ([Yoo et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35397649/))

That does not produce a simple "athlete cutoff." A study of competitive athletes found large discipline-specific differences in HOMA-IR. Some athlete groups had higher values than physically active controls, and BMI and alanine aminotransferase were among the factors associated with HOMA-IR within the athletes. ([Chen et al., 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19596184/))

This means that "physically active" is not a sufficient interpretation by itself. Sport type, body size, metabolic health, and the individual laboratory method still matter. Neither cited study establishes a validated HOMA-IR threshold specifically for athletes. ([Yoo et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35397649/); [Chen et al., 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19596184/); [Rohlfing et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40802520/))

## Is the main value of HOMA-IR in tracking it over time?

Using the same laboratory and insulin assay is preferable to comparing values across assay platforms. As a practical strategy, a consistent trend interpreted alongside changes in waist circumference, glucose regulation, lipids, blood pressure, or other metabolic measures may be more informative than an isolated result, although it is not proof of changing physiological insulin sensitivity. ([Rohlfing et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40802520/); [Carobene et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38987271/); [Xiang et al., 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4139101/); [Sacks et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/clinchem/article/69/8/808/7226244))

However, the evidence does not support treating longitudinal HOMA trends as a precise record of changing physiological insulin sensitivity. ([Xiang et al., 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4139101/))

In two longitudinal studies, cross-sectional correlations between HOMA2-derived insulin sensitivity and an intravenous-glucose-tolerance-test measure were 0.61-0.69. Correlations between **changes** in the two measures were only 0.35-0.39. Agreement on whether insulin sensitivity had increased or decreased was also limited: kappa values for HOMA2-%S were 0.17-0.23, while values across HOMA2-%S and Matsuda analyses ranged from 0.17 to 0.32. ([Xiang et al., 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4139101/))

This means two people - or the same person at two time points - can show a change in HOMA without an equivalent change in a more direct sensitivity measure, and vice versa. ([Xiang et al., 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4139101/))

Trending is therefore a reasonable practical strategy, but it should be described as:

> A way to reduce some measurement ambiguity and look for persistent patterns - not a validated substitute for directly measuring changes in insulin sensitivity. ([Rohlfing et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40802520/); [Carobene et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38987271/); [Xiang et al., 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4139101/))

## Which competing claim is best supported?

### Claim 1: HOMA-IR is the most accessible and informative clinical marker

**Partly supported, but overstated.**

HOMA-IR can be accessible when fasting insulin testing is available. In the 2026 cohort, HOMA-IR and QUICKI were the strongest indexes based on fasting insulin and performed better than indexes that did not measure insulin, but OGTT-derived indexes performed better overall. Insulin assays are not fully standardized, and proposed HOMA-IR cutoffs vary across studies rather than forming universal clinical decision limits. ([Vazquez et al., 2026](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/111/8/2251/8512919); [Rohlfing et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40802520/); [Lee et al., 2016](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0163424); [Gayoso-Diz et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24131857/))

### Claim 2: It should remain a population tool and not guide individuals

**The caution is supported, but the conclusion is too absolute.**

HOMA-IR is well suited to epidemiology and risk stratification, and it should not independently determine a diagnosis or treatment. Nevertheless, a repeated value obtained with a consistent assay can contribute contextual information for an individual when interpreted alongside established metabolic measures and with attention to assay and biological variability. ([González-González et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36181637/); [Sacks et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/clinchem/article/69/8/808/7226244); [Rohlfing et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40802520/); [Carobene et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38987271/))

### Claim 3: It is better than glucose alone but misses postprandial and peripheral resistance

**Best supported overall.**

Because HOMA-IR combines fasting insulin and glucose, it can distinguish between identical fasting glucose values accompanied by different insulin concentrations. But it samples only the basal state. It does not directly test meal responses, glucose tolerance, or tissue-specific glucose disposal. Dynamic indexes and direct methods provide information it cannot. ([Matthews et al., 1985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3899825/); [Wallace et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.2337/diacare.27.6.1487); [Abdul-Ghani et al., 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17192339/); [Vazquez et al., 2026](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/111/8/2251/8512919))

### Claim 4: The value is primarily in trending

**Operationally sensible, but scientifically overstated.**

Same-assay tracking avoids inter-assay bias, but within-person variability is substantial, and longitudinal changes in HOMA correlate only modestly with changes measured by more direct methods. Trends are suggestive rather than definitive. ([Rohlfing et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40802520/); [Carobene et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38987271/); [Xiang et al., 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4139101/))

The most defensible synthesis is therefore:

> HOMA-IR is a useful fasting risk marker that adds information beyond glucose alone, but it is neither a universal diagnostic test nor a complete assessment of insulin sensitivity. Repeated same-assay measurements can add context, although modest changes may reflect variability rather than true physiological change. ([Matthews et al., 1985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3899825/); [Vazquez et al., 2026](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/111/8/2251/8512919); [Rohlfing et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40802520/); [Carobene et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38987271/); [Xiang et al., 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4139101/))

## Quick reference

- **Formula:** fasting glucose in mg/dL × fasting insulin in mIU/L ÷ 405. ([Wallace et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.2337/diacare.27.6.1487))

- **Reference comparison:** the pooled correlation between log HOMA-IR and clamp-measured insulin sensitivity is approximately -0.60. ([Otten et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24891021/))

- **Cutoff:** no universally accepted value; cohort-derived thresholds in the cited studies have ranged from below 1.5 to above 3. ([Lee et al., 2016](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0163424); [Gayoso-Diz et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24131857/); [da Silva et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36468919/))

- **Repeatability:** a within-person coefficient of variation of 26.7% was observed in a ten-week European study. ([Carobene et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38987271/))

- **Best use:** population research, metabolic-risk context, and cautious repeated assessment - not stand-alone diagnosis. ([González-González et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36181637/); [Sacks et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/clinchem/article/69/8/808/7226244); [Xiang et al., 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4139101/))


## Practical interpretation

- A low HOMA-IR reflects a lower fasting glucose-insulin product, but it does not rule out impaired post-meal glucose handling. ([Wallace et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.2337/diacare.27.6.1487); [Abdul-Ghani et al., 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17192339/))

- A high result indicates a higher fasting glucose-insulin product and can be compatible with more insulin being required to maintain fasting glucose, but it does not identify the responsible tissue or establish a disease diagnosis. ([Matthews et al., 1985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3899825/); [Wallace et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.2337/diacare.27.6.1487); [Abdul-Ghani et al., 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17192339/))

- Values should be interpreted using the laboratory's assay and reference information rather than internet cutoffs alone. ([Rohlfing et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40802520/); [Sacks et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/clinchem/article/69/8/808/7226244))

- Comparisons are most credible when they use the same laboratory, assay, HOMA version, and comparable fasting conditions. ([Wallace et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.2337/diacare.27.6.1487); [Rohlfing et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40802520/); [Carobene et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38987271/))

- HOMA-IR is most useful when considered alongside fasting glucose, HbA1c, an OGTT when clinically indicated, triglycerides and HDL cholesterol, blood pressure, waist or adiposity measures, medications, and the broader clinical picture. ([Sacks et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/clinchem/article/69/8/808/7226244); [Vazquez et al., 2026](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/111/8/2251/8512919))
