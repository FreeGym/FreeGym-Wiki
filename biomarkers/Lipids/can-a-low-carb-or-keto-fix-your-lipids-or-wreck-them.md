# Can a Low-Carb or Keto Diet Fix Your Lipids - or Wreck Them?

A low-carbohydrate or ketogenic diet can do both. It can lower triglycerides and raise HDL cholesterol while simultaneously increasing LDL cholesterol and apolipoprotein B. These changes are not contradictory: the diet may improve markers associated with insulin-resistant dyslipidemia while increasing the number of atherogenic particles circulating in the blood. ([2026 meta-analysis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12872-026-05799-5 "Ketogenic diet-induced changes in adult lipid metabolism"), [controlled feeding trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33801247/ "A ketogenic low-carbohydrate high-fat diet increases LDL cholesterol in healthy, young, normal-weight women"))

Across randomized trials, the average LDL increase is modest. However, pooled averages do not establish how often individuals have small, absent, or very large responses. A poorly quantified subgroup - often lean and physically active - can experience extreme increases. The unresolved question is not whether this response exists. It does. The unresolved question is whether very high diet-induced LDL and ApoB carry the same long-term cardiovascular consequences in this metabolically healthy subgroup as they do in broader populations. ([2026 meta-analysis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12872-026-05799-5 "Ketogenic diet-induced changes in adult lipid metabolism"), [BMI meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38237807/ "Increased LDL cholesterol on a low-carbohydrate diet in adults with normal but not high body weight"), [2026 clinical review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42047192/ "Ketogenic diets and LDL cholesterol in adults with normal weight"))

As of **September 2, 2026**, the most defensible conclusion has two parts:

- ApoB-containing lipoproteins have a causal role in atherosclerosis, and cumulative exposure matters. ([European Atherosclerosis Society consensus](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/38/32/2459/3745109 "Low-density lipoproteins cause atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease"), [ApoB Mendelian-randomization analysis](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1003062 "A multivariable Mendelian-randomisation analysis"), [cumulative LDL cohort study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34550307/ "Association between cumulative LDL exposure and cardiovascular events"))

- The precise absolute risk associated with extreme diet-induced ApoB elevation in lean mass hyper-responders remains unknown. ([2026 clinical review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42047192/ "Ketogenic diets and LDL cholesterol in adults with normal weight"), [2024 cross-sectional KETO Trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39372369/ "Carbohydrate restriction-induced elevations in LDL-cholesterol and atherosclerosis"))


## The average lipid response to ketogenic diets

A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis of 53 randomized controlled trials found that ketogenic diets reduced triglycerides by an average of approximately 22 mg/dL and increased HDL cholesterol by about 3.5 mg/dL. At the same time, LDL cholesterol increased by approximately 8 mg/dL and total cholesterol by approximately 8 mg/dL. There was substantial between-study heterogeneity, meaning the average obscured important differences among studies. Controlled trials and clinical reports also document much larger responses in some people. ([Full text](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12872-026-05799-5 "Ketogenic diet-induced changes in adult lipid metabolism"), [controlled feeding trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33801247/ "A ketogenic low-carbohydrate high-fat diet increases LDL cholesterol in healthy, young, normal-weight women"), [2026 clinical review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42047192/ "Ketogenic diets and LDL cholesterol in adults with normal weight"))

In trial-level subgroup analyses, LDL increases were larger in studies with a mean participant age of 50 years or younger, a mean BMI of 30 or lower, and an intervention duration of 12 weeks or less. These study-level associations should not be treated as validated predictors for an individual. Most trials were short and were designed to measure biomarkers rather than heart attacks, strokes, or cardiovascular mortality. The certainty of evidence was rated moderate for triglycerides and HDL, with downgrading for indirectness, and low for LDL and total cholesterol, with downgrading for indirectness and imprecision relative to the prespecified threshold for clinical importance. ([Full text](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12872-026-05799-5 "Ketogenic diet-induced changes in adult lipid metabolism"))

Evidence in athletes is limited. In a 2018 cross-sectional study of 20 highly trained ultra-endurance athletes, the 10 athletes who habitually consumed a very-low-carbohydrate, high-fat diet had total cholesterol that was 65% higher, LDL cholesterol that was 83% higher, and HDL cholesterol that was 60% higher than the 10 athletes consuming a high-carbohydrate diet. Total LDL particle concentration was also higher. The study was small, cross-sectional, and unable to determine cardiovascular outcomes or establish that diet alone caused the between-group differences. ([Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6173254/ "Paradox of hypercholesterolaemia in highly trained, keto-adapted athletes"))

A 2024 meta-analysis of 41 controlled trials found a strong inverse association between BMI and the LDL response. In trials with a mean participant BMI below 25, carbohydrate restriction increased LDL cholesterol by approximately 41 mg/dL. LDL was largely unchanged in trials with a mean BMI between 25 and 35 and fell modestly in trials enrolling participants with obesity. This was a study-level meta-regression rather than a prospective LMHR study, but it supports the observation that leaner populations can have larger LDL responses. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38237807/ "Increased low-density lipoprotein cholesterol on a low-carbohydrate diet in adults with normal but not high body weight"))

A controlled crossover feeding trial provides evidence that the phenomenon is not explained entirely by self-reported diets. Seventeen healthy, young, normal-weight women completed a four-week ketogenic diet and a control diet. LDL cholesterol rose in every participant during the ketogenic phase, with an average treatment effect of 1.82 mmol/L - approximately 70 mg/dL. ApoB-100, large buoyant LDL, and small dense LDL all increased. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33801247/ "A ketogenic low-carbohydrate high-fat diet increases LDL cholesterol in healthy, young, normal-weight women"))

The practical point is that **a lean body habitus and a favorable pre-diet triglyceride-to-HDL ratio may be associated with a larger LDL response at the group level, but low triglycerides and high HDL do not guarantee that LDL and ApoB will remain low in a particular person**. ([BMI meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38237807/ "BMI and LDL response to carbohydrate restriction"), [LMHR survey](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8796252/ "Evidence for a lean mass hyper-responder phenotype"), [controlled feeding trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33801247/ "A ketogenic low-carbohydrate high-fat diet increases LDL cholesterol in healthy, young, normal-weight women"))

## What the lean mass hyper-responder phenotype means

The original lean mass hyper-responder paper analyzed a web survey of 548 adults following carbohydrate-restricted diets. The authors defined the phenotype using three on-diet lipid criteria: ([Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8796252/ "Elevated LDL cholesterol with a carbohydrate-restricted diet"))

- LDL cholesterol of at least 200 mg/dL

- HDL cholesterol of at least 80 mg/dL

- Triglycerides of no more than 70 mg/dL


One hundred respondents met those criteria. They were leaner than the other respondents and had a mean on-diet LDL cholesterol of approximately 320 mg/dL, despite reporting similar pre-diet LDL levels. Lower BMI and a lower pre-diet triglyceride-to-HDL ratio were associated with larger LDL increases. ([Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8796252/ "Evidence for a lean mass hyper-responder phenotype"))

That study established a recognizable lipid pattern, not a validated diagnosis or a low-risk category. Participants were recruited through an online survey, already knew their lipid responses, and self-reported much of their information. The authors acknowledged selection bias and limited generalizability. The study cannot estimate how common the phenotype is, prove that it represents one biological mechanism, or determine cardiovascular risk. ([Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8796252/ "Evidence for a lean mass hyper-responder phenotype"))

The word **lean** is also somewhat misleading. The formal LMHR definition consists only of the LDL, HDL, and triglyceride triad; measured body composition, physical fitness, insulin sensitivity, inflammatory markers, and absence of atherosclerosis are not part of the definition. A person can satisfy the lipid cutoffs without satisfying additional clinical or metabolic criteria. ([Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8796252/ "Evidence for a lean mass hyper-responder phenotype"))

## The proposed Lipid Energy Model

The Lipid Energy Model proposes that a lean person consuming very little carbohydrate relies heavily on fat-based fuel. Fatty acids released from adipose tissue travel to the liver, where they are repackaged into triglyceride-rich VLDL particles. Lipoprotein lipase removes triglycerides from those particles for delivery to muscle and other tissues, with some remnants ultimately remodeled into LDL and surface components contributing to HDL formation. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35629964/ "The Lipid Energy Model"))

This could explain the combination of high LDL, high HDL, and low triglycerides as a consequence of increased lipid trafficking rather than the triglyceride-rich, low-HDL pattern commonly associated with insulin resistance. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35629964/ "The Lipid Energy Model"))

The mechanism is biologically plausible and testable. It is **not evidence that the resulting particles are harmless**. The authors describe the model as a hypothesis and acknowledge the need for prospective imaging and outcome studies. Explaining why ApoB rises does not establish that arterial walls are unaffected by the additional circulating particles. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35629964/ "The Lipid Energy Model"), [2026 clinical review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42047192/ "Ketogenic diets and LDL cholesterol in adults with normal weight"))

Other factors may also contribute. The original survey did not fully characterize changes in saturated fat, unsaturated fat, fiber, energy balance, or other aspects of diet composition. Reviews of ketogenic-diet responses identify diet composition and individual metabolic differences as plausible contributors to variation. The phenotype should therefore not automatically be attributed to one energy-flux mechanism. ([LMHR survey](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8796252/ "Evidence for a lean mass hyper-responder phenotype"), [2026 meta-analysis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12872-026-05799-5 "Ketogenic diet-induced changes in adult lipid metabolism"), [2026 clinical review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42047192/ "Ketogenic diets and LDL cholesterol in adults with normal weight"))

## Do low triglycerides and high HDL cancel the LDL risk?

The broader evidence does not support treating high HDL as an antidote to high ApoB. ([ApoB Mendelian-randomization analysis](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1003062 "A multivariable Mendelian-randomisation analysis"))

In a multivariable Mendelian-randomization analysis using data from as many as 441,016 participants, ApoB was the lipid-related measure that retained a robust association with coronary heart disease after accounting for LDL cholesterol, triglycerides, and related apolipoproteins. The apparent protective associations of HDL-related measures were attenuated toward null after accounting for ApoB. ([Full text](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1003062 "A multivariable Mendelian-randomisation analysis"))

Evidence from genetic studies, epidemiological cohorts, and randomized lipid-lowering trials supports a dose-dependent and cumulative relationship between exposure to ApoB-containing particles and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. Risk reflects both particle concentration and duration of exposure. Other risk factors influence absolute risk, but they do not demonstrate that prolonged ApoB exposure becomes biologically irrelevant. ([European Atherosclerosis Society consensus](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/38/32/2459/3745109 "Low-density lipoproteins cause atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease"), [cumulative LDL cohort study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34550307/ "Association between cumulative LDL exposure and cardiovascular events"))

Excellent blood pressure, glucose control, body composition, smoking status, and physical fitness can make one person's absolute risk lower than that of another person with the same ApoB. That is different from demonstrating that ApoB has no effect. No LMHR-specific outcome study has shown that low triglycerides, high HDL, or low insulin neutralizes prolonged exposure to very high ApoB. ([European Atherosclerosis Society consensus](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/38/32/2459/3745109 "Low-density lipoproteins cause atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease"), [2026 clinical review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42047192/ "Ketogenic diets and LDL cholesterol in adults with normal weight"))

The defensible position therefore lies between two overconfident extremes:

- It is not justified to claim that an LDL cholesterol of 300 mg/dL carries precisely the same absolute risk in a lean mass hyper-responder as it does in someone who also has diabetes, hypertension, smoking exposure, or genetically mediated familial hypercholesterolemia. Existing LMHR studies do not provide the event data needed for that comparison. ([2026 clinical review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42047192/ "Ketogenic diets and LDL cholesterol in adults with normal weight"), [2024 cross-sectional KETO Trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39372369/ "Carbohydrate restriction-induced elevations in LDL-cholesterol and atherosclerosis"))

- It is also not justified to claim that an LDL cholesterol of 300 mg/dL is harmless because triglycerides are 50 mg/dL and HDL is 90 mg/dL. Supporting outcome evidence for that reassurance is lacking. ([ApoB Mendelian-randomization analysis](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1003062 "A multivariable Mendelian-randomisation analysis"), [European Atherosclerosis Society consensus](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/38/32/2459/3745109 "Low-density lipoproteins cause atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease"), [2026 clinical review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42047192/ "Ketogenic diets and LDL cholesterol in adults with normal weight"))


## ApoB is the better tie-breaker - but not an escape hatch

LDL cholesterol measures the mass of cholesterol contained within LDL particles. ApoB more closely reflects the number of circulating atherogenic particles because each LDL, IDL, VLDL-remnant, and lipoprotein(a) particle contains one ApoB molecule. ([ApoB Mendelian-randomization analysis](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1003062 "A multivariable Mendelian-randomisation analysis"))

Two people can therefore have the same LDL cholesterol but different ApoB concentrations. One may have fewer, cholesterol-rich particles; the other may have a larger number of particles carrying less cholesterol each. The opportunity for particles to enter and become retained in the arterial wall is more closely related to particle number than to the average amount of cholesterol carried per particle. ([ApoB Mendelian-randomization analysis](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1003062 "A multivariable Mendelian-randomisation analysis"), [European Atherosclerosis Society consensus](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/38/32/2459/3745109 "Low-density lipoproteins cause atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease"))

In the CARDIA cohort, 2,794 initially young adults were followed for approximately 25 years. Higher ApoB predicted later coronary artery calcium. When ApoB and LDL cholesterol were discordant, coronary-calcium risk generally tracked more closely with ApoB. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26791067/ "Discordance between apolipoprotein B and LDL cholesterol in young adults predicts coronary artery calcification"))

This makes ApoB particularly useful when LDL cholesterol and ApoB appear to tell different stories. All else equal, an LDL cholesterol of 250 mg/dL accompanied by disproportionately lower ApoB indicates fewer atherogenic particles than the same LDL cholesterol accompanied by very high ApoB and may imply lower risk. It does not follow that the first profile is necessarily low-risk; LMHR-specific event data defining an acceptably low ApoB concentration do not exist. ([ApoB Mendelian-randomization analysis](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1003062 "A multivariable Mendelian-randomisation analysis"), [CARDIA study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26791067/ "Discordance between apolipoprotein B and LDL cholesterol"), [2026 clinical review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42047192/ "Ketogenic diets and LDL cholesterol in adults with normal weight"))

Nor should a keto dieter assume that larger LDL particles mean ApoB stayed normal. ApoB rose alongside LDL cholesterol in the controlled ketogenic feeding trial, even though both large buoyant and small dense LDL subfractions increased. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33801247/ "A ketogenic low-carbohydrate high-fat diet increases LDL cholesterol in healthy, young, normal-weight women"))

ApoB therefore helps answer **how many atherogenic particles are circulating now**. It does not measure how much plaque has already accumulated, and favorable ApoB-LDL discordance has not been shown to eliminate risk in LMHR. ([CARDIA study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26791067/ "Discordance between apolipoprotein B and LDL cholesterol"), [MESA study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31604582/ "Clinical significance of zero coronary artery calcium"), [2026 clinical review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42047192/ "Ketogenic diets and LDL cholesterol in adults with normal weight"))

## What a zero CAC score does - and does not - mean

Coronary artery calcium scoring measures calcified coronary plaque. It is a measure of disease burden already present, rather than a direct measure of the current rate at which ApoB-containing particles are entering and becoming retained in the arterial wall. ([MESA study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31604582/ "Clinical significance of zero coronary artery calcium"), [European Atherosclerosis Society consensus](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/38/32/2459/3745109 "Low-density lipoproteins cause atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease"))

In the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis, 246 participants had LDL cholesterol of at least 190 mg/dL. Their mean age was 63 and mean LDL cholesterol was 215 mg/dL. Thirty-seven percent had a CAC score of zero. Over a median follow-up of 13.2 years, participants with CAC zero had an estimated 10-year cardiovascular event risk of approximately 3.7%, compared with approximately 20% among those with detectable calcium. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31604582/ "Clinical significance of zero coronary artery calcium in individuals with LDL cholesterol at least 190 mg/dL"))

That is important evidence: **a zero CAC score can identify substantially lower near- to intermediate-term risk even among people with severe hypercholesterolemia**. ([MESA study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31604582/ "Clinical significance of zero coronary artery calcium"))

It does not prove lifetime immunity. CAC detects calcified plaque and can miss earlier noncalcified plaque. In a 2025 cohort of 23,777 symptomatic patients with CAC zero who also underwent coronary CT angiography, approximately 11% had noncalcified coronary plaque. Higher LDL cholesterol was associated with both noncalcified plaque and subsequent coronary events; the association with plaque was strongest among participants aged 45 or younger. This was a symptomatic clinical cohort, not an LMHR cohort or a general-population screening sample, but it demonstrates why CAC zero does not exclude earlier noncalcified plaque, particularly in younger people. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40795407/ "Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol and cardiovascular risk in the absence of calcifications on computed tomography"))

A positive CAC score indicates calcified coronary atherosclerosis. Plaque detected on coronary CT angiography indicates coronary atherosclerosis, including noncalcified plaque. CAC zero means that no calcified plaque was detected; a normal coronary CT angiogram means that no plaque was detected by that examination. Neither result can show that another 20 years of severe ApoB elevation will be harmless. ([Western Denmark Heart Registry](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40795407/ "LDL cholesterol and cardiovascular risk in the absence of calcifications on computed tomography"), [European Atherosclerosis Society consensus](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/38/32/2459/3745109 "Low-density lipoproteins cause atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease"), [2026 clinical review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42047192/ "Ketogenic diets and LDL cholesterol in adults with normal weight"))

## What the non-retracted LMHR imaging study found

The principal peer-reviewed LMHR and near-LMHR imaging study is a 2024 cross-sectional analysis of 80 people following ketogenic diets. Participants had diet-induced LDL cholesterol of at least 190 mg/dL, HDL cholesterol of at least 60 mg/dL, triglycerides of no more than 80 mg/dL, and no familial-hypercholesterolemia mutation identified by the study's genetic testing. Thirty-five of the 80 participants met all three stricter original LMHR thresholds. They were matched 1:1 with 80 participants from the Miami Heart cohort on age, gender, race, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, and smoking status. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39372369/ "Carbohydrate restriction-induced elevations in LDL-cholesterol and atherosclerosis"))

The ketogenic group had the following characteristics: ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39372369/ "Carbohydrate restriction-induced elevations in LDL-cholesterol and atherosclerosis"))

- A mean age of 55.5 years

- A mean BMI of 22.5

- A mean LDL cholesterol of 272 mg/dL

- An average reported ketogenic-diet duration of 4.7 years

- A median CAC score of zero


Median CAC and coronary CT angiography plaque scores were not significantly higher than in the comparison group, whose mean LDL cholesterol was approximately 123 mg/dL. Within the ketogenic cohort, LDL cholesterol was not correlated with cross-sectional plaque burden. The study was not designed as an equivalence or noninferiority trial, so a nonsignificant difference cannot exclude a clinically important difference. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39372369/ "Carbohydrate restriction-induced elevations in LDL-cholesterol and atherosclerosis"))

This finding should not be dismissed. In this selected group of middle-aged people, the study did not detect a statistically significant difference in measured plaque burden between the ketogenic group and the chosen controls. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39372369/ "Carbohydrate restriction-induced elevations in LDL-cholesterol and atherosclerosis"))

It also does not establish cardiovascular safety. The study was small, observational, and cross-sectional. Ninety percent of participants were White. BMI was lower in the ketogenic group and was not among the listed matching variables. There were no pre-diet scans, and cumulative lifetime LDL or ApoB exposure was not measured or matched, so the study could not determine when existing plaque developed. It measured neither plaque progression nor cardiovascular events. The restricted range of already very high LDL levels within the ketogenic group also limits the informativeness of the internal LDL-plaque correlation. Genetic testing that does not identify a monogenic familial-hypercholesterolemia variant does not exclude every inherited or polygenic contributor. An average exposure of 4.7 years may also be too brief to reveal the full consequences of LDL concentrations that could persist for several decades. ([2024 cross-sectional KETO Trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39372369/ "Carbohydrate restriction-induced elevations in LDL-cholesterol and atherosclerosis"), [2026 clinical review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42047192/ "Ketogenic diets and LDL cholesterol in adults with normal weight"))

The study was funded by the Citizen Science Foundation, and one coauthor was affiliated with that foundation. The authors reported no other relationships relevant to the article. This funding does not invalidate the findings, but independent replication is particularly important when a study is small, highly selected, and addresses a hypothesis developed by members of the research team. ([Full text](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2772963X2400303X "Carbohydrate restriction-induced elevations in LDL-cholesterol and atherosclerosis"))

## The 2026 correction: the longitudinal KETO-CTA paper was retracted

A longitudinal KETO-CTA analysis was published in _JACC: Advances_ in 2025 and initially presented as evidence about one-year plaque progression. That article has since been retracted. The retraction notice reported methodological concerns and errors considered too extensive to address through a corrigendum. The retracted findings should not be used as evidence for or against LMHR safety. ([Retracted article](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40192608/ "Longitudinal data from the KETO-CTA study"), [retraction notice](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacadv.2026.102824 "Retraction notice"))

A reanalysis of the same cohort was posted as a medRxiv preprint in 2026. It has not completed peer review, and the preprint platform states that it reports medical research that has not yet been evaluated and should not guide clinical practice. Its findings should be treated as provisional until the methodology and analyses undergo independent peer review. ([medRxiv preprint](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.01.15.26343955v1 "The impact of sustained LDL-C elevation on plaque changes"))

Consequently, **as of September 2, 2026, the peer-reviewed group-level LMHR cardiovascular evidence available remains cross-sectional**. There is no valid peer-reviewed longitudinal group study showing either that very high diet-induced LDL or ApoB accelerates plaque progression in this phenotype or that it does not. There are also no LMHR-specific cohort data estimating rates of heart attack, stroke, cardiovascular mortality, or all-cause mortality. ([2026 clinical review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42047192/ "Ketogenic diets and LDL cholesterol in adults with normal weight"), [2024 cross-sectional KETO Trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39372369/ "Carbohydrate restriction-induced elevations in LDL-cholesterol and atherosclerosis"), [retraction notice](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacadv.2026.102824 "Retraction notice"), [2026 preprint](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.01.15.26343955v1 "The impact of sustained LDL-C elevation on plaque changes"))

## What longer-term evidence outside LMHR suggests

Long-term cohort evidence supports the importance of cumulative LDL exposure. An analysis of 18,288 participants from four prospective US cohorts found that cumulative LDL exposure during young adulthood and middle age predicted later coronary heart disease independently of the LDL level measured in middle age. This was not an LMHR or ketogenic-diet study, so it cannot supply the missing phenotype-specific risk estimate. It does support concern about maintaining markedly elevated LDL for many years merely because near-term imaging is negative. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34550307/ "Association between cumulative LDL cholesterol exposure during young adulthood and middle age and risk of cardiovascular events"))

A UK Biobank analysis compared 305 people reporting a low-carbohydrate, high-fat dietary pattern with 1,220 matched controls. The dietary group had higher LDL cholesterol and ApoB and experienced more major cardiovascular events during follow-up. Because the diet was assessed using a limited number of self-reported 24-hour dietary records, the sample was small, and the design was observational, residual confounding and dietary misclassification are substantial limitations. The participants were not selected using LMHR criteria. The study is therefore a warning signal, not a direct test of the phenotype. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39081652/ "Association of a low-carbohydrate high-fat diet with plasma lipid levels and cardiovascular risk"))

Imaging trials of less-extreme low-carbohydrate diets are also indirect. In the two-year DIRECT-Carotid randomized trial, low-fat, Mediterranean, and low-carbohydrate weight-loss diets produced similar regression in carotid vessel-wall volume, with no significant difference among diets. Participants had overweight or obesity, and the low-carbohydrate intervention was not designed to reproduce ketogenic LMHR physiology or extreme LDL elevation. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20194883/ "Dietary intervention to reverse carotid atherosclerosis"))

These studies strengthen the general evidence base but do not resolve whether a lean person with triglycerides of 50 mg/dL, HDL cholesterol of 90 mg/dL, ApoB of 180 mg/dL, and LDL cholesterol of 300 mg/dL has the same event rate as other people with comparable ApoB exposure. ([2026 clinical review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42047192/ "Ketogenic diets and LDL cholesterol in adults with normal weight"))

## What the evidence currently supports

The confidence categories below are qualitative judgments based on study design, directness, consistency, and precision. They are not a formal GRADE assessment.

### High-confidence conclusions

- Ketogenic diets generally lower triglycerides and raise HDL over the short term, while producing a variable LDL response. ([Full text](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12872-026-05799-5 "Ketogenic diet-induced changes in adult lipid metabolism"))

- Some lean, healthy, or athletic people experience much larger increases in LDL and ApoB than the trial average. ([Controlled feeding trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33801247/ "A ketogenic low-carbohydrate high-fat diet increases LDL cholesterol in healthy, young, normal-weight women"), [athlete study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6173254/ "Paradox of hypercholesterolaemia in highly trained, keto-adapted athletes"), [2026 clinical review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42047192/ "Ketogenic diets and LDL cholesterol in adults with normal weight"))

- ApoB-containing particles have a causal role in atherosclerosis in the broader human evidence, and cumulative exposure is relevant. ([ApoB Mendelian-randomization analysis](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1003062 "A multivariable Mendelian-randomisation analysis"), [European Atherosclerosis Society consensus](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/38/32/2459/3745109 "Low-density lipoproteins cause atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease"), [cumulative LDL cohort study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34550307/ "Association between cumulative LDL exposure and cardiovascular events"))

- High HDL and low triglycerides have not been shown to neutralize high ApoB. ([ApoB Mendelian-randomization analysis](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1003062 "A multivariable Mendelian-randomisation analysis"), [2026 clinical review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42047192/ "Ketogenic diets and LDL cholesterol in adults with normal weight"))


### Moderate-confidence conclusions

- Lower BMI and a favorable pre-diet triglyceride-to-HDL profile are associated with larger LDL responses to carbohydrate restriction, although the prevalence, individual predictive accuracy, and determinants remain uncertain. ([BMI meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38237807/ "BMI and LDL response to carbohydrate restriction"), [LMHR survey](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8796252/ "Evidence for a lean mass hyper-responder phenotype"))

- ApoB can provide more useful risk information than LDL cholesterol alone when the two measurements are discordant. ([CARDIA study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26791067/ "Discordance between apolipoprotein B and LDL cholesterol"), [ApoB Mendelian-randomization analysis](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1003062 "A multivariable Mendelian-randomisation analysis"))

- CAC can refine estimates of calcified plaque burden and near-term risk, while coronary CT angiography can identify both calcified and noncalcified coronary plaque. Neither has validated lifetime safety in LMHR. ([MESA study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31604582/ "Clinical significance of zero coronary artery calcium"), [CCTA study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40795407/ "LDL cholesterol and cardiovascular risk in the absence of calcifications on computed tomography"), [2026 clinical review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42047192/ "Ketogenic diets and LDL cholesterol in adults with normal weight"))


### Low-confidence or preliminary conclusions

- In selected LMHR and near-LMHR participants, no statistically significant difference in cross-sectional coronary plaque was detected relative to matched controls after an average reported 4.7 years on ketogenic diets. The study was not an equivalence trial, and the result has not established long-term safety. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39372369/ "Carbohydrate restriction-induced elevations in LDL-cholesterol and atherosclerosis"))

- The Lipid Energy Model may explain part of the phenotype, but it has not been validated as a complete explanation and makes no demonstrated prediction of cardiovascular safety. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35629964/ "The Lipid Energy Model"), [2026 clinical review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42047192/ "Ketogenic diets and LDL cholesterol in adults with normal weight"))


### Evidence that is still missing

Current reviews and the available imaging literature identify the following evidence gaps: ([2026 clinical review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42047192/ "Ketogenic diets and LDL cholesterol in adults with normal weight"), [2024 cross-sectional KETO Trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39372369/ "Carbohydrate restriction-induced elevations in LDL-cholesterol and atherosclerosis"), [2026 preprint](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.01.15.26343955v1 "The impact of sustained LDL-C elevation on plaque changes"))

- Independently replicated longitudinal CAC or CCTA progression studies in LMHR

- Long-term LMHR cardiovascular-event and mortality studies

- Prospective comparisons stratified by ApoB-LDL discordance

- Randomized tests of dietary modifications or lipid-lowering therapies specifically in LMHR

- Evidence defining how ApoB exposure relates to outcomes in LMHR and which levels correspond to acceptably low absolute risk across different ages and exposure durations


## Practical interpretation

- **Confirm that the response is persistent.** A dramatic result should be repeated after the dietary pattern and body weight have stabilized. A diet-associated increase does not by itself exclude inherited or secondary contributors to severe hypercholesterolemia. ([2026 ACC/AHA guideline](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001423 "2026 guideline on the management of dyslipidemia"), [2026 clinical review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42047192/ "Ketogenic diets and LDL cholesterol in adults with normal weight"))

- **Measure ApoB when LDL cholesterol and other markers appear discordant.** ApoB provides a closer estimate of atherogenic particle number than LDL cholesterol, while non-HDL cholesterol measures the cholesterol carried by ApoB-containing particles. A clearly elevated ApoB is inconsistent with reassurance based solely on larger LDL size. ([ApoB Mendelian-randomization analysis](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1003062 "A multivariable Mendelian-randomisation analysis"), [CARDIA study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26791067/ "Discordance between apolipoprotein B and LDL cholesterol"), [controlled feeding trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33801247/ "A ketogenic low-carbohydrate high-fat diet increases LDL cholesterol in healthy, young, normal-weight women"))

- **Use coronary imaging selectively to assess existing disease, not to declare lifetime immunity.** CAC can refine risk in appropriate asymptomatic adults, while coronary CT angiography can identify calcified and noncalcified plaque in selected clinical contexts. Positive plaque materially strengthens concern. CAC zero can substantially lower estimated near-term risk in a middle-aged or older person, but it is not proof that continued severe ApoB elevation is safe - particularly in a younger person who has had less time to develop calcified plaque. ([MESA study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31604582/ "Clinical significance of zero coronary artery calcium"), [CCTA study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40795407/ "LDL cholesterol and cardiovascular risk in the absence of calcifications on computed tomography"), [2026 ACC/AHA guideline](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001423 "2026 guideline on the management of dyslipidemia"))

- **Test whether the lipid response can be modified.** Options include replacing some saturated fat with unsaturated fat, increasing soluble fiber, or modestly increasing minimally processed carbohydrate while preserving the parts of the diet that improved metabolic control. Replacing saturated fat with unsaturated fat lowers LDL cholesterol. A meta-analysis of 181 randomized trials found that soluble-fiber supplementation reduced LDL cholesterol and ApoB; each additional 5 g/day of soluble fiber was associated with an average LDL reduction of approximately 5.6 mg/dL. Moderate carbohydrate reintroduction has also produced large LDL reductions in small LMHR-related case reports. ([AHA dietary-fat advisory](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28620111/ "Dietary fats and cardiovascular disease"), [soluble-fiber meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36796439/ "Soluble fiber supplementation and serum lipid profile"), [LMHR case series](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8796252/ "Evidence for a lean mass hyper-responder phenotype"))

- **Do not assume that returning to a conventional high-carbohydrate diet is the only possible dietary intervention.** In the original five-person LMHR-related case series, increasing carbohydrate intake from below 25 g/day to approximately 50-100 g/day was associated with LDL reductions ranging from 100 to 480 mg/dL. That uncontrolled case series cannot establish an ideal intake, prove that carbohydrate itself was the sole cause, or show that nutritional ketosis was maintained, but it shows that the response can be rapidly diet-responsive in some individuals. ([Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8796252/ "Evidence for a lean mass hyper-responder phenotype"), [2026 clinical review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42047192/ "Ketogenic diets and LDL cholesterol in adults with normal weight"))


Current 2026 ACC/AHA guidance places LDL cholesterol of at least 190 mg/dL in a severe hypercholesterolemia pathway, recommends maximally tolerated statin therapy for adults with severe hypercholesterolemia, and specifically advises counseling people whose severe hypercholesterolemia is attributed to a ketogenic diet on ways to mitigate the LDL elevation while preserving the diet's primary goal. The guideline does not identify LMHR as an exception to that pathway. ([2026 ACC/AHA guideline](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001423 "2026 guideline on the management of dyslipidemia"))

Persistent extreme ApoB elevation warrants an individualized discussion that incorporates age, exposure duration, family history, blood pressure, smoking, diabetes status, imaging results, diet quality, treatment options, and the person's tolerance for unresolved long-term risk. ([2026 ACC/AHA guideline](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001423 "2026 guideline on the management of dyslipidemia"), [2026 clinical review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42047192/ "Ketogenic diets and LDL cholesterol in adults with normal weight"))

## Bottom line

A low-carbohydrate or ketogenic diet can **fix one part of the lipid profile while worsening another**. Lower triglycerides and higher HDL are favorable biomarker changes. They do not, by themselves, establish lower cardiovascular event risk or that a large increase in ApoB is harmless. ([2026 meta-analysis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12872-026-05799-5 "Ketogenic diet-induced changes in adult lipid metabolism"), [ApoB Mendelian-randomization analysis](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1003062 "A multivariable Mendelian-randomisation analysis"), [2026 clinical review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42047192/ "Ketogenic diets and LDL cholesterol in adults with normal weight"))

The LMHR phenotype is sufficiently unusual that existing population studies cannot provide a precise individual risk estimate. In that limited sense, the honest answer is **we do not know**. ([2026 clinical review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42047192/ "Ketogenic diets and LDL cholesterol in adults with normal weight"), [2024 cross-sectional KETO Trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39372369/ "Carbohydrate restriction-induced elevations in LDL-cholesterol and atherosclerosis"))

But the uncertainty is narrower than is sometimes claimed. We do not know the LMHR-specific event rate or exactly how much metabolic health modifies absolute risk. We do know that ApoB-containing particles are causally involved in atherosclerosis in broader human evidence, that exposure accumulates over time, and that high HDL has not been shown to switch off that process. ([European Atherosclerosis Society consensus](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/38/32/2459/3745109 "Low-density lipoproteins cause atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease"), [ApoB Mendelian-randomization analysis](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1003062 "A multivariable Mendelian-randomisation analysis"), [cumulative LDL cohort study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34550307/ "Association between cumulative LDL exposure and cardiovascular events"), [2026 clinical review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42047192/ "Ketogenic diets and LDL cholesterol in adults with normal weight"))

An LDL cholesterol of 250-400 mg/dL accompanied by markedly elevated ApoB should therefore be treated as an **uncertain but potentially consequential exposure**, not dismissed because triglycerides are low, HDL is high, or a current CAC score is zero. Disproportionately lower ApoB and an absence of detected plaque would make the near-term picture more favorable, but neither has been validated as a lifetime guarantee in LMHR. ([ApoB Mendelian-randomization analysis](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1003062 "A multivariable Mendelian-randomisation analysis"), [MESA study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31604582/ "Clinical significance of zero coronary artery calcium"), [CCTA study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40795407/ "LDL cholesterol and cardiovascular risk in the absence of calcifications on computed tomography"), [2026 clinical review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42047192/ "Ketogenic diets and LDL cholesterol in adults with normal weight"))

## Conflict of interest and funding considerations

The [2026 general ketogenic-diet meta-analysis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12872-026-05799-5 "Ketogenic diet-induced changes in adult lipid metabolism") reported no study funding and no competing interests. The [controlled ketogenic feeding trial in lean women](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33801247/ "A ketogenic low-carbohydrate high-fat diet increases LDL cholesterol in healthy, young, normal-weight women") reported no author conflicts. The [2018 athlete study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6173254/ "Paradox of hypercholesterolaemia in highly trained, keto-adapted athletes") was supported by Quest Nutrition and the Robert C. and Veronica Atkins Foundation; three authors disclosed book royalties, ownership or equity in Virta Health, or employment by Virta Health.

The [original LMHR paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8796252/ "Evidence for a lean mass hyper-responder phenotype") reported no direct study funding, but several authors disclosed diet-related book royalties, community financial support, affiliations with the Citizen Science Foundation or Own Your Labs, and related nutrition or advocacy activities. The [2024 BMI meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38237807/ "BMI and LDL response to carbohydrate restriction") also included authors affiliated with the Citizen Science Foundation.

The [Lipid Energy Model paper](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35629964/ "The Lipid Energy Model") reported no external funding but included disclosures involving diet-book royalties, nutrition consulting, intellectual property, Patreon support, and related organizations. The [2024 cross-sectional KETO study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39372369/ "Carbohydrate restriction-induced elevations in LDL-cholesterol and atherosclerosis") was funded by the Citizen Science Foundation and included a coauthor affiliated with that foundation.

The [2026 clinical review of ketogenic-diet-induced LDL elevation in adults with normal weight](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42047192/ "Ketogenic diets and LDL cholesterol in adults with normal weight") reported food-industry research grants and honoraria for one author that were unrelated to the article; the other author reported no disclosures.

Some authors of the [ApoB Mendelian-randomization analysis](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1003062 "A multivariable Mendelian-randomisation analysis") and the [European Atherosclerosis Society consensus paper](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/38/32/2459/3745109 "Low-density lipoproteins cause atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease") disclosed pharmaceutical research funding, consulting, or other industry relationships. The [MESA severe-LDL/CAC study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31604582/ "Clinical significance of zero coronary artery calcium") reported no competing financial interests.

These disclosures do not automatically invalidate any study. They increase the importance of study design, independent replication, complete reporting, and placing small observational findings below randomized, genetic, and long-term outcome evidence.
