# Niacin: When Better Lipid Markers Did Not Mean Better Outcomes

Niacin has unusually broad effects across the conventional lipid panel. At therapeutic doses, it can raise HDL cholesterol by roughly 15% to 35%, lower LDL cholesterol by 5% to 25%, and lower triglycerides by 20% to 50%, although the response varies with dose, formulation, and baseline lipid levels.[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2835557/) A meta-analysis of 14 placebo-controlled trials also found a mean Lp(a) reduction of approximately 23%.[2](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.metabol.2016.08.007)

That combination once made niacin look almost uniquely attractive. It improved several measurements associated with cardiovascular risk using a single drug.[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2835557/)[2](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.metabol.2016.08.007) Early clinical evidence appeared to support the biological case.[3](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.1975.03240160024021)[4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3782631/) But contemporary randomized trials found that adding niacin to effective statin-based therapy did not prevent cardiovascular events, while exposing patients to substantial adverse effects.[5](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1107579)[8](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1300955)

The history is therefore more nuanced than either "niacin never worked" or "the modern trials unfairly killed a useful drug." Niacin produced a genuine benefit in one important pre-statin trial,[3](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.1975.03240160024021)[4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3782631/) failed convincingly as an add-on to contemporary LDL-lowering therapy,[5](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1107579)[8](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1300955) and has no established role as an Lp(a)-lowering treatment despite lowering the marker itself.[2](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.metabol.2016.08.007)[7](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2013.06.051)[15](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2025.11.016)

## Why niacin looked so promising

Niacin's attraction was not based only on HDL cholesterol. It lowered triglycerides and LDL cholesterol, changed several atherogenic lipoprotein fractions, and reduced Lp(a), all while substantially raising HDL cholesterol.[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2835557/)[2](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.metabol.2016.08.007)

However, these effects were measurements of biological activity, not proof of clinical benefit.[5](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1107579)[8](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1300955)[13](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.g4379) A lipid concentration can be associated with cardiovascular risk without becoming a valid treatment target.[13](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.g4379) A drug may also alter several favorable markers while producing countervailing metabolic or toxic effects.[8](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1300955)[12](https://doi.org/10.1136/heartjnl-2015-308055)

Niacin ultimately became one of the clearest demonstrations of that distinction.

## The Coronary Drug Project: a real but historical benefit

The Coronary Drug Project enrolled 8,341 men with previous myocardial infarction between 1966 and 1975, before statins and much of contemporary secondary prevention care.[3](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.1975.03240160024021)[4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3782631/)[15](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2025.11.016) Participants assigned to niacin received 3 g per day of immediate-release nicotinic acid.[3](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.1975.03240160024021)

During the treatment period, niacin reduced definite recurrent nonfatal myocardial infarction. Using the full treatment-period life-table analysis, the estimated cumulative incidence was approximately 10.4% in the niacin group and 14.7% in the placebo group. All-cause mortality, however, was not significantly reduced during active treatment.[3](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.1975.03240160024021)

A later follow-up produced an intriguing result. Approximately 15 years after randomization, mortality was 52.0% among participants originally assigned to niacin and 58.2% among those assigned to placebo, an absolute difference of 6.2 percentage points.[4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3782631/)

That finding should not be dismissed, but it also should not be overstated. Assigned treatment had ended about nine years earlier, and the mortality analysis was conducted as extended post-trial follow-up. It therefore estimates the long-term effect of approximately six years of assignment to niacin, not the effect of continuing niacin for 15 years. Nevertheless, because follow-up retained the original randomized groups, the mortality difference is stronger than an ordinary observational association. It is best interpreted as a statistically significant post-trial legacy finding that may have followed the earlier reduction in reinfarction, not as proof that continued niacin therapy improves survival in modern practice.[4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3782631/)

## AIM-HIGH: no benefit despite improved lipids

AIM-HIGH tested a substantially different question. It enrolled 3,414 adults with established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, low HDL cholesterol, elevated triglycerides, and LDL cholesterol already intensively treated with simvastatin, with ezetimibe permitted when needed. Extended-release niacin at 1.5 to 2 g per day was added to this background therapy.[5](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1107579)

At two years, median HDL cholesterol in the niacin group had risen from 35 to 42 mg/dL, triglycerides had fallen from 164 to 122 mg/dL, and LDL cholesterol had fallen from 74 to 62 mg/dL. Relative changes from baseline were a 25.0% increase in HDL cholesterol, a 28.6% reduction in triglycerides, and a 12.0% reduction in LDL cholesterol with niacin, compared with changes of 9.8%, 8.1%, and 5.5%, respectively, with placebo. Yet the primary cardiovascular endpoint occurred in 16.4% of the niacin group and 16.2% of the placebo group, producing a hazard ratio of 1.02, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.87 to 1.21.[5](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1107579)

The trial was stopped early after a prespecified futility boundary was crossed. Although an unexpected numerical imbalance in ischemic stroke contributed to concern, a subsequent analysis did not establish a causal effect. Because relatively few strokes occurred, however, an increased risk could not be excluded.[20](https://doi.org/10.1161/STROKEAHA.113.001529) The primary finding was simpler: the cardiovascular event curves were nearly superimposable, with no observed incremental benefit. AIM-HIGH alone did not exclude a modest benefit, but the much larger HPS2-THRIVE trial substantially narrowed that uncertainty.[5](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1107579)[8](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1300955)

AIM-HIGH did use an open-label niacin run-in before randomization, so its randomized population had already demonstrated an ability to tolerate at least 1.5 g per day. This means the trial probably understated the difficulty of initiating niacin in ordinary practice.[5](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1107579)

AIM-HIGH was funded by the US National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute with additional support from Abbott Laboratories. Abbott supplied the extended-release niacin, matching placebo, and ezetimibe, while Merck supplied simvastatin. Neither company had a role in the oversight or design of the study or in the analysis or interpretation of the data. These industry relationships should be disclosed, although the null result did not favor the commercial product.[5](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1107579)

## HPS2-THRIVE: a larger null trial with measurable harm

HPS2-THRIVE randomized 25,673 adults with established vascular disease who were already receiving effective statin-based treatment. The intervention contained 2 g of extended-release niacin plus 40 mg of laropiprant, a drug intended to reduce niacin-related flushing. The control group received placebo.[8](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1300955)

Compared with placebo, the combination lowered LDL cholesterol by an additional 10 mg/dL, raised HDL cholesterol by 6 mg/dL, and lowered triglycerides by approximately 33 mg/dL. Nevertheless, major vascular events occurred in 13.2% of the treatment group and 13.7% of the placebo group, corresponding to a rate ratio of 0.96, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.90 to 1.03.[8](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1300955)

The small numerical difference was not statistically significant. The confidence interval excluded a proportional cardiovascular benefit much larger than approximately 10%, including the large benefit that had been hypothesized. At the same time, treatment increased serious disturbances of diabetes control, new diabetes diagnoses, and serious gastrointestinal, musculoskeletal, skin, infection, and bleeding events.[8](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1300955)

Among participants who already had diabetes, serious disturbances of glycemic control occurred in 11.1% of the niacin-laropiprant group and 7.5% of the placebo group. New diabetes was diagnosed in 5.7% and 4.3%, respectively, among participants without diabetes at baseline. Serious infections increased by approximately 1.4 percentage points and serious bleeding by 0.7 percentage points.[8](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1300955)

The tolerability problem was even greater than the randomized results alone suggest. More than 38,000 people entered an active-treatment run-in, but only 25,673 proceeded to randomization. Roughly one third were excluded before randomization, mainly because of adverse effects or other tolerability problems.[9](https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/eht055)

There is an important attribution caveat. HPS2-THRIVE tested niacin and laropiprant together, without a laropiprant-only group. It therefore cannot determine how much of every adverse effect came from niacin. Hyperglycemia, gastrointestinal symptoms, and skin reactions were consistent with known niacin effects, while myopathy was a recognized risk particularly when niacin was combined with some statins. The infection and bleeding findings were less expected and cannot be assigned specifically to niacin.[8](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1300955)[9](https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/eht055)

HPS2-THRIVE was funded by a grant from Merck, the manufacturer of the tested combination and several background therapies. Oxford University's Clinical Trial Service Unit was the regulatory sponsor and reported designing, conducting, analyzing, and interpreting the trial independently of the funder. The trial's lack of efficacy and unfavorable safety findings should be interpreted with that sponsorship disclosed.[8](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1300955)[9](https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/eht055)

## What the trials actually establish

|Claim|Evidence-based verdict|
|---|---|
|Modern trials closed the case for niacin added to statins|Largely correct for contemporary secondary prevention. AIM-HIGH and HPS2-THRIVE found no incremental cardiovascular benefit when niacin-based treatment was added to effective statin therapy.[5](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1107579)[8](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1300955)|
|The Coronary Drug Project no longer matters|Too dismissive. It found fewer recurrent nonfatal myocardial infarctions and a later mortality finding, but under a treatment environment very different from modern care.[3](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.1975.03240160024021)[4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3782631/)|
|Niacin still has a niche for elevated Lp(a)|Not established. Niacin lowers Lp(a), but randomized outcomes evidence has not established clinical benefit from using niacin for elevated Lp(a).[2](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.metabol.2016.08.007)[7](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2013.06.051)[15](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2025.11.016)|
|The trials prove that HDL, triglycerides, and Lp(a) do not matter|Incorrect. They show that niacin's particular combination of marker changes did not produce net benefit. They do not establish that every selective intervention against these pathways will fail.[5](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1107579)[8](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1300955)[13](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.g4379)[14](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCGEN.117.001696)|
|The trials prove niacin is useless as monotherapy|Too broad. They directly answer the add-on-to-statin question. Modern monotherapy evidence is limited, but that uncertainty does not establish a useful indication.[11](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2019.2224)[15](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2025.11.016)|

## Did AIM-HIGH reveal a high-triglyceride subgroup?

A secondary AIM-HIGH analysis examined whether baseline lipoprotein patterns identified patients who benefited from niacin. No significant benefit appeared across individual tertiles of LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, or triglycerides.[6](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2013.07.023)

In an exploratory subgroup with both high triglycerides and very low HDL cholesterol, the hazard ratio was 0.74, but the result was not statistically significant. A still narrower subgroup produced a nominally significant hazard ratio of 0.64, but it involved only 439 participants and depended on more restrictive cutoffs.[6](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2013.07.023)

The investigators appropriately described these findings as hypothesis-generating because multiple subgroup comparisons increase the probability of a chance-positive result. These exploratory analyses therefore did not establish a niacin-responsive high-triglyceride, low-HDL population.[6](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2013.07.023)

## Does Lp(a) provide a reason to use niacin?

Niacin does lower Lp(a). The pooled average reduction of approximately 23% is comparable in magnitude to the 26.9% median Lp(a) reduction observed with evolocumab, a clinically available PCSK9-directed LDL-lowering drug.[2](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.metabol.2016.08.007)[15](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2025.11.016)[16](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.118.037184) It is therefore inaccurate to describe niacin as the only clinically available drug that lowers Lp(a).[15](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2025.11.016)[16](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.118.037184)

The more important question is whether niacin's Lp(a) reduction prevents events. AIM-HIGH provides the most relevant direct evidence. Niacin lowered Lp(a) by approximately 19% to 21%, but higher Lp(a) remained associated with residual cardiovascular risk and niacin produced no overall treatment benefit.[7](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2013.06.051)

A HPS2-THRIVE substudy likewise found that the proportional Lp(a) response varied with baseline Lp(a) and apo(a) isoform characteristics. The average reduction was approximately 31%, but it was only about 18% in the quintile with the highest baseline Lp(a) and low apo(a) isoform size. Investigators estimated that the Lp(a)-mediated coronary benefit expected from reductions of this size would have been small.[14](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCGEN.117.001696)

These findings do not prove that Lp(a) is an invalid therapeutic target. They show that niacin's modest and variable Lp(a) reduction, embedded within a drug with substantial off-target toxicity, did not deliver a favorable clinical result.[7](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2013.06.051)[8](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1300955)[14](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCGEN.117.001696)

Selective investigational agents have produced much larger Lp(a) reductions in phase 2 trials. Pelacarsen lowered Lp(a) by up to approximately 80%, olpasiran produced reductions exceeding 90% at higher doses, and a single 400 mg dose of lepodisiran produced a placebo-adjusted time-averaged reduction of approximately 94% from days 60 to 180.[17](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1905239)[18](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2211023)[19](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2415818)

Those studies established effects on the biomarker, not reductions in myocardial infarction, stroke, or cardiovascular mortality. They were also industry funded: the pelacarsen phase 2 trial was funded by Akcea Therapeutics, the olpasiran trial by Amgen, and the lepodisiran trial by Eli Lilly.[17](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1905239)[18](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2211023)[19](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2415818) Definitive outcomes evidence is therefore required before even these far more potent Lp(a)-lowering strategies can be assumed to prevent events.

Cost or lack of access to investigational therapies does not convert niacin's biomarker effect into demonstrated cardiovascular benefit.

## What about people who cannot tolerate statins?

AIM-HIGH and HPS2-THRIVE did not test niacin as contemporary monotherapy in statin-intolerant patients. It is therefore technically correct that these trials establish a lack of add-on benefit, not universal ineffectiveness under every possible treatment scenario.[5](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1107579)[8](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1300955)

However, no modern cardiovascular outcomes trial has established that niacin improves outcomes in a population selected for statin intolerance.[11](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2019.2224)[15](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2025.11.016) A 2019 systematic review found apparent benefits in trials where niacin was used without statins, but those estimates were driven mainly by two trials conducted in the 1970s and 1980s. The authors concluded that any possible monotherapy role would require new testing in contemporary care.[11](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2019.2224)

This distinction matters. Absence of a modern monotherapy trial creates uncertainty, not evidence of benefit. Current lipid guidelines favor maximally tolerated statin therapy and nonstatin drugs with contemporary outcomes evidence, while advising that niacin generally be avoided because of poor tolerability, adverse effects, and lack of demonstrated net benefit.[15](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2025.11.016)

## The adverse-effect burden is clinically important

Niacin's common flushing and itching can interfere with adherence even when they are not medically dangerous. More consequential risks include worsened glycemic control, new diabetes, gastrointestinal toxicity, hyperuricemia and gout, hepatotoxicity, and muscle toxicity, particularly with some statin combinations.[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2835557/)[8](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1300955)

A meta-analysis of 11 trials involving 26,340 participants without diabetes found a 34% relative increase in new-onset diabetes, corresponding to approximately one additional diabetes diagnosis for every 43 people treated for five years.[12](https://doi.org/10.1136/heartjnl-2015-308055)

A Cochrane review of 23 randomized trials involving 39,195 participants found no significant reduction in all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, myocardial infarction, or stroke. Participants assigned to niacin were more than twice as likely to discontinue treatment because of adverse effects.[10](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD009744.pub2)

The safety profile might be acceptable for a drug with a substantial outcome benefit. Once that benefit disappeared in modern add-on trials, the same adverse effects became difficult to justify.[5](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1107579)[8](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1300955)

## The correct surrogate-endpoint lesson

The niacin story is not proof that lipid markers are meaningless. LDL cholesterol lowering through validated interventions reduces cardiovascular risk,[15](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2025.11.016) and therapies directed at triglyceride-rich lipoproteins or Lp(a) must be judged on their own randomized outcomes evidence.[8](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1300955)[15](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2025.11.016)

The narrower lesson is stronger and more defensible: favorable changes in HDL cholesterol, triglycerides, LDL cholesterol, and Lp(a) cannot be added together and treated as a guarantee of benefit.[5](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1107579)[8](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1300955)[13](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.g4379)[14](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCGEN.117.001696)

A meta-analysis of 39 trials involving 117,411 participants found that pharmacologically raising HDL cholesterol did not reduce myocardial infarction, stroke, or mortality when effective statin therapy was already present.[13](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.g4379) That does not establish that HDL biology is irrelevant. It shows that the measured concentration of HDL cholesterol is not, by itself, a validated treatment target.[13](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.g4379)

Niacin also demonstrates why a drug must be evaluated as a whole intervention. Its potential benefit from changing lipoproteins must be weighed against hyperglycemia, treatment discontinuation, liver and muscle toxicity, and other adverse effects.[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2835557/)[8](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1300955)[10](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD009744.pub2)[12](https://doi.org/10.1136/heartjnl-2015-308055) Clinical outcomes represent the net result of all those mechanisms, including ones that a routine lipid panel cannot reveal.[5](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1107579)[8](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1300955)

## Conclusion

Niacin should not be described as a drug that never produced evidence of benefit. In the pre-statin Coronary Drug Project, it reduced recurrent nonfatal myocardial infarction, and long-term follow-up produced a notable mortality finding.[3](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.1975.03240160024021)[4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3782631/)

But the modern evidence is equally clear within its proper scope. Adding extended-release niacin to effective statin-based therapy did not reduce cardiovascular events in AIM-HIGH, and adding niacin plus laropiprant did not reduce major vascular events in HPS2-THRIVE.[5](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1107579)[8](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1300955) The latter strategy also increased several categories of serious adverse events.[8](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1300955)

Niacin's Lp(a) effect does not rescue it. Randomized outcomes evidence has not established that prescribing niacin for elevated Lp(a) improves clinical outcomes.[2](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.metabol.2016.08.007)[7](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2013.06.051)[15](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2025.11.016) Its possible use as contemporary monotherapy in statin-intolerant patients remains unproven,[11](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2019.2224)[15](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2025.11.016) while better-tolerated nonstatin therapies with outcomes evidence are available.[15](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2025.11.016)

The most durable conclusion is therefore not that biomarkers are useless. It is that improving several biomarkers at once is still only a hypothesis until randomized trials show that patients actually live longer or experience fewer cardiovascular events.[5](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1107579)[8](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1300955)[13](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.g4379)[15](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2025.11.016)
