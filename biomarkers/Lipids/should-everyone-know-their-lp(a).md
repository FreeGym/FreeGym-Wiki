# Should Everyone Know Their Lp(a)?

Most adults should probably be offered an Lp(a) measurement at least once. The case is strong enough that recent European and US lipid guidelines now recommend or support one-time adult testing. Their justification is **risk identification and more intensive prevention**. It is not based on a completed randomized trial showing that universal screening prevents cardiovascular events, and it does not imply that every person with an elevated result should receive an Lp(a)-lowering drug. ([2025 ESC/EAS focused update](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/46/42/4359/8234482); [2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001423); [Current Atherosclerosis Reports](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11883-026-01453-9))

The distinction matters. Lp(a) is causally associated with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and calcific aortic valve stenosis, but no completed cardiovascular-outcomes trial has yet demonstrated that selectively lowering Lp(a) reduces events. As of **August 15, 2026**, the Lp(a)HORIZON trial registry listed the study as active, not recruiting, and a peer-reviewed review published on August 13, 2026 reported that results were expected in late 2026. ([European Atherosclerosis Society consensus statement](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/43/39/3925/6670882); [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04023552); [Current Atherosclerosis Reports](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11883-026-01453-9))

## What Lp(a) is

Lp(a) is an LDL-like particle containing apolipoprotein B-100 covalently attached to an additional protein, apolipoprotein(a). Its concentration is more than 90 percent genetically determined, varies substantially among individuals and ancestry groups, and is only minimally influenced by diet or exercise. ([European Atherosclerosis Society consensus statement](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/43/39/3925/6670882); [2025 ESC/EAS focused update](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/46/42/4359/8234482))

The relationship with cardiovascular disease is continuous rather than divided into naturally "normal" and "abnormal" groups. Risk begins to rise modestly around 30 mg/dL and becomes more clinically important above approximately 50 mg/dL, with progressively greater risk at higher concentrations. The 2025 ESC/EAS update estimates that levels above 50 mg/dL affect at least 20 percent of the population. ([2025 ESC/EAS focused update](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/46/42/4359/8234482))

Mass concentration in mg/dL and particle concentration in nmol/L are not perfectly interchangeable because the size of apolipoprotein(a) varies. Different guidelines therefore attach slightly different nmol/L values to the same approximate mg/dL threshold. A single universal conversion factor should not be used. ([European Atherosclerosis Society consensus statement](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/43/39/3925/6670882); [2025 ESC/EAS focused update](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/46/42/4359/8234482))

## How strong is the causal evidence?

The evidence that Lp(a) is causal, not merely correlated with cardiovascular disease, is supported by convergent genetic and epidemiologic findings. ([European Atherosclerosis Society consensus statement](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/43/39/3925/6670882))

In a 2009 genetic association study, two common LPA variants that raise Lp(a) were associated with substantially higher odds of coronary disease. Because genetic variants are assigned at conception and generally precede the development of lifestyle risk factors or disease, these findings reduce, although do not entirely eliminate, concerns about reverse causation and confounding. ([New England Journal of Medicine](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20032323/); [European Atherosclerosis Society consensus statement](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/43/39/3925/6670882))

For calcific aortic-valve disease, a 2013 genome-wide association study identified an LPA variant associated with approximately twice the odds of aortic-valve calcification. The association was replicated across cohorts, and the same variant predicted incident clinical aortic stenosis during prospective follow-up. ([New England Journal of Medicine](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23388002/))

Mendelian-randomization analyses also suggest that the cardiovascular effect depends on the **absolute amount** by which Lp(a) differs. Estimates have varied: one analysis calculated that a genetically predicted lifelong difference of about 102 mg/dL might produce an effect comparable with a 38.7 mg/dL difference in LDL-C, while another estimated about 66 mg/dL. The disagreement reflects differences in instruments, assays and statistical assumptions, and illustrates why these estimates should not be interpreted as direct predictions of the effect of drug treatment begun later in life. ([JAMA Cardiology](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamacardiology/fullarticle/2685216); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31017618/))

These genetic studies reflect differences in exposure operating over much of a lifetime. They do not prove that starting an Lp(a)-lowering drug at age 60 will reproduce the effect of having genetically lower Lp(a) from birth. That is the central question cardiovascular-outcomes trials must answer. ([JAMA Cardiology](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamacardiology/fullarticle/2685216); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31017618/); [European Atherosclerosis Society consensus statement](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/43/39/3925/6670882))

## Is one measurement really enough?

For people with clearly low or clearly high values, Lp(a) is usually stable enough for one measurement to identify the lifelong risk category. ([European Journal of Preventive Cardiology](https://academic.oup.com/eurjpc/article/32/9/716/7840282))

In a cohort of 11,669 people with two measurements separated by a median of 4.5 years, 96.4 percent of those initially below 30 mg/dL remained in the low category, while 89.9 percent of those at or above 50 mg/dL remained in the high category. Among those initially between 30 and 50 mg/dL, 51.2 percent changed category, leaving 48.8 percent in the intermediate category. The cohort was clinically selected and predominantly White among participants with recorded race, which limits generalizability. ([European Journal of Preventive Cardiology](https://academic.oup.com/eurjpc/article/32/9/716/7840282))

"One test for life" is therefore a useful simplification, not an absolute rule. A repeat measurement may be reasonable when the first result is borderline and management depends on confirmation. Assay variation should also be considered when interpreting discordant results. The 2025 ESC/EAS update notes that Lp(a) can rise after menopause and considers repeat measurement reasonable when a premenopausal result was borderline. ([European Journal of Preventive Cardiology](https://academic.oup.com/eurjpc/article/32/9/716/7840282); [European Atherosclerosis Society consensus statement](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/43/39/3925/6670882); [2025 ESC/EAS focused update](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/46/42/4359/8234482))

## How much does Lp(a) improve risk assessment?

Lp(a) is independently associated with cardiovascular risk, but its average effect on population-level risk-prediction statistics is modest. ([JAMA Cardiology](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamacardiology/fullarticle/2771455))

In a UK Biobank subgroup of 144,350 participants of White European ancestry classified as being at borderline-to-intermediate predicted risk by QRISK3, adding measured Lp(a) increased the area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve from 0.640 to 0.642. That improvement was statistically significant but small, meaning that Lp(a) does not dramatically reorder risk predictions across the entire population. ([JAMA Cardiology](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamacardiology/fullarticle/2771455))

Its practical value is more likely to occur in particular individuals: someone near a treatment threshold, someone with premature disease not explained by conventional risk factors, or someone whose family history suggests inherited risk. This is why current guidelines use elevated Lp(a) as a **risk modifier** or risk-enhancing factor, particularly when a person is near a treatment threshold. ([2025 ESC/EAS focused update](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/46/42/4359/8234482); [2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001423))

The result also needs to be interpreted alongside the person's existing atherosclerotic burden. In the primary analysis of the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis, elevated Lp(a) was defined as the highest race-specific quintile. Participants with both elevated Lp(a) and coronary calcium of at least 100 had a hazard ratio of 4.71 compared with those with neither finding. Those with elevated Lp(a) but a coronary-calcium score of zero had a hazard ratio of 1.31, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.73 to 2.35. ([Journal of the American College of Cardiology](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacc.2021.11.058))

That finding should not be interpreted as proof that a zero calcium score eliminates all lifetime Lp(a)-associated risk, particularly in younger people. Over the study's follow-up, however, a zero score identified much lower observed risk than the combination of elevated Lp(a) and substantial coronary calcium. The finding indicates that Lp(a)'s near-term absolute significance depends partly on whether atherosclerosis and other risk factors are already present. ([Journal of the American College of Cardiology](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacc.2021.11.058); [European Atherosclerosis Society consensus statement](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/43/39/3925/6670882))

## Is the result actionable without an Lp(a)-specific drug?

Yes, but the strength of the actionability evidence varies. ([2025 ESC/EAS focused update](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/46/42/4359/8234482); [2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001423); [Journal of the American Heart Association](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/JAHA.125.046519))

The 2025 ESC/EAS update recommends considering elevated Lp(a) when refining cardiovascular risk and states that early risk-factor management and more intensive LDL-C lowering are reasonable. The 2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline similarly recommends one-time testing and more intensive management of LDL-C and other modifiable risk factors when Lp(a) is elevated. ([2025 ESC/EAS focused update](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/46/42/4359/8234482); [2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001423))

The best-established rationale is that elevated Lp(a) changes the estimated **baseline risk**. Because the absolute benefit of preventive treatment is generally greater when baseline risk is greater, the same LDL-C or blood-pressure reduction may be more worthwhile in someone with markedly elevated Lp(a). This rationale concerns absolute risk; it should not be interpreted as proof that Lp(a) changes the relative biological effect of lowering LDL-C or blood pressure. ([2025 ESC/EAS focused update](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/46/42/4359/8234482); [2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001423); [European Atherosclerosis Society consensus statement](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/43/39/3925/6670882))

There is emerging observational evidence that testing is associated with changes in clinical behavior. In a 2026 Veterans Affairs cohort, people who underwent Lp(a) testing had approximately twice the odds of lipid-lowering treatment intensification, 27 percent greater odds of subsequent LDL-C testing and 22 percent greater odds of attaining an LDL-C goal than propensity-matched people who were not tested. ([Journal of the American Heart Association](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/JAHA.125.046519))

However, this was not a randomized screening trial. Only 0.1 percent of veterans with LDL-C testing had also undergone Lp(a) testing, suggesting that clinicians selected unusual or higher-risk patients for measurement. Only 12.4 percent of the matched cohort were women, and residual confounding could explain part of the apparent effect. The study was funded by Novartis, two authors were affiliated with the company, and several others disclosed pharmaceutical relationships. The authors reported that the funder had no role in study conduct or reporting. ([Journal of the American Heart Association](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/JAHA.125.046519))

Testing can also prompt cascade testing of first-degree relatives, because high Lp(a) clusters in families. This may identify relatives who warrant earlier assessment and treatment of LDL-C, blood pressure, smoking and other conventional risk factors. ([European Atherosclerosis Society consensus statement](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/43/39/3925/6670882))

Current guidelines and recent reviews do not cite a completed randomized trial comparing universal Lp(a) screening with usual care for myocardial infarction, stroke, cardiovascular death or aortic stenosis. The proposed actionability is supported by causal evidence, risk models, guidelines, observational treatment changes and economic modeling, not by a randomized screening-outcomes trial. ([2025 ESC/EAS focused update](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/46/42/4359/8234482); [2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001423); [Journal of the American Heart Association](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/JAHA.125.046519); [Atherosclerosis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0021915025013450); [Current Atherosclerosis Reports](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11883-026-01453-9))

## Does testing cause harmful anxiety?

The argument that measuring an incompletely modifiable risk factor could create psychological harm is plausible, but the available studies do not establish widespread net harm. ([Journal of Clinical Lipidology](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40883135/); [Lipids in Health and Disease](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12944-025-02785-2))

An online survey of 1,001 people who reported elevated Lp(a) found that 70.7 percent reported no decision regret and 71.7 percent had minimal anxiety. However, participants were self-selected people who already knew their results, so the findings cannot establish how an unselected screening population would respond. Nearly half also reported barriers related to testing or care. ([Journal of Clinical Lipidology](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40883135/))

In the prospective German ELITE cohort, 481 of 841 participants with elevated Lp(a) completed the awareness questionnaire, and 39.3 percent of those respondents reported concern or anxiety. Participants received individualized prevention recommendations, and several risk factors and use of lipid-lowering treatment improved during follow-up, but the study was nonrandomized and could not separate the effects of testing, counseling and repeated clinical contact. ([Lipids in Health and Disease](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12944-025-02785-2))

The available evidence therefore supports neither extreme. Testing is not proven to create more psychological harm than clinical benefit, but anxiety is real for a substantial minority in the available studies. The appropriate response is careful communication: an elevated value is a risk factor, not a prediction that a cardiovascular event is inevitable. ([Journal of Clinical Lipidology](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40883135/); [Lipids in Health and Disease](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12944-025-02785-2); [European Atherosclerosis Society consensus statement](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/43/39/3925/6670882))

## Is universal testing cost-effective?

A modeling study is encouraging, but the evidence remains preliminary. ([Atherosclerosis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0021915025013450))

A 2025 microsimulation study modeled one-time Lp(a) testing in 10,000 adults aged 40 to 69 without cardiovascular disease. People not otherwise categorized as high risk were reclassified as high risk if Lp(a) was at least 105 nmol/L, leading to initiation of a statin and blood-pressure-lowering treatment. Testing changed treatment for 1,807 people. ([Atherosclerosis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0021915025013450))

The model estimated gains of 217 quality-adjusted life-years in Australia and 255 in the United Kingdom. The estimated incremental cost-effectiveness ratio was AUD 12,134 per quality-adjusted life-year in Australia, while testing was cost-saving in the UK model. Societal-perspective savings were estimated at AUD 85 and £263 per person, respectively. ([Atherosclerosis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0021915025013450))

These are modeled results, not observed outcomes from a screening program. They depend on assumptions that identified patients will start treatment, adhere to it, achieve predicted reductions in conventional risk factors and experience the treatment effects used in the simulation. Results from two high-income healthcare systems may not generalize to countries with different test prices, medication access or background cardiovascular risk. ([Atherosclerosis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0021915025013450))

Similarly, this analysis does not establish a single global price for Lp(a) testing. It uses local laboratory and healthcare costs, so cash prices and cost-effectiveness will vary by market and health system. ([Atherosclerosis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0021915025013450))

## What Lp(a)HORIZON can answer

Lp(a)HORIZON is a phase 3, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of pelacarsen 80 mg every four weeks. It enrolled 8,323 people with established cardiovascular disease, including prior myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke or symptomatic peripheral-artery disease, and Lp(a) of at least 70 mg/dL. The trial design paper reports this threshold as approximately 149 nmol/L using the study assay; the pair should not be treated as a universal unit conversion. Its primary endpoint includes cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke and urgent coronary revascularization requiring hospitalization. ([American Heart Journal](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40185318/); [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04023552); [European Atherosclerosis Society consensus statement](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/43/39/3925/6670882))

Pelacarsen is an antisense oligonucleotide that reduces hepatic production of apolipoprotein(a). A phase 2 trial produced a mean Lp(a) reduction of about 80 percent with 20 mg weekly, a regimen that informed later development. Lp(a)HORIZON uses 80 mg every four weeks. ([New England Journal of Medicine](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1905239); [American Heart Journal](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40185318/))

A separate 2026 randomized trial of 51 patients receiving regular lipoprotein apheresis found that pelacarsen produced a placebo-adjusted Lp(a) reduction of 72 percent and substantially reduced the number of apheresis sessions performed under the trial's prespecified protocol. That trial established biological efficacy in a highly selected population; it did not test whether pelacarsen prevents cardiovascular events. It was funded by Novartis, several authors were company employees or held company stock, and medical-writing support was company funded. ([European Heart Journal](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/47/25/3284/8494384))

As of August 15, 2026, no peer-reviewed Lp(a)HORIZON cardiovascular-outcomes result was identifiable. The registry listed the study as active, not recruiting, a 2026 pelacarsen trial publication described HORIZON as ongoing, and a peer-reviewed review published on August 13, 2026 reported that results were expected in late 2026. ([ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04023552); [European Heart Journal](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/47/25/3284/8494384); [Current Atherosclerosis Reports](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11883-026-01453-9))

Because HORIZON enrolled people with established cardiovascular disease and Lp(a) of at least 70 mg/dL, even a clearly positive result would initially apply most directly to that relatively narrow population. It would not by itself demonstrate benefit in: ([American Heart Journal](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40185318/); [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04023552))

- healthy young adults identified through universal screening. ([American Heart Journal](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40185318/); [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04023552))

- people with moderate elevations around 30 to 50 mg/dL. ([American Heart Journal](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40185318/); [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04023552))

- people with elevated Lp(a) but no established cardiovascular disease. ([American Heart Journal](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40185318/); [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04023552))

- prevention or slowing of calcific aortic stenosis. ([American Heart Journal](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40185318/); [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04023552))

- populations receiving the drug for several decades. ([American Heart Journal](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40185318/); [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04023552))


These populations, outcomes and treatment durations are outside the trial's enrolled population, primary endpoint or planned follow-up. ([American Heart Journal](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40185318/); [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04023552))

A positive trial would provide randomized evidence that lowering Lp(a) reduces cardiovascular events in this high-risk secondary-prevention population. It would strengthen, but not complete, the argument for universal screening. ([American Heart Journal](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40185318/); [Current Atherosclerosis Reports](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11883-026-01453-9))

## Industry influence and the "market creation" critique

The clinical interest in Lp(a) cannot reasonably be dismissed as a marker invented to sell a drug. The causal evidence includes genetic association, Mendelian-randomization and prospective cohort studies that are independent of whether pelacarsen or any other drug ultimately succeeds. ([New England Journal of Medicine](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20032323/); [European Atherosclerosis Society consensus statement](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/43/39/3925/6670882))

Nevertheless, commercial influence deserves scrutiny. The pelacarsen studies cited here were industry sponsored, HORIZON is sponsored by Novartis, the apheresis trial was funded by Novartis, and the Veterans Affairs study received Novartis funding and included two company-affiliated authors. The European Atherosclerosis Society consensus statement also discloses relationships between some authors and pharmaceutical companies. Those relationships do not invalidate the evidence, but they increase the importance of preregistered endpoints, complete reporting and independent replication. ([New England Journal of Medicine](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1905239); [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04023552); [European Heart Journal](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/47/25/3284/8494384); [Journal of the American Heart Association](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/JAHA.125.046519); [European Atherosclerosis Society consensus statement](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/43/39/3925/6670882))

There are also safeguards worth noting. The 2025 ESC/EAS focused update states that its development funding came entirely from the two professional societies without healthcare-industry involvement, and that members with conflicts relevant to a topic were asked to abstain from voting on that topic. ([2025 ESC/EAS focused update](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/46/42/4359/8234482))

The more defensible version of the critique is not that Lp(a) lacks clinical significance. It is that the certainty of the causal association can be mistakenly extended to claims that have not yet been demonstrated: that a particular drug will prevent events, that moderate elevations warrant drug treatment, or that universal screening will be cost-effective in every healthcare system. ([American Heart Journal](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40185318/); [Current Atherosclerosis Reports](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11883-026-01453-9); [Atherosclerosis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0021915025013450))

## Lp(a) distribution by ethnicity

Lp(a) concentrations differ substantially among self-reported racial and ethnic groups. In the UK Biobank analysis described below, the association per 50 nmol/L increase appeared broadly similar in White, South Asian and Black participants despite different underlying distributions. ([Arteriosclerosis, Thrombosis, and Vascular Biology](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/ATVBAHA.120.315291))

In 460,506 UK Biobank participants, median concentrations were 19 nmol/L in White participants, 31 nmol/L in South Asian participants, 75 nmol/L in Black participants and 16 nmol/L in Chinese participants. The adjusted hazard ratios for ASCVD per 50 nmol/L increase were 1.11, 1.10 and 1.07 in White, South Asian and Black participants, respectively. ([Arteriosclerosis, Thrombosis, and Vascular Biology](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/ATVBAHA.120.315291))

These findings suggest that higher average concentrations in some populations should not automatically be interpreted as a harmless ancestry-specific norm. At the same time, the categories were self-reported, some minority subgroups were much smaller than the White subgroup, and much of the broader genetic and risk-prediction literature remains disproportionately based on people of European ancestry. ([Arteriosclerosis, Thrombosis, and Vascular Biology](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/ATVBAHA.120.315291); [European Atherosclerosis Society consensus statement](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/43/39/3925/6670882))

## Assessment of the competing claims

### 1. Universal testing should be recommended once

**Largely supported as a clinical-policy recommendation, but not proven by a randomized screening trial.** ([2025 ESC/EAS focused update](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/46/42/4359/8234482); [2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001423))

The test identifies a common, predominantly inherited and causal risk factor; clear low and high results are usually stable; and current European and US guidelines support at least one adult measurement. The missing evidence includes a randomized trial showing that universal screening reduces clinical events and cost-effectiveness studies across a broader range of health systems. ([European Atherosclerosis Society consensus statement](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/43/39/3925/6670882); [European Journal of Preventive Cardiology](https://academic.oup.com/eurjpc/article/32/9/716/7840282); [2025 ESC/EAS focused update](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/46/42/4359/8234482); [2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001423); [Atherosclerosis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0021915025013450))

### 2. Testing creates anxiety without actionable treatment

**Too strong and not supported by the available evidence.** ([Journal of Clinical Lipidology](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40883135/); [Lipids in Health and Disease](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12944-025-02785-2); [European Atherosclerosis Society consensus statement](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/43/39/3925/6670882))

Some people experience meaningful concern, but the available studies do not establish widespread net psychological harm. Elevated Lp(a) can influence treatment of LDL-C and other risk factors, trigger family testing and improve recognition of otherwise unexplained risk. Whether these changes translate into fewer events remains uncertain. ([Journal of Clinical Lipidology](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40883135/); [Lipids in Health and Disease](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12944-025-02785-2); [European Atherosclerosis Society consensus statement](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/43/39/3925/6670882); [Journal of the American Heart Association](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/JAHA.125.046519))

### 3. Elevated Lp(a) changes management of other risk factors

**Supported by guidelines and observational evidence, although not yet by a randomized management trial.** ([2025 ESC/EAS focused update](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/46/42/4359/8234482); [2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001423); [Journal of the American Heart Association](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/JAHA.125.046519))

A high result raises estimated baseline risk and can therefore justify earlier or more intensive treatment of modifiable factors. The Veterans Affairs study found that testing was associated with more lipid-treatment intensification and better LDL-C goal attainment, but causal effects on treatment behavior and cardiovascular events remain unproven. ([2025 ESC/EAS focused update](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/46/42/4359/8234482); [2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001423); [Journal of the American Heart Association](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/JAHA.125.046519))

### 4. Advocacy exaggerates moderate elevations and creates a treatment market

**Partly justified, but it should not be extended to denying Lp(a)'s causal importance.** ([European Atherosclerosis Society consensus statement](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/43/39/3925/6670882); [JAMA Cardiology](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamacardiology/fullarticle/2771455); [Current Atherosclerosis Reports](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11883-026-01453-9))

Risk rises continuously rather than beginning abruptly at 50 mg/dL, so moderate elevations are not biologically meaningless. However, average improvement in risk-model discrimination is small, absolute risk depends heavily on the rest of the clinical picture, and there is no completed outcomes evidence supporting an Lp(a)-specific drug for moderate elevations. Industry relationships make restraint especially important when extrapolating beyond completed trials. ([2025 ESC/EAS focused update](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/46/42/4359/8234482); [JAMA Cardiology](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamacardiology/fullarticle/2771455); [American Heart Journal](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40185318/); [Current Atherosclerosis Reports](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11883-026-01453-9); [European Heart Journal](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/47/25/3284/8494384); [Journal of the American Heart Association](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/JAHA.125.046519))

## Quick reference values

- **Below 30 mg/dL or 75 nmol/L:** generally categorized as low risk by the 2024 National Lipid Association update. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38565461/))

- **30 to 50 mg/dL or 75 to 125 nmol/L:** intermediate range; the risk increase is generally modest, and repeat measurement may be useful when management depends on the result. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38565461/); [European Journal of Preventive Cardiology](https://academic.oup.com/eurjpc/article/32/9/716/7840282); [2025 ESC/EAS focused update](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/46/42/4359/8234482))

- **At least 50 mg/dL or 125 nmol/L:** high Lp(a) category and a risk-enhancing level in the National Lipid Association framework. The ESC/EAS guideline uses approximately 105 nmol/L with the same 50 mg/dL threshold, illustrating why mg/dL and nmol/L should not be converted mechanically. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38565461/); [2025 ESC/EAS focused update](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/46/42/4359/8234482); [European Atherosclerosis Society consensus statement](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/43/39/3925/6670882))

- **Very high concentrations:** carry progressively greater risk; there is no biological cliff at a single threshold. ([2025 ESC/EAS focused update](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/46/42/4359/8234482); [European Atherosclerosis Society consensus statement](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/43/39/3925/6670882))


## Practical interpretation

- Most adults should be offered one Lp(a) measurement, preferably when a routine lipid profile is already being obtained, with interpretation and counseling based on absolute cardiovascular risk. ([2025 ESC/EAS focused update](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/46/42/4359/8234482); [2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001423); [European Atherosclerosis Society consensus statement](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/43/39/3925/6670882))

- A clearly low or clearly high result usually does not require routine repetition. A borderline result may merit confirmation, particularly when it would change treatment or when measured before menopause. ([European Journal of Preventive Cardiology](https://academic.oup.com/eurjpc/article/32/9/716/7840282); [2025 ESC/EAS focused update](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/46/42/4359/8234482))

- Elevated Lp(a) should be presented as a **risk modifier**, not a disease diagnosis or a guarantee of a future event. Its importance depends on age, family history, LDL-C, blood pressure, smoking, diabetes, existing cardiovascular disease and evidence of atherosclerosis. ([European Atherosclerosis Society consensus statement](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/43/39/3925/6670882); [Journal of the American College of Cardiology](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacc.2021.11.058))

- The current action is to optimize established preventive measures, especially LDL-C reduction, blood-pressure control, smoking cessation and diabetes management, and to consider testing close relatives. Lifestyle changes remain worthwhile because they reduce overall cardiovascular risk even though they do not substantially change the Lp(a) number. ([2025 ESC/EAS focused update](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/46/42/4359/8234482); [European Atherosclerosis Society consensus statement](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/43/39/3925/6670882))

- No one should be promised that lowering Lp(a) pharmacologically will prevent an event until a cardiovascular-outcomes trial demonstrates that benefit. HORIZON is designed to answer that question in high-risk secondary prevention, not in every person found through one-time screening. ([American Heart Journal](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40185318/); [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04023552); [Current Atherosclerosis Reports](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11883-026-01453-9))


The best-supported conclusion is therefore: **most adults should know their Lp(a), but they should also know what the result can and cannot currently tell them.** One-time testing is a reasonable method of identifying inherited cardiovascular risk. It becomes clinically useful when the result leads to proportionate prevention rather than alarm, and when causal evidence about the biomarker is not confused with still-pending evidence about Lp(a)-lowering drugs. ([2025 ESC/EAS focused update](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/46/42/4359/8234482); [2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001423); [European Atherosclerosis Society consensus statement](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/43/39/3925/6670882); [Current Atherosclerosis Reports](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11883-026-01453-9))
