# Omega-3 for Cardiovascular Prevention: EPA, DHA, and the REDUCE-IT Question

“Omega-3” is not one intervention. Low-dose mixtures of eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), prescription icosapent ethyl, and high-dose EPA+DHA preparations in specialized populations have produced different results. The trial record does not support routine over-the-counter fish oil for cardiovascular prevention, but neither does it justify concluding that every omega-3 formulation is ineffective. ([Cochrane review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32114706/); [REDUCE-IT](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415628/); [STRENGTH](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33190147/); [PISCES](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41201837/))

The strongest positive evidence remains REDUCE-IT, which studied 4 g/day of prescription icosapent ethyl in selected statin-treated patients. Its 25% relative reduction in cardiovascular events was clinically important, but the mineral-oil comparator arm showed adverse changes in several lipid and inflammatory biomarkers. Those findings make a biologically relevant comparator effect plausible, although the trial did not include a neutral third arm that could identify or quantify such an effect. The most defensible interpretation is therefore narrower than either extreme: icosapent ethyl probably has cardiovascular activity in REDUCE-IT-like patients, but the magnitude that would have been seen against a genuinely neutral comparator remains uncertain. ([REDUCE-IT](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415628/); [REDUCE-IT biomarker substudy](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35762321/); [Copenhagen modeling analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34455435/))

## The Greenland Inuit origin story is weaker than often presented

The familiar history begins with reports that Greenland Inuit consumed large amounts of marine fat while experiencing unusually little coronary disease. That observation helped generate decades of research into EPA, DHA, platelet function, triglycerides, arrhythmias, inflammation, and arterial biology. ([Historical reappraisal](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12535749/))

Later historical and epidemiologic reappraisals found that the original studies did not reliably establish a low incidence of coronary disease. Death certification, access to medical care, diagnostic ascertainment, population size, and selective interpretation all limited the early evidence; cardiovascular mortality as a whole was not clearly low. The Inuit story was therefore a useful hypothesis-generating observation, not proof that fish oil prevents cardiovascular disease. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12535749/))

## Low-dose supplementation: mostly a negative cardiovascular story

VITAL randomized 25,871 generally healthy adults to 1 g/day of marine omega-3 oil, providing approximately 460 mg EPA and 380 mg DHA, or placebo. Over a median follow-up of about 5 years, supplementation did not significantly reduce the primary composite of myocardial infarction, stroke, or cardiovascular death. A reduction in myocardial infarction appeared among secondary analyses, but the trial did not establish a reduction in its primary cardiovascular endpoint. VITAL was publicly funded; the omega-3 and placebo capsules were donated by Pronova/BASF. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415637/))

ASCEND randomized 15,480 adults with diabetes but no known cardiovascular disease to approximately 1 g/day of omega-3 oil or placebo. Serious vascular events occurred in 8.9% of the omega-3 group and 9.2% of the placebo group, a nonsignificant difference. The mean follow-up was 7.4 years. Given its large sample, substantial event count, long follow-up, near-null point estimate, and confidence interval, ASCEND makes a large protective effect from this regimen unlikely. ASCEND was principally supported by nonprofit and public research funders; the omega-3 and matching placebo capsules, along with some funding for packaging, were provided by Mylan and previously by Solvay/Abbott. ([New England Journal of Medicine](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1804989))

A 2020 Cochrane review of 86 randomized trials involving 162,796 participants concluded that increasing EPA and DHA had little or no effect on all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, cardiovascular events, stroke, or arrhythmia. Some broader meta-analyses have found small reductions in coronary events, particularly when EPA-only studies are included, so “no biological effect whatsoever” would be too strong. The aggregate evidence nevertheless does not support an ordinary 1-g mixed fish-oil capsule as a reliable way to prevent major cardiovascular events. ([Cochrane review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32114706/); [38-trial meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34505026/))

The conclusion applies to cardiovascular-event prevention, not to whether fish belongs in a healthy dietary pattern. Eating fish, taking a low-dose supplement, and receiving a prescription omega-3 drug are different interventions. ([American Heart Association seafood advisory](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29773586/); [2023 US chronic coronary disease guideline](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001168); [REDUCE-IT](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415628/))

## REDUCE-IT tested a different drug, dose, and population

REDUCE-IT randomized 8,179 statin-treated patients with fasting triglycerides of 135 to 499 mg/dL and LDL cholesterol of 41 to 100 mg/dL. Participants had established cardiovascular disease or diabetes plus additional risk factors. They received either icosapent ethyl, an EPA-only ethyl-ester formulation given at 4 g/day, or a mineral-oil comparator. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415628/))

After a median 4.9 years, the primary composite endpoint occurred in 17.2% of the icosapent-ethyl group and 22.0% of the comparator group. The hazard ratio was 0.75, corresponding to a 25% relative reduction, a 4.8-percentage-point absolute reduction, and a number needed to treat of approximately 21 over the trial period. The observed effect was not readily explained by the modest triglyceride difference alone. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415628/))

The positive result came with tradeoffs. Hospitalization for atrial fibrillation or flutter occurred in 3.1% versus 2.1%, while serious bleeding occurred in 2.7% versus 2.1%; the bleeding difference did not meet conventional statistical significance. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415628/))

**Funding disclosure:** REDUCE-IT was sponsored by Amarin, the manufacturer of icosapent ethyl. Company personnel participated in the clinical-development program, and several investigators disclosed financial relationships with Amarin. Manufacturer sponsorship does not negate randomization or the observed result, but it makes independent replication and comparator choice especially important. ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7004453/))

## STRENGTH produced a discordant null result

STRENGTH enrolled 13,078 statin-treated patients with high cardiovascular risk, hypertriglyceridemia, and low HDL cholesterol. It compared 4 g/day of an omega-3 carboxylic-acid formulation containing EPA plus DHA with a corn-oil comparator. The trial was stopped early for futility: the primary cardiovascular endpoint occurred in 12.0% of the omega-3 group and 12.2% of the comparator group, giving a hazard ratio of 0.99. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33190147/))

A subsequent analysis examined achieved blood concentrations rather than treatment assignment alone. Patients reaching the highest EPA levels did not have fewer cardiovascular events, and those reaching the highest DHA levels did not have more events. Because this was an exploratory analysis rather than a separately randomized comparison of EPA and DHA, it cannot settle the mechanism, but it argues against the simple proposition that more circulating EPA necessarily protects while DHA necessarily harms. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33993205/))

**Funding disclosure:** STRENGTH was sponsored by AstraZeneca, which developed the tested EPA+DHA product. REDUCE-IT and STRENGTH were therefore both industry-sponsored: one strongly positive and one clearly negative. ([STRENGTH](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33190147/); [REDUCE-IT](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415628/))

The divergent findings could reflect formulation, EPA exposure, population differences, comparator oils, biological effects not captured by triglycerides, or chance. REDUCE-IT and STRENGTH were not a randomized head-to-head test of EPA-only therapy versus EPA+DHA, so their contrast cannot by itself prove that DHA canceled EPA’s benefit. ([REDUCE-IT](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415628/); [STRENGTH](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33190147/); [STRENGTH achieved-level analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33993205/); [direct EPA-versus-DHA systematic review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2024.1423228/full))

## The mineral-oil placebo question

The REDUCE-IT biomarker substudy found that seven atherosclerosis-related biomarkers rose in participants receiving mineral oil: interleukin-1β, interleukin-6, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, oxidized LDL cholesterol, homocysteine, lipoprotein(a), and lipoprotein-associated phospholipase A2. LDL cholesterol also rose in the mineral-oil arm in the parent trial. Participants receiving icosapent ethyl generally had much smaller changes. The between-group biomarker differences therefore largely reflected increases in the comparator arm rather than decreases in the active arm. ([REDUCE-IT biomarker substudy](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35762321/); [REDUCE-IT](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415628/))

This matters because REDUCE-IT directly estimated the effect of icosapent ethyl relative to mineral oil. If mineral oil worsened cardiovascular risk, the observed hazard ratio would combine benefit from EPA with harm from the comparator rather than measuring EPA against a truly inert control. Because the trial did not include a neutral third arm, however, the biomarker pattern cannot determine whether mineral oil caused the changes, icosapent ethyl prevented changes that would otherwise have occurred, or both. ([REDUCE-IT](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415628/); [REDUCE-IT biomarker substudy](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35762321/))

An independent analysis used the Copenhagen General Population Study to model the cardiovascular consequences of the triglyceride, LDL-cholesterol, and C-reactive-protein changes reported in REDUCE-IT and STRENGTH. It estimated an active-versus-comparator hazard ratio of approximately 0.88 from those measured changes, compared with REDUCE-IT’s observed hazard ratio of 0.75. The authors concluded that comparator effects could explain part, but not all, of the REDUCE-IT-STRENGTH divergence. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34455435/))

That analysis is informative but not definitive. It reconstructed trial-like populations from an observational cohort, assumed that changes in three biomarkers translated into risk in a particular way, and could not account for every possible biological effect of either oil. It shows that a partial comparator contribution is plausible; it does not show that REDUCE-IT’s entire benefit was an artifact. ([Copenhagen modeling analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34455435/))

The ideal resolution would be another adequately powered cardiovascular-outcome trial comparing icosapent ethyl with a demonstrably neutral oil or with a no-oil placebo under otherwise identical conditions. REDUCE-IT therefore remains the pivotal cardiovascular-outcome trial for this exact formulation and population. ([REDUCE-IT](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415628/); [2025 ESC/EAS focused dyslipidemia update](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40878289/); [2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001423))

## Does DHA neutralize EPA?

There are plausible pharmacologic differences between EPA and DHA. DHA can raise LDL cholesterol in some hypertriglyceridemic patients, whereas purified EPA usually does not. Direct comparative trials also report differences in triglycerides, blood pressure, heart rate, and some platelet-related and inflammatory measures. These observations make an EPA-specific effect possible, but mechanisms and surrogate outcomes cannot establish clinical-event superiority. ([Direct EPA-versus-DHA systematic review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2024.1423228/full))

A 2024 systematic review identified 24 publications from nine randomized trials directly comparing at least 2 g/day of near-pure EPA with near-pure DHA. Both reduced triglycerides and influenced oxidative, inflammatory, and platelet-related measures. DHA generally produced a slightly larger triglyceride reduction and appeared more effective for lowering blood pressure and heart rate, although it was also more likely to raise LDL cholesterol. The evidence base was small and consisted of surrogate outcomes rather than cardiovascular-event trials. ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2024.1423228/full))

That review received no financial support and reported no commercial or financial conflicts of interest. It found differential effects, not a consistent pattern in which EPA was favorable and DHA antagonistic. ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2024.1423228/full))

The PISCES trial further weakens a universal “DHA cancels EPA” theory. Published online in November 2025 and in print in January 2026, PISCES randomized 1,228 maintenance-hemodialysis patients to 4 g/day of fish oil containing 1.6 g EPA and 0.8 g DHA or corn oil. Over 3.5 years, the rate of serious cardiovascular events was lower with EPA+DHA, with a hazard ratio of 0.57. The study was principally supported by the Heart and Stroke Foundation of Canada and other public or nonprofit sources; DSM, previously Ocean Nutrition Canada, supplied the active and placebo capsules in kind. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41201837/); [New England Journal of Medicine](https://www.nejm.org/doi/abs/10.1056/NEJMoa2513032))

PISCES does not rehabilitate routine fish-oil supplementation in the general population. Hemodialysis patients have exceptionally high cardiovascular risk, altered fatty-acid biology, and mechanisms of cardiovascular disease that differ from ordinary primary-prevention populations. Its primary endpoint measured the rate of recurrent serious events rather than exactly reproducing REDUCE-IT’s time-to-first-event composite; a separate time-to-first cardiovascular event or death analysis was less extreme, with a hazard ratio of 0.73. It does, however, show that an EPA+DHA formulation can be beneficial relative to corn oil in at least one defined clinical setting. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41201837/))

## Other EPA-only cardiovascular trials

JELIS randomized 18,645 Japanese patients with hypercholesterolemia to statin therapy alone or statin therapy plus 1.8 g/day of EPA. It reported a 19% relative reduction in major coronary events. JELIS was open-label, used pravastatin 10 mg or simvastatin 5 mg, and was conducted in a population with high baseline fish consumption. Its primary-prevention subgroup did not independently demonstrate a statistically significant benefit. ([JELIS](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17398308/))

**Funding disclosure:** Mochida Pharmaceutical, the manufacturer of the EPA product, supplied study medication and supported the trial; the sponsor was responsible for data collection and analysis. The investigators retained responsibility for the study concept and interpretation, but the commercial role should be considered. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20145342/))

RESPECT-EPA randomized 2,506 Japanese patients with chronic coronary disease, low EPA-to-arachidonic-acid ratios, and statin treatment; 2,460 were included in the full analysis set. Adding 1.8 g/day of EPA produced a borderline reduction in the broad primary endpoint that did not meet the conventional threshold for statistical significance. A narrower secondary coronary endpoint was significantly reduced. New atrial fibrillation occurred more often in the EPA group. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38873793/))

RESPECT-EPA was supported by the Japan Heart Foundation rather than the product manufacturer, although several investigators disclosed pharmaceutical relationships. It provides some independent support for EPA-only treatment, but because the primary result narrowly missed significance and the study was open-label, not placebo-controlled, and underpowered relative to its original assumptions, it is not a clean replication of REDUCE-IT. ([AHA Journals](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.123.065520))

Taken together, JELIS, REDUCE-IT, and RESPECT-EPA make an EPA-specific cardiovascular effect credible. They do not establish that icosapent ethyl would reproduce a 25% relative reduction against an inert placebo in every eligible population. ([JELIS](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17398308/); [REDUCE-IT](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415628/); [RESPECT-EPA](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38873793/))

## Current clinical position

The FDA added a cardiovascular risk-reduction indication for icosapent ethyl on **December 13, 2019**, based principally on REDUCE-IT. ([Approval perspective](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7584545/); [REDUCE-IT](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415628/))

The 2025 ESC/EAS focused dyslipidemia update states that icosapent ethyl at 2 g twice daily should be considered, in addition to statin therapy, for high- or very-high-risk patients with triglycerides of approximately 135 to 499 mg/dL. This is a targeted recommendation, not an endorsement of ordinary fish-oil supplements. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40878289/))

The 2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline likewise limits consideration of icosapent ethyl to selected high-risk, statin-treated adults with persistent moderate hypertriglyceridemia. It does not generalize REDUCE-IT to low-risk adults or to nonprescription EPA+DHA products. ([PubMed](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001423))

The 2023 US chronic coronary disease guideline explicitly concluded that adding nonprescription omega-3 or fish-oil supplements to statin therapy is not beneficial for reducing cardiovascular risk. ([AHA Journals](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001168))

## Atrial fibrillation and bleeding

A meta-analysis of cardiovascular-outcome trials found that long-term marine omega-3 supplementation increased atrial-fibrillation risk by approximately 25%, with stronger signals in trials using higher doses. This pattern appeared in both EPA-only and mixed-formulation trials, supporting a safety signal across formulations rather than a DHA-specific problem. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34612056/))

A separate meta-analysis found no statistically significant increase in bleeding across omega-3 trials overall. High-dose purified EPA was associated with a small absolute excess, approximately six additional bleeding events per 1,000 treated patients in that analysis, with risk influenced by dose rather than background antiplatelet therapy alone. **Funding disclosure:** the meta-analysis received an educational grant from Amarin, although the authors reported no personal conflicts; the funding source should be kept in mind. ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11179820/))

For an individual patient, atrial-fibrillation history, anticoagulant and antiplatelet use, bleeding risk, triglyceride level, baseline cardiovascular risk, and expected absolute benefit should all be weighed in treatment decisions. ([Atrial-fibrillation meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34612056/); [bleeding meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11179820/); [2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001423))

## Effects beyond cardiovascular-event prevention

### Triglycerides

Triglyceride lowering is the best-established non-event indication. Prescription omega-3 doses around 4 g/day commonly lower triglycerides by approximately 20% to 30% in moderate hypertriglyceridemia and by 30% or more when baseline levels are very high. EPA+DHA products may raise LDL cholesterol in severe hypertriglyceridemia, whereas EPA-only formulations generally do not. Dose-response analyses indicate that triglyceride lowering becomes substantially more pronounced above approximately 2 g/day. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31422671/))

This is a pharmacologic use, not evidence that a low-dose supplement prevents heart attacks. Triglyceride lowering is also a surrogate outcome: formulations that lower triglycerides have not consistently reduced cardiovascular events. ([American Heart Association triglyceride advisory](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31422671/); [REDUCE-IT](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415628/); [STRENGTH](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33190147/))

### Cognition

Randomized evidence does not establish that omega-3 supplements prevent cognitive decline or dementia in cognitively healthy older adults. A 2024 meta-analysis reported possible benefits in selected cognitive domains, particularly executive function, but effects were heterogeneous; that analysis did not evaluate incident dementia. ([Cochrane prevention review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22696350/); [2024 cognition meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38468309/))

PreventE4 subsequently showed that high-dose DHA increased cerebrospinal-fluid DHA and related target-engagement measures, but that biochemical target engagement did not translate into meaningful improvement in cognition or hippocampal-volume outcomes over the trial period. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42315445/))

### Depression

VITAL-DEP randomized more than 18,000 older adults to 1 g/day of marine omega-3 or placebo. It found no preventive benefit. Depression or clinically relevant depressive symptoms were slightly more frequent with omega-3 supplementation, with a hazard ratio of 1.13 and a 95% confidence interval of 1.01 to 1.26, while longitudinal mood scores did not differ significantly. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34932079/))

For treatment of established depression, a Cochrane review found a small average symptom reduction, but the estimated effect was below a commonly used threshold for clinical importance and the certainty of evidence was very low. Omega-3 may remain a research question or adjunct in selected patients, but it is not a well-established stand-alone antidepressant treatment. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34817851/))

### Inflammation and joint symptoms

Meta-analyses have sometimes found modest reductions in C-reactive protein, tumor necrosis factor, or interleukin-6, while other analyses have found no significant effect on one or more of those markers. Differences in dose, population, background inflammation, formulation, duration, and analytic methods probably contribute to the inconsistent findings. Changes in inflammatory biomarkers are not equivalent to improvements in symptoms or disease outcomes. ([Umbrella meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35914448/); [2024 type 2 diabetes meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39243880/))

Trials and reviews in rheumatoid arthritis suggest small improvements in some pain, morning-stiffness, or swollen-joint measures, but results are heterogeneous and several clinically important measures do not consistently improve. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28067815/)) Osteoarthritis meta-analyses are likewise mixed, with some reporting symptom improvement and others finding no clear benefit. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37226250/); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35458170/)) These findings do not support a blanket “anti-inflammatory” or “joint health” claim for ordinary fish-oil supplements.

- **ASCEND:** 15,480 adults with diabetes; approximately 1 g/day; serious vascular events 8.9% versus 9.2%, not significant. ([ASCEND](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1804989))

- **REDUCE-IT:** 8,179 high-risk statin-treated patients; 4 g/day icosapent ethyl; primary events 17.2% versus 22.0%; hazard ratio 0.75; approximate number needed to treat 21. ([REDUCE-IT](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415628/))

- **STRENGTH:** 13,078 high-risk statin-treated patients; 4 g/day EPA+DHA; primary events 12.0% versus 12.2%; hazard ratio 0.99. ([STRENGTH](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33190147/))

- **RESPECT-EPA:** 2,506 patients randomized and 2,460 included in the full analysis set; 1.8 g/day EPA; primary endpoint borderline and not conventionally significant, with a favorable secondary coronary endpoint. ([RESPECT-EPA](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38873793/))

- **PISCES:** 1,228 maintenance-hemodialysis patients; 4 g/day preparation containing 1.6 g EPA and 0.8 g DHA; serious cardiovascular-event rate hazard ratio 0.57. ([PISCES](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41201837/))

- **Important adverse effect:** high-dose omega-3 treatment increases atrial-fibrillation risk; major bleeding risk appears small but may rise modestly with high-dose purified EPA. ([Atrial-fibrillation meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34612056/); [bleeding meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11179820/))


## Verdict on the competing claims

### 1. “REDUCE-IT is real and pharmacologically specific to EPA-only therapy”

**Partly supported, but overstated.**

REDUCE-IT was a large, randomized, clinically important positive trial, supported by the earlier JELIS result and partially by RESPECT-EPA. Current guidelines reasonably support icosapent ethyl for patients resembling those enrolled in REDUCE-IT. ([REDUCE-IT](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415628/); [JELIS](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17398308/); [RESPECT-EPA](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38873793/); [2025 ESC/EAS focused dyslipidemia update](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40878289/); [2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001423))

What has not been established is that DHA neutralizes EPA, that every eligible patient should receive the drug, or that the full 25% relative reduction would recur against an inert comparator. Direct EPA-versus-DHA trials address surrogate risk factors rather than cardiovascular events, STRENGTH’s achieved-level analysis did not support DHA antagonism, and PISCES found a large benefit from EPA+DHA in hemodialysis. ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2024.1423228/full); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33993205/); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41201837/))

### 2. “The REDUCE-IT benefit is partly or wholly attributable to mineral oil”

**Partial contribution is plausible; complete attribution is unsupported.**

The mineral-oil arm experienced adverse changes in several lipid and inflammatory biomarkers associated with cardiovascular risk, and independent modeling indicates that comparator effects could explain a meaningful portion of the observed contrast. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35762321/); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34455435/))

The model did not reproduce the entire REDUCE-IT effect, however, and it was not a randomized comparison of mineral oil with an inert placebo. The biomarker data make a biologically relevant comparator effect plausible, but without a neutral third arm they cannot establish mineral oil’s causal contribution. “Mineral oil explains everything” goes beyond the evidence. ([Copenhagen modeling analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34455435/); [REDUCE-IT biomarker substudy](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35762321/); [REDUCE-IT](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415628/))

### 3. “OTC fish oil should be removed from cardiovascular-prevention recommendations”

**Supported.**

VITAL, ASCEND, Cochrane evidence, STRENGTH, and current guidelines do not support recommending ordinary low-dose fish-oil supplements to prevent cardiovascular events. Continuing to present a standard 1-g capsule as a proven heart-attack-prevention strategy conflates mechanistic plausibility, triglyceride lowering at higher doses, dietary fish, and prescription icosapent ethyl. ([VITAL](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415637/); [ASCEND](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1804989); [Cochrane review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32114706/); [STRENGTH](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33190147/); [2023 US chronic coronary disease guideline](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001168))

This conclusion should not be broadened into advising patients to discontinue a prescribed omega-3 drug or into dismissing fish as food. ([REDUCE-IT](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415628/); [2025 ESC/EAS focused dyslipidemia update](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40878289/); [American Heart Association seafood advisory](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29773586/))

### 4. “Non-cardiovascular uses justify a broader view”

**Correct in principle, but the evidence is indication-specific.**

Omega-3 supplementation should not be judged solely by cardiovascular events when it is being used to treat high triglycerides. That indication is strongly supported at prescription doses. Evidence for preventing cognitive decline or depression is not convincing, while treatment effects in depression, rheumatoid arthritis, osteoarthritis, and inflammatory conditions are generally small, uncertain, heterogeneous, or population-specific. ([American Heart Association triglyceride advisory](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31422671/); [Cochrane cognition review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22696350/); [VITAL-DEP](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34932079/); [Cochrane depression review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34817851/); [arthritis review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28067815/); [osteoarthritis meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37226250/); [inflammation umbrella meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35914448/))

A failed cardiovascular claim does not automatically disprove every other use, but a plausible mechanism or favorable biomarker does not establish a clinically meaningful benefit either. ([American Heart Association triglyceride advisory](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31422671/); [2024 cognition meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38468309/); [Cochrane depression review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34817851/); [inflammation umbrella meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35914448/))

## Practical interpretation

- A routine low-dose OTC fish-oil capsule should not be recommended as a proven method of preventing myocardial infarction, stroke, or cardiovascular death. ([Cochrane review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32114706/); [2023 US chronic coronary disease guideline](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001168))

- Prescription icosapent ethyl is a reasonable option to discuss for selected high-risk, statin-treated patients with persistent triglyceride elevation who resemble the REDUCE-IT population. Its evidence should not be extrapolated to other fish-oil products. ([REDUCE-IT](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415628/); [2025 ESC/EAS focused dyslipidemia update](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40878289/); [2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001423))

- Expected absolute benefit is greatest in patients with high baseline cardiovascular risk. Atrial-fibrillation and bleeding risks should be considered explicitly. ([REDUCE-IT](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415628/); [atrial-fibrillation meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34612056/); [bleeding meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11179820/))

- Severe or persistent hypertriglyceridemia is a separate therapeutic problem that requires clinician-directed evaluation and standardized therapy. Prescription omega-3 is one treatment option, but an ordinary OTC supplement should not be treated as an equivalent substitute. ([American College of Cardiology expert consensus pathway](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34332805/); [American Heart Association triglyceride advisory](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31422671/))

- Claims involving cognition, mood, inflammation, or joint health should be evaluated against trials for that specific outcome, not inferred from triglyceride changes or cardiovascular mechanisms. ([Cochrane cognition review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22696350/); [VITAL-DEP](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34932079/); [inflammation umbrella meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35914448/); [arthritis review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28067815/))
