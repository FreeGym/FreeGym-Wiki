# Should GLP-1s Be Stopped Before Surgery or Endoscopy?

GLP-1-based drugs can delay gastric emptying, so some patients retain food or fluid despite following standard fasting instructions. The evidence supports a real increase in residual gastric contents, but current observational evidence has not detected a corresponding increase in pulmonary aspiration. ([Elkin et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/anae.16601); [Chen et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2025.0081))

This distinction matters. Retained gastric content is a plausible risk factor and can lead to a delayed or canceled procedure, but it is a surrogate outcome rather than aspiration itself. The available evidence does not support either extreme: routinely stopping every GLP-1 drug before every procedure, or continuing treatment without considering the patient, treatment phase, symptoms, and procedure. A more defensible approach is individualized risk assessment, with selective withholding, a preprocedure liquid diet, gastric ultrasound, or airway precautions when the expected risk is higher. ([Kindel et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00464-024-11263-2); [Hocking et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1177/0310057X251355288))

## Residual gastric content is not the same as aspiration

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis included 28 observational studies examining perioperative outcomes in people using GLP-1 receptor agonists. GLP-1 use was associated with substantially higher odds of residual gastric content: across 18 studies involving 165,522 patients, the pooled odds ratio was 5.96. ([Elkin et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/anae.16601))

In the same review, nine studies involving 185,414 patients and 471 aspiration events did not detect higher odds of pulmonary aspiration. The pooled odds ratio was 1.04, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.87 to 1.25. The certainty of this evidence was rated low because the included studies were observational and aspiration was uncommon, so the estimate does not establish equivalence or zero risk. ([Elkin et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/anae.16601))

The review also found that withholding at least one scheduled dose was associated with lower odds of residual gastric content, with a pooled odds ratio of 0.51. That estimate came from only five observational studies involving 1,706 patients and was rated very low certainty, so it did not establish the best withholding interval or prove that withholding prevents aspiration. ([Elkin et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/anae.16601))

## What prospective gastric-emptying studies show

A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis pooled ten prospective studies involving 300 participants. Compared with control treatment, GLP-1 receptor agonists increased the measured gastric-emptying half-time by an average of approximately 74 minutes, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 46 to 101 minutes. The studies used methods such as scintigraphy and breath testing rather than relying only on symptoms or retrospective endoscopy records. ([Chen et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12630-026-03114-6))

The estimated delay was greater with short-acting drugs than with long-acting drugs: approximately 116 minutes versus 39 minutes. Treatment lasting less than ten weeks was associated with an estimated delay of 82 minutes, compared with 48 minutes after longer treatment. These subgroup findings are consistent with a stronger effect during early treatment and some attenuation over time, but they should not be interpreted as precise waiting periods for individual patients. ([Chen et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12630-026-03114-6))

The certainty of these estimates was rated very low because the studies were small and highly heterogeneous (I² = 97.7 percent), with risks of publication bias and indirectness. Five studies examined liraglutide, three examined lixisenatide, two examined exenatide, and only one included semaglutide; none directly examined tirzepatide. The apparent short-acting versus long-acting difference therefore cannot be assumed to describe every modern weekly drug. ([Chen et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12630-026-03114-6))

## Standard fasting may not produce an empty stomach

A 2024 prospective cross-sectional study used gastric ultrasound in 124 appropriately fasted patients undergoing elective procedures. Increased residual gastric content was found in 56 percent of patients using a weekly GLP-1 receptor agonist, compared with 19 percent of nonusers. After adjustment, GLP-1 use was associated with a prevalence ratio of 2.48 and an absolute prevalence difference of approximately 31 percentage points. ([Sen et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamasurg.2024.0111))

The study did not find a clear relationship between the number of days the drug had been interrupted and residual content. That does not prove withholding is ineffective: only 62 participants were GLP-1 users, interruption was not randomized, and the study was not designed to determine an optimal washout period. ([Sen et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamasurg.2024.0111))

The prospective [RESIDUAL study](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12871-026-03999-2), published in 2026, examined 93 patients after weekly injectable GLP-1 drugs had been withheld for at least seven days. Residual gastric content was detected in 42 percent of the 52 previous GLP-1 users and 24 percent of 41 nonusers. The unadjusted prevalence ratio was 1.73, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.96 to 3.75; after adjustment, it was 1.65, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.70 to 3.70, and the adjusted absolute prevalence difference was 15.4 percentage points, with a 95 percent confidence interval of -10.0 to 35.6. The study was underpowered and statistically inconclusive, but its findings show descriptively that a seven-day hold does not guarantee an empty stomach. ([Boudreau et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12871-026-03999-2))

## The first randomized withholding trial

The [2026 OCULUS trial](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2026.0027) directly randomized 60 patients undergoing elective upper endoscopy to either continue treatment or withhold one scheduled dose. Participants had been on a stable dose for at least one month. For weekly drugs, the withholding group had been medication-free for 7 to 13 days; daily drugs were withheld for at least one day. ([Ahmad et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2026.0027))

Clinically significant residual gastric volume was found in 25.0 percent of patients who continued treatment and 3.1 percent of those who withheld a dose. The absolute difference was 21.9 percentage points, with a 90 percent confidence interval of 7.0 to 36.7 percentage points, and the trial was stopped early after crossing its prespecified interim stopping boundary. ([Ahmad et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2026.0027))

Among patients having upper endoscopy alone after an ordinary diet and standard fasting, residual volume was detected in 46.7 percent of the continuation group and 5.0 percent of the withholding group. No cases were detected among patients having combined upper endoscopy and colonoscopy, but that subgroup contained only 25 patients and had also completed a 24-hour clear-liquid diet and bowel preparation. The effects of the liquid diet, bowel preparation, and procedure selection therefore cannot be separated. ([Ahmad et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2026.0027))

All patients with clinically significant residual volume were asymptomatic. All primary-outcome events were retained contents that precluded an adequate endoscopic examination. No aspiration, unplanned intubation, prolonged monitoring, or hospitalization occurred in either group. Symptoms did not exclude residual content in this trial, while the trial was much too small to determine whether withholding reduces aspiration. ([Ahmad et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2026.0027))

Two published corrections fixed transposed continuation and withholding labels in the visual abstract and Figure 2. The group assignments and values reported here follow the corrected article. ([Visual abstract correction, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2026.1710); [Figure correction, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2026.2322))

The study also excluded patients with documented gastroparesis, achalasia, gastric outlet obstruction, previous foregut surgery, chronic opioid use, or planned general anesthesia. Its results most directly show that withholding one dose reduced clinically significant residual gastric volume before elective upper endoscopy in selected, stable-dose patients; they do not establish a universal rule for major surgery, emergency procedures, or patients with known gastric dysmotility. ([Ahmad et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2026.0027))

## Does GLP-1 use increase aspiration?

In addition to the 2025 meta-analysis, a large claims-based cohort study examined 366,476 surgical patients, including 5,931 who had a GLP-1 prescription within 30 days before surgery. Claims-coded postoperative aspiration pneumonia occurred in 0.7 percent of GLP-1 users and 0.6 percent of nonusers. After adjustment, the study did not detect a statistically significant increase in aspiration pneumonia: the odds ratio was 0.78, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.57 to 1.06. ([Chen et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2025.0081))

The study could not confirm whether patients actually took the drug immediately before surgery, whether doses were withheld, whether fasting instructions were followed, or whether procedures were canceled because of gastric contents. Claims data also provide limited detail about anesthesia technique and airway management. The result is reassuring at the population level, but it does not establish equivalent risk or prove that aspiration risk is zero in an individual high-risk patient. ([Chen et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2025.0081))

Aspiration is rare enough that even large observational studies can be affected by outcome misclassification, incomplete medication data, and clinical selection. Patients judged to be at higher risk may already have had their procedures postponed, their medications withheld, or their airways protected, which could reduce the apparent risk associated with continued treatment. This is a plausible limitation rather than a demonstrated explanation for the null results. ([Elkin et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/anae.16601); [Chen et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2025.0081))

## Does stopping for one or two weeks solve the problem?

The randomized [OCULUS trial](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2026.0027) shows that withholding one scheduled dose can substantially reduce residual gastric volume before elective upper endoscopy. The [RESIDUAL study](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12871-026-03999-2) shows that some patients still have residual content after a seven-day interruption. These results are not necessarily contradictory: withholding may reduce the average probability of retained content without guaranteeing an empty stomach in every patient. ([Ahmad et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2026.0027); [Boudreau et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12871-026-03999-2))

A 2026 retrospective upper-endoscopy study compared patients who continued GLP-1 treatment, withheld treatment for two weeks, or did not use a GLP-1 drug. Residual gastric content was found in 5.83 percent of patients who continued treatment, 1.61 percent of those who withheld it for two weeks, and 0.40 percent of nonusers. Continued treatment was associated with an adjusted odds ratio of 10.68 compared with nonuse. ([Dong et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00464-026-13121-9))

The two-week group was not statistically different from nonusers, but it included only 62 exposed patients and produced a very wide confidence interval for the odds ratio, from 0.37 to 23.89. A nonsignificant difference in a small study does not demonstrate that two weeks restores risk to normal or that two weeks is superior to a shorter interval. ([Dong et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00464-026-13121-9))

No comparative trial has established a single withholding period that works for every drug, dose, treatment duration, and patient. The interval required for gastric emptying to return to an individual patient's baseline remains unknown, and a sufficiently long pharmacologic washout could create substantial interruption of diabetes or obesity treatment. Current fixed withholding intervals therefore remain pragmatic policies rather than fully validated biological thresholds. ([Kindel et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00464-024-11263-2))

## Liquid diets and gastric ultrasound

A 24-hour liquid-only diet is recommended in recent [multisociety guidance](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00464-024-11263-2) for patients whose risk of delayed emptying is considered higher. [Australian and New Zealand recommendations](https://doi.org/10.1177/0310057X251355288) advise continuing GLP-1 or dual GIP/GLP-1 therapy while using a 24-hour clear-fluid diet before procedures, followed by standard fasting. These are consensus recommendations rather than conclusions from randomized aspiration-outcome trials. ([Kindel et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00464-024-11263-2); [Hocking et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1177/0310057X251355288))

The absence of residual volume in the small [OCULUS subgroup](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2026.0027) that completed a clear-liquid diet and colonoscopy preparation is encouraging, but the subgroup was exploratory and did not isolate the effect of the liquid diet. Direct trials comparing continued treatment plus a liquid diet against withholding with an ordinary diet are still needed. ([Ahmad et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2026.0027))

Point-of-care gastric ultrasound can identify visible solids and estimate gastric fluid volume immediately before a procedure. Current guidance suggests using it selectively when the result would change management and trained personnel are available. No trial has yet shown that ultrasound screening itself reduces aspiration, and its use is limited by the availability of trained personnel, institutional resources, inter-user variability, and credentialing requirements. ([Kindel et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00464-024-11263-2); [Hocking et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1177/0310057X251355288))

When residual content is suspected or confirmed, options include postponing an elective procedure, modifying the anesthetic and airway plan, or managing the patient as having a full stomach. For an urgent procedure, withholding a drug immediately beforehand cannot reverse an established gastric-emptying effect, so risk management becomes more important than the missed dose itself. These recommendations are based primarily on expert consensus rather than comparative outcome trials. ([Kindel et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00464-024-11263-2); [Hocking et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1177/0310057X251355288))

## Who appears to be at higher risk?

Recent [multisociety guidance](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00464-024-11263-2) identifies several factors that may increase concern about delayed gastric emptying:

* **Dose escalation or early treatment:** Gastric-emptying effects appear stronger earlier in treatment, and gastrointestinal adverse effects commonly emerge while the dose is being increased. Prospective pooled estimates suggest a larger delay during the first ten weeks, although the evidence is very low certainty. ([Chen et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12630-026-03114-6); [Kindel et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00464-024-11263-2))

* **Active gastrointestinal symptoms:** Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, dyspepsia, or significant constipation should increase concern. Symptoms are not a reliable rule-out test, however, because every residual-volume case in the [OCULUS trial](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2026.0027) was asymptomatic. The trial was not designed to estimate the diagnostic sensitivity or specificity of symptoms. ([Ahmad et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2026.0027); [Kindel et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00464-024-11263-2))

* **Higher doses or other causes of delayed emptying:** Known gastroparesis, gastric outlet disease, neurological disorders affecting gastric motility, previous foregut surgery, and medications such as opioids may add to the risk. The [OCULUS trial](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2026.0027) excluded several of these groups, so its findings do not quantify their risk. ([Ahmad et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2026.0027); [Kindel et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00464-024-11263-2))

* **Drug formulation:** Short-acting agents produced a larger average delay than long-acting agents in one prospective meta-analysis, but most included studies examined older agents and only one examined semaglutide. Drug-specific conclusions for semaglutide and tirzepatide remain limited. ([Chen et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12630-026-03114-6))

* **Procedure and airway plan:** Upper gastrointestinal procedures performed under deep sedation without a protected airway may warrant additional caution because retained gastric contents can preclude an adequate examination and make regurgitation more consequential. Current guidance recommends incorporating procedural and anesthetic factors into individualized risk assessment. ([Ahmad et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2026.0027); [Kindel et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00464-024-11263-2))

## Quick reference

* GLP-1 receptor agonists prolonged gastric-emptying half-time by an estimated 74 minutes in a meta-analysis of ten prospective studies, but the certainty was very low. ([Chen et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12630-026-03114-6))

* GLP-1 use was associated with approximately sixfold higher odds of residual gastric content in a 2025 observational meta-analysis. ([Elkin et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/anae.16601))

* The same meta-analysis did not detect higher aspiration odds: OR 1.04, 95 percent CI 0.87 to 1.25. ([Elkin et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/anae.16601))

* A cohort of 366,476 surgical patients likewise did not detect a significant association with postoperative aspiration pneumonia. ([Chen et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2025.0081))

* In a 60-patient randomized upper-endoscopy trial, withholding one scheduled dose reduced clinically significant residual volume from 25.0 percent to 3.1 percent. ([Ahmad et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2026.0027))

* A seven-day hold does not guarantee an empty stomach, and a two-week hold has not been proven equivalent to nonuse. ([Boudreau et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12871-026-03999-2); [Dong et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00464-026-13121-9))

* A 24-hour clear-liquid diet and selective gastric ultrasound are reasonable risk-management options, but neither has yet been shown in a randomized trial to reduce aspiration. ([Kindel et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00464-024-11263-2); [Hocking et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1177/0310057X251355288))

## Practical interpretation

* **Withholding one dose is an evidence-supported option before selected elective upper endoscopies.** The randomized [OCULUS trial](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2026.0027) provides direct evidence that withholding one scheduled dose reduces procedure-limiting residual gastric volume in stable-dose patients undergoing elective upper endoscopy after an ordinary diet and standard fasting. It did not show that continuation is unsafe or establish a reduction in aspiration. ([Ahmad et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2026.0027))

* **Blanket withholding before every procedure is also not supported.** Large observational data have not detected increased aspiration, and withholding can disrupt diabetes treatment, weight-management treatment, scheduling, and access to medication. The balance may favor continuation in stable, asymptomatic, lower-risk patients when an appropriate perioperative protocol is in place. ([Elkin et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/anae.16601); [Kindel et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00464-024-11263-2))

* **A one-week hold is not a guarantee.** It can reduce residual-content risk at the group level, particularly before elective upper endoscopy, but some patients retain gastric contents after seven days. Decisions should not rely only on the date of the last injection. ([Ahmad et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2026.0027); [Boudreau et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12871-026-03999-2))

* **Higher-risk patients need an individualized plan.** Dose escalation, active gastrointestinal symptoms, known gastric dysmotility, other medications that slow emptying, and a procedure or anesthetic plan with greater aspiration consequences should prompt consideration of postponement, a liquid diet, gastric ultrasound, selective withholding, or full-stomach airway precautions. ([Kindel et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00464-024-11263-2); [Hocking et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1177/0310057X251355288))

* **Symptoms alone are insufficient.** Significant symptoms increase concern, but an asymptomatic patient can still have substantial residual content. ([Ahmad et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2026.0027))

* **Patients should not stop treatment independently.** The prescribing clinician, proceduralist, and anesthesia team should balance gastric risk against glycemic control, treatment interruption, and the consequences of postponing the procedure. ([Kindel et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00464-024-11263-2))

The most evidence-consistent conclusion is that GLP-1 drugs should not automatically be stopped before every surgery or endoscopy. Continuation is reasonable for many stable, lower-risk patients, while withholding one scheduled dose is now an evidence-supported option before elective upper endoscopy performed after ordinary fasting in selected, stable-dose patients. Patients in dose escalation, those with symptoms or gastric-motility disorders, and those undergoing procedures with greater aspiration consequences warrant additional precautions. The evidence is considerably stronger for reducing residual gastric contents than for reducing aspiration, so any policy claiming that a fixed hold prevents aspiration goes beyond the available data. ([Elkin et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/anae.16601); [Ahmad et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2026.0027); [Kindel et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00464-024-11263-2))

## Funding and conflict-of-interest context

* [**Chen et al. gastric-emptying meta-analysis**](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12630-026-03114-6)**:** The review reported internal departmental support from Toronto Western Hospital/University Health Network rather than GLP-1 manufacturer funding. One author disclosed consulting for an ultrasound manufacturer, and another disclosed consulting relationships with pharmaceutical companies and other research support not identified as sponsorship of this review. The very low certainty of the findings is driven primarily by the limitations of the underlying studies rather than direct manufacturer sponsorship of the review.

* [**Elkin et al. residual-content and aspiration meta-analysis**](https://doi.org/10.1111/anae.16601)**:** The review reported Australian Government Research Training Program scholarship support rather than direct manufacturer sponsorship. Several authors nevertheless disclosed relationships involving Novo Nordisk or Eli Lilly, including manuscript support or investigator-initiated research support paid to institutions. Those relationships should be considered alongside the review's observational evidence base, rare aspiration outcome, and low certainty.

* [**Sen et al. gastric-ultrasound study**](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamasurg.2024.0111)**:** The study reported National Institutes of Health support and no conflicts of interest. No GLP-1 manufacturer funding was reported.

* [**Ahmad et al. OCULUS randomized trial**](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2026.0027)**:** The trial was funded by a Cleveland Clinic Catalyst Grant and an American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy Clinical Research Award. The funders reportedly had no role in the study. One author disclosed consulting for Novo Nordisk outside the submitted work, and another disclosed endoscopy-industry relationships and a device patent. The trial was not directly funded by a GLP-1 manufacturer, but the Novo Nordisk relationship should still be noted.

* [**Boudreau et al. RESIDUAL study**](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12871-026-03999-2)**:** Funding was institutional and departmental, and the authors reported no competing interests.

* [**Dong et al. two-week withholding study**](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00464-026-13121-9)**:** The study reported public or hospital research grants, and the authors reported no relevant financial conflicts.

* [**Chen et al. surgical aspiration cohort**](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2025.0081)**:** No GLP-1 manufacturer conflict was reported. One author disclosed personal fees from orthopedic device manufacturers outside the submitted work, which were not directly related to GLP-1 treatment.

* [**Kindel et al. multisociety guidance**](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00464-024-11263-2)**:** This was a consensus document rather than a funded comparative trial. Some contributors disclosed consulting relationships with Novo Nordisk, while others reported relationships with medical-device companies. Its recommendations should therefore be understood as expert interpretation of limited evidence, not as independent proof that any specific protocol prevents aspiration.

* [**Hocking et al. Australian and New Zealand recommendations**](https://doi.org/10.1177/0310057X251355288)**:** The authors reported no conflicts of interest. The recommendations remain consensus-based because direct comparative outcome evidence for the proposed 24-hour liquid-diet strategy is limited.
