# Do GLP-1s Reset Your Body’s Weight Set Point?

Current human evidence does not show that GLP-1-based medications permanently reset a fixed biological weight set point. The better-supported interpretation is that they maintain a lower observed average body weight while treatment continues. Randomized withdrawal trials do not directly measure a set point or establish that the lower weight is biologically defended without treatment. When the medication is withdrawn, substantial weight regain is common. This argues strongly against a complete, treatment-independent reset, but it does not prove that every biological effect disappears or that every person will return fully to baseline. ([Budini et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2026.103796); [Speakman and Hall, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2022.0231))

Here, “GLP-1-based treatment” includes GLP-1 receptor agonists such as liraglutide and semaglutide. Tirzepatide is technically a dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist, but its withdrawal data are relevant to the same question about incretin-based weight regulation. ([Aronne et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2023.24945))

## What a “set-point reset” would mean

A strict reset would mean that, after treatment is removed, the lower weight becomes a new treatment-independent equilibrium: weight remains relatively stable without the drug, and the biological pressures opposing weight loss - such as increased hunger, altered appetite hormones, or reduced energy expenditure - are durably reduced or reorganized. ([Sumithran et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1105816); [Nunes et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114521001094))

That outcome cannot be established simply by observing a weight-loss plateau during treatment. A plateau observed while a pharmacological signal remains active does not show that the same equilibrium will persist once that signal is removed. Randomized withdrawal trials directly test that distinction and show divergent trajectories after continuation versus withdrawal. ([Rubino et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224); [Aronne et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2023.24945))

“Set point” is also a theoretical model rather than a directly measurable clinical variable. Contemporary models include a single set point, dynamic equilibrium, control-theory or “settling-point” models, and models with upper and lower intervention boundaries. A 2023 review concluded that further experiments are needed to distinguish among them. The review does not identify a validated clinical test that assigns a person a single body-weight set point or demonstrates that a drug has permanently reset it. ([Speakman and Hall, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2022.0231))

## What happens while treatment continues

Withdrawal trials provide some of the clearest evidence that ongoing drug exposure matters. ([Rubino et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224); [Aronne et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2023.24945))

In STEP 4, 803 participants who completed a 20-week semaglutide run-in and reached the 2.4 mg weekly maintenance dose were randomized after losing an average of 10.6 percent of their weight. Over the following 48 weeks, participants who continued semaglutide lost another 7.9 percent, while those switched to placebo regained 6.9 percent, despite lifestyle intervention in both groups. This indicates that continued semaglutide exposure, rather than the initial weight loss alone, was maintaining the trajectory. ([Rubino et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224))

In SURMOUNT-4, participants who completed a 36-week open-label tirzepatide lead-in and reached a maximum tolerated dose of 10 or 15 mg lost an average of 20.9 percent. During the subsequent 52-week randomized phase, those continuing tirzepatide lost another 5.5 percent, whereas those switched to placebo regained 14.0 percent. At week 88, 89.5 percent of those continuing tirzepatide had maintained at least 80 percent of their initial loss, compared with 16.6 percent after withdrawal. ([Aronne et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2023.24945))

Both trials used enriched randomized-withdrawal designs: only participants who completed and tolerated the active-treatment lead-in were randomized. The results therefore provide strong causal evidence among treatment-tolerant completers, but may not generalize fully to everyone who starts therapy. ([Rubino et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224); [Aronne et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2023.24945))

Longer trials show that the effect can remain durable **while treatment is maintained**. In the SELECT trial, mean weight change at 208 weeks was -10.2 percent with semaglutide and -1.5 percent with placebo, with the semaglutide trajectory remaining broadly stable after approximately week 65. This is evidence of sustained efficacy over four years, not evidence that the effect would persist for four years after discontinuation. ([Ryan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-024-02996-7))

Together, these findings show that GLP-1-based treatment can maintain a lower observed average body weight **under continued pharmacological exposure**. They do not demonstrate that this lower weight is biologically defended without treatment or that weight regulation has been permanently reset. ([Rubino et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224); [Aronne et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2023.24945); [Ryan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-024-02996-7))

## What happens after stopping

The STEP 1 extension followed a selected subset of 327 participants for one year after semaglutide and structured lifestyle treatment were stopped. Participants formerly assigned to semaglutide regained 11.6 percentage points of body weight - approximately two-thirds of the weight they had lost. They nevertheless remained, on average, 5.6 percent below their original baseline at the end of follow-up. Cardiometabolic improvements also moved back toward baseline. The remaining 5.6 percent loss does not establish a partial biological reset. The extension was exploratory, stopped structured lifestyle treatment as well as semaglutide, lacked a randomized continued-treatment counterfactual during the extension, and ended while regain was continuing. ([Wilding et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725))

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of 18 randomized trials, including 3,771 participants, found that people with obesity gained a pooled 5.63 kg after GLP-1 receptor agonist discontinuation. HbA1c increased by 0.25 percentage points, and waist circumference, BMI, systolic blood pressure, and fasting glucose also worsened. Heterogeneity was very high, so the pooled number should not be interpreted as the amount every patient will regain. ([Tzang et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2025.103680))

A 2026 GLP-1-specific systematic review identified 48 relevant studies. Its primary nonlinear model used six randomized trials with 3,236 participants and estimated that 60 percent of treatment-induced weight loss had been regained one year after cessation. A mixed-effects exponential recovery model predicted that regain might eventually plateau at approximately 75.3 percent of the weight initially lost. This was a model-derived asymptote, not an observed plateau: all trajectories after 52 weeks were extrapolated rather than directly observed, and most included studies had a moderate risk of bias. The review reported no funding. ([Budini et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2026.103796))

A separate 2026 BMJ review examined 37 studies of weight-management medications, encompassing 9,341 participants. Across medications, weight returned at an average rate of approximately 0.4 kg per month, and the authors projected a return to baseline weight after about 1.7 years. For newer incretin medications, the estimated regain rate was approximately 0.8 kg per month and the projected return to baseline was about 1.5 years. Direct post-cessation data for these newer medications generally did not extend beyond 52 weeks, so the later trajectory was again modelled rather than observed. The review was supported principally by public and charitable funding, with no funder role in the analysis; two authors also disclosed partial funding through a Novo Nordisk Foundation research grant. ([West et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2025-085304))

The two 2026 models therefore differ: one predicts a partial plateau below baseline, while the other projects eventual return to baseline. Neither long-term trajectory has yet been directly observed. The disagreement is not necessarily contradictory evidence from patients. The reviews used different study inclusion criteria and model forms, and both extrapolated beyond the period for which strong direct data exist. ([Budini et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2026.103796); [West et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2025-085304))

## Does regain prove that no set-point change occurred?

Regain after discontinuation is strong evidence against a **complete and permanent reset**. If the lower weight had become fully self-sustaining, removing the medication would not be expected to produce the consistent regain pattern seen in STEP 4, SURMOUNT-4, STEP 1, and subsequent evidence syntheses. ([Rubino et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224); [Aronne et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2023.24945); [Wilding et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725); [Tzang et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2025.103680); [Budini et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2026.103796); [West et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2025-085304))

But “regain proves that absolutely no change occurred” is too strong. In STEP 1, participants still averaged 5.6 percent below baseline one year after stopping. That residual loss does not itself establish a partial biological reset because the extension lacked a randomized continued-treatment counterfactual and ended while regain was continuing. Some individuals retained considerably more weight loss than others, and the two 2026 reviews do not agree on whether average regain eventually plateaus below baseline. Possible explanations include persistent behavioral changes, altered food preferences, changes in body composition, or residual biological effects, but these are hypotheses rather than mechanisms established by the extension. ([Wilding et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725); [Budini et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2026.103796); [West et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2025-085304))

The defensible conclusion is therefore narrower: **withdrawal data do not support a complete, treatment-independent reset, while a smaller partial or individual-specific durable effect remains possible but unproven.** ([Wilding et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725); [Tzang et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2025.103680); [Budini et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2026.103796); [West et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2025-085304))

## Hormones, appetite, and metabolic adaptation

Direct post-withdrawal profiling of leptin, ghrelin, insulin, resting energy expenditure, and related signals remains an important gap not comprehensively addressed by the major withdrawal trials. Those trials primarily measured body weight, waist circumference, glycaemia, blood pressure, and lipids. They did not comprehensively establish whether appetite hormones or energy expenditure had been permanently reprogrammed after the medication was removed. ([Rubino et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224); [Aronne et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2023.24945))

There is evidence that GLP-1 receptor activation can modify weight-loss-related hormonal signals **during active treatment**. In a randomized trial of 52 people who had lost 12 percent of their weight through diet, one year of liraglutide attenuated the increase in soluble leptin receptors and the decline in free leptin compared with a control group not receiving liraglutide. Both groups maintained their weight, and low-calorie meal replacements could be used when weight increased. Because the measurements were made during active treatment, the study supports an active pharmacological effect on leptin-related physiology but does not show that the effect persists after liraglutide is withdrawn. The study was supported by Danish public research funding; meal-replacement products were donated, and the investigators reported that the sponsors were not involved in study design or analysis. ([Iepsen et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1038/ijo.2014.177))

Evidence from diet-induced weight loss provides useful context, although it cannot be assumed to represent GLP-1 withdrawal. A study enrolled 50 adults in a very-low-energy diet program that produced an average loss of 13.5 kg. Leptin, peptide YY, cholecystokinin, insulin, ghrelin, and subjective hunger remained significantly altered one year later in directions that could favor regain. The study was publicly and charitably funded. ([Sumithran et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1105816))

A small 2026 prospective study followed 28 women through 36 weeks of semaglutide and 12 weeks of withdrawal. After stopping, 71.4 percent regained weight, averaging 5.1 kg among those with regain, and 78.5 percent reported appetite rebound. The study also included parallel animal experiments; the hypothalamic gene-expression findings came from those animal experiments, not from direct measurement of the women’s hypothalami. The microbiome and bile-acid associations were observational and do not establish causality. Because the human study was small, single-arm, women-only, and had a short withdrawal period, its findings are hypothesis-generating rather than proof of a hypothalamic reset or reversal. ([Wang et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.70571))

Energy expenditure is similarly nuanced. A systematic review found that adaptive thermogenesis was observed in 27 of 33 weight-loss studies, but estimates were generally smaller or non-significant in studies using stronger methodology. Adaptive thermogenesis also tended to diminish or disappear after weight stabilization or restoration of neutral energy balance. This does not support the popular idea of uniform, permanent “metabolic damage,” although changes in energy expenditure may contribute to regain in some circumstances. ([Nunes et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114521001094))

Overall, appetite-related compensation is biologically plausible and supported by non-GLP-1 weight-loss research, but the studies reviewed here do not adequately map the exact leptin, ghrelin, insulin, and energy-expenditure trajectory after modern GLP-1 withdrawal. ([Sumithran et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1105816); [Nunes et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114521001094); [Rubino et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224); [Aronne et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2023.24945))

## Set point versus settling point

A settling-point model offers one plausible framework for interpreting the evidence, rather than establishing that body weight is governed by one immutable number. In this framework, weight reflects a dynamic equilibrium produced by interacting physiological, behavioral, and environmental influences. Medication exposure can be treated as one such input: changing it can move the equilibrium, and removing it can move the equilibrium again. ([Speakman and Hall, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2022.0231))

Post-treatment exercise data illustrate this interaction. In a follow-up to a randomized trial, participants first lost weight through a low-calorie diet and then completed one year of liraglutide, supervised exercise, both, or placebo. One year after all treatment ended, regain was 6.0 kg greater following liraglutide alone than following supervised exercise. Previous combination treatment also produced better retained weight and body composition than liraglutide alone, although only 109 participants attended the post-treatment assessment. ([Jensen et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2024.102475))

This study does not prove that exercise resets a set point or that it will prevent regain after semaglutide or tirzepatide. It suggests that the post-treatment trajectory is not determined solely by prior drug exposure: participants previously assigned to supervised exercise had a different post-treatment weight trajectory. The study was funded by Helsefonden and the Novo Nordisk Foundation; Novo Nordisk A/S supplied liraglutide and placebo pens, and several investigators disclosed pharmaceutical-industry relationships. The article reported no role for the funding partners in study design or analysis, but the product supply and disclosed relationships should still be considered when interpreting the findings. ([Jensen et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2024.102475))

## Does longer treatment produce a more durable reset?

Longer continued treatment can clearly maintain weight loss for longer. SELECT demonstrated sustained semaglutide-associated weight loss during up to four years of treatment. That answers the question of **on-treatment durability**, not what happens after four years of treatment are stopped. ([Ryan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-024-02996-7))

Current evidence does not establish that a longer course creates greater post-treatment durability. A 2025 meta-analysis of anti-obesity medications found no study-level association between treatment duration and subsequent regain. A formal correction published on March 25, 2026 clarified that the analysis included 11 unique studies contributing 15 time-point comparisons, rather than 15 independent studies, and added a multilevel analysis to account for within-study dependence. The core conclusion about a general trend of regain was unchanged. However, the analysis combined different medications and populations, used aggregate data, relied on indirect comparisons across studies, and had limited long-term follow-up. Its null meta-regression should not be interpreted as evidence that treatment duration has no effect. It was not a trial in which otherwise similar participants were randomized to different treatment durations and then observed for an equal period after withdrawal. The review reported no funding or competing interests. ([Wu et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-025-04200-0); [Wu et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-026-04817-9))

Therefore, longer exposure might produce more persistent adaptations, but the claim remains unproven. The necessary study would randomize treatment duration, standardize the withdrawal phase, and repeatedly measure weight, food intake, hunger, leptin, ghrelin, insulin, body composition, and energy expenditure. ([Wu et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-025-04200-0); [Wu et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-026-04817-9))

## Funding bias and evidence limitations

Several pivotal trials were manufacturer-sponsored. ([Wilding et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725); [Rubino et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224); [Ryan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-024-02996-7); [Aronne et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2023.24945))

- STEP 1 was funded by Novo Nordisk. STEP 4 was also funded by Novo Nordisk, whose representatives participated in study design and conduct, data collection, management and analysis, interpretation, and manuscript preparation. ([Wilding et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725); [Rubino et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224))

- SELECT’s sponsor, Novo Nordisk, designed the protocol with the academic steering committee; several authors were company employees, and company-funded editorial assistance was disclosed. ([Ryan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-024-02996-7))

- SURMOUNT-4 was sponsored by Eli Lilly. The company was involved in study design and conduct, data collection and analysis, interpretation, and manuscript preparation, and several authors were employees and shareholders. ([Aronne et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2023.24945))


These conflicts do not invalidate randomized results, particularly the large differences between continuation and withdrawal groups. They do require appropriate caution, especially where sponsor employees participated throughout the research process. ([Rubino et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224); [Aronne et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2023.24945); [Ryan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-024-02996-7))

The main withdrawal finding is also reproduced in evidence syntheses without direct pharmaceutical-company funding. The 2026 GLP-1-specific nonlinear review reported no funding, the 2025 anti-obesity medication review reported no funding or competing interests, and the 2026 BMJ review was principally publicly funded, although two authors disclosed partial funding through a Novo Nordisk Foundation research grant. Replication across multiple randomized withdrawal trials and independently conducted evidence syntheses makes it unlikely that the direction of the post-discontinuation effect is solely an artifact of sponsor involvement. However, most high-quality primary data remain manufacturer-sponsored, so effect magnitudes, subgroup findings, and long-term projections still require cautious interpretation. ([Budini et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2026.103796); [Wu et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-025-04200-0); [West et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2025-085304))

Nevertheless, the evidence base remains limited by short post-withdrawal follow-up, substantial heterogeneity, incomplete hormone and energy-expenditure measurements, selected extension samples, enriched withdrawal designs, and heavy reliance on manufacturer-sponsored pivotal trials. ([Wilding et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725); [Rubino et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224); [Aronne et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2023.24945); [Tzang et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2025.103680); [Budini et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2026.103796); [West et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2025-085304))

## Quick reference values

- **STEP 1 extension:** approximately two-thirds of semaglutide-associated weight loss was regained within one year; average weight remained 5.6 percent below baseline. ([Wilding et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725))

- **GLP-1-specific 2026 meta-regression:** approximately 60 percent of lost weight regained at one year. The predicted 75.3 percent eventual plateau was model-derived and based on extrapolation after week 52. ([Budini et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2026.103796))

- **2025 GLP-1 discontinuation meta-analysis:** pooled regain of 5.63 kg in participants with obesity, with very high heterogeneity. ([Tzang et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2025.103680))

- **STEP 4:** after the semaglutide run-in, continued treatment produced a further 7.9 percent loss, while withdrawal produced 6.9 percent regain over 48 weeks. ([Rubino et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224))

- **SURMOUNT-4:** after the tirzepatide run-in, continued treatment produced a further 5.5 percent loss, while withdrawal produced 14.0 percent regain over 52 weeks. ([Aronne et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2023.24945))


## Practical interpretation

- GLP-1-based medications can help maintain a substantially lower average body weight **while treatment remains active**, with semaglutide data extending to four years. ([Rubino et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224); [Aronne et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2023.24945); [Ryan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-024-02996-7))

- Current evidence does not show that they permanently reset a fixed set point after treatment ends. ([Budini et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2026.103796))

- Regain after discontinuation is consistent with loss of an active treatment effect. In randomized withdrawal trials, substantial regain occurred after withdrawal despite continued lifestyle intervention. ([Rubino et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2021.3224); [Aronne et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2023.24945))

- Regain strongly argues against a complete reset, but it does not prove that all treatment effects vanish or that every person returns fully to baseline. ([Wilding et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725))

- A dynamic settling-point model - based on interacting physiological, behavioral, and environmental influences - is one plausible framework for interpreting the available evidence, rather than proof that everyone has one permanently fixed weight number. ([Speakman and Hall, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2022.0231))

- Whether several years of treatment produce greater post-withdrawal durability remains unknown. ([Wu et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-025-04200-0); [Wu et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-026-04817-9))

- Because discontinuation can affect both weight and glycaemic control, stopping or altering treatment should be planned with the prescribing clinician rather than treated as a simple test of whether the body has been “reset.” ([Tzang et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2025.103680))
