# GLP-1s and Mental Health: Depression and Suicidal Ideation

The best current evidence does not support a class-wide causal increase in suicidal ideation, self-harm, or suicide with glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) at the population level, although it cannot establish zero risk in every patient, product, or exposure period. ([Tran et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40267-026-01266-5); [Ebrahimi et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2025.0091))

Depression is less settled. Randomized-trial syntheses have not found average worsening of depressive symptoms or serious psychiatric adverse events, but observational studies range from modestly higher depression diagnoses to substantially lower rates of worsening mental illness. The disagreement appears to be driven partly by comparator choice, outcome definitions, psychiatric exclusions, and residual confounding rather than by one consistent pharmacological effect. ([Tran et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40267-026-01266-5); [Pierret et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2025.0679); [Chang et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.70175); [Taipale et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2215-0366%2826%2900014-3))

## Why the concern arose

The initial concern came largely from individual adverse-event reports and pharmacovigilance databases. A 2024 analysis of the World Health Organization's spontaneous-reporting database identified 107 suicide- or self-injury-related reports involving semaglutide and 162 involving liraglutide. Semaglutide had disproportionate reporting of suicidal ideation, with a reporting odds ratio of 1.45. ([Schoretsanitis et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.23385))

A reporting odds ratio asks whether an event is reported more frequently for one drug than for other drugs in the same database. It does not measure incidence and cannot establish that the drug caused the event. Spontaneous-report databases have no exposed or untreated denominator and are vulnerable to missing clinical information, selection and reporting biases, confounding by indication, and channeling bias. The stronger signal among reports that also mentioned antidepressants or benzodiazepines could reflect baseline psychiatric vulnerability, a drug-related effect, or both; the analysis cannot distinguish these explanations or establish a harmful drug interaction. ([Schoretsanitis et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.23385); [Tran et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40267-026-01266-5))

A direct brain effect is biologically plausible because GLP-1 signaling can influence appetite, reward processing, neuroinflammation, stress responses, and neuroplasticity. However, the proposed mechanisms do not point exclusively toward harm. Much of the preclinical literature instead predicts potentially beneficial effects on neuroinflammation, hippocampal function, and reward-related behavior. Large randomized trials designed to test antidepressant efficacy in people with primary mood disorders are still lacking, so mechanistic plausibility cannot establish either antidepressant benefit or psychiatric toxicity in humans. ([Carmellini et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/life15091422))

## What randomized trials show about suicidal ideation and behavior

The most comprehensive subject-level analysis to date combined 91 randomized, placebo-controlled trials involving 107,910 participants and approximately 271,000 person-years of follow-up. There were 55 suicidal ideation or behavior events in GLP-1 RA groups and 44 in placebo groups. The incidence was 3.6 events per 10,000 person-years in both groups, producing a rate ratio of 1.0, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.7 to 1.5. Results were similar in diabetes and obesity trials. ([Tran et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40267-026-01266-5))

In 22 trials that prospectively used the Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale rather than relying mainly on routine adverse-event coding, the rate ratio was 0.7, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.5 to 1.0. This does not prove a protective effect, but it provides no indication of excess risk under structured prospective assessment. ([Tran et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40267-026-01266-5))

A separate 2025 meta-analysis examined 27 randomized trials that systematically recorded suicide or self-harm events. It included 32,354 participants assigned to a GLP-1 RA and 27,042 assigned to placebo. Event rates were 0.047 and 0.042 per 100 person-years, respectively, and the pooled rate ratio was 0.76, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.48 to 1.21. No subgroup analysis identified a statistically clear excess, but molecule-specific estimates were sparse and imprecise. A formal correction updated the reported person-years without changing the study's conclusions. ([Ebrahimi et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2025.0091); [Correction, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2025.1716))

These results are reassuring but not absolute proof of zero risk. Suicidal behavior was rare, leaving confidence intervals compatible with a moderate relative increase or decrease, although the corresponding average absolute differences would remain small at the observed event rate. Many obesity trials excluded participants with a recent major depressive episode, previous suicide attempt, active suicidal ideation, or other serious psychiatric illness. In addition, most trials did not make psychiatric safety their primary outcome, and many events in the larger pooled analysis were reconstructed from routine adverse-event coding rather than collected prospectively with a dedicated instrument. ([Tran et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40267-026-01266-5); [Ebrahimi et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2025.0091))

## Depression and broader psychiatric outcomes

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of 80 randomized trials included 107,860 participants. Across 30 trials reporting serious psychiatric adverse events, there was no statistically clear difference between GLP-1 RAs and placebo. The pooled effect on measured depressive symptoms was centered near the null: Hedges g 0.02, with a 95 percent confidence interval from -0.51 to 0.55. The wide interval permits moderate effects in either direction and therefore does not establish equivalence for depressive symptoms. The certainty of the depression-specific result was rated low because relatively few trials measured depression systematically and the measurements were heterogeneous. ([Pierret et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2025.0679))

The 2026 subject-level trial analysis also found no statistically significant increase in depression, anxiety, irritability, or psychosis. Rate ratios were approximately 0.9 to 1.3, with confidence intervals that included no difference. ([Tran et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40267-026-01266-5))

A different 2026 systematic review reported a pooled odds ratio of 1.49 for depression or related mood outcomes. However, the published report contains unresolved internal inconsistencies that prevent reliable interpretation of that number. It states that 19 studies were included, while the heterogeneity statistic has 7 degrees of freedom, implying eight pooled estimates. Its forest plot also appears to contain a duplicated study and an unidentified row, and it assigns Shapiro et al. an estimate that is not readily reconcilable with that study's primary adjusted results. Until these discrepancies are corrected and the extracted effects are transparently reported, the pooled estimate should not be used as a reliable quantitative summary. ([Bi et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-026-08016-x); [Shapiro et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2024-080679))

When the evidence is separated by study design, randomized trials do not show average depression worsening. The observational evidence remains important for outcomes in routine practice and in patients often excluded from trials, but its estimates are heterogeneous and vary with comparator, adjustment, and outcome ascertainment. ([Tran et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40267-026-01266-5); [Pierret et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2025.0679); [Chang et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.70175); [Shapiro et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2024-080679); [Taipale et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2215-0366%2826%2900014-3))

## Why observational studies disagree

A 2026 new-user, active-comparator cohort study matched 25,704 GLP-1 RA users to 25,704 sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitor users with type 2 diabetes and overweight or obesity. At one year, a composite of a new depression diagnosis or antidepressant initiation occurred in 17.0 percent of GLP-1 RA users and 14.8 percent of comparator users. The adjusted hazard ratio was 1.09, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 1.04 to 1.14, an absolute difference of 2.2 percentage points and a much smaller association than earlier non-user comparisons suggested. In a sensitivity analysis restricted to people with sufficient prior electronic-health-record history, the association attenuated to a hazard ratio of 1.03, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.97 to 1.09. ([Chang et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.70175))

The outcome in that study was not a validated depression scale or an adjudicated clinical diagnosis. It was a composite of depression diagnosis codes or antidepressant initiation, so outcome misclassification is possible. The database also leaves room for incomplete outcome capture, treatment discontinuation or switching, and residual differences in why clinicians selected one diabetes drug over another. Because the comparison was between two active treatments, the estimate is a relative difference between GLP-1 RAs and sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors, not a comparison with no treatment. It therefore does not by itself identify whether the difference reflects GLP-1-related harm, sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitor-related benefit, residual prescribing differences, or some combination of these factors. ([Chang et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.70175))

Comparator selection can materially change the apparent result. In a large United Kingdom cohort, crude analyses suggested more suicidality among GLP-1 RA users than among users of dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors or sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors. After adjustment for diabetes severity, psychiatric history, socioeconomic factors, and other confounders, the associations moved to the null: hazard ratio 1.02, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.85 to 1.23, versus dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, and 0.91, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.73 to 1.12, versus sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors. Suicidal ideation, self-harm, and suicide were also null when considered separately. ([Shapiro et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2024-080679))

Other large active-comparator studies were broadly reassuring. A Swedish-Danish cohort of 124,517 GLP-1 RA users and 174,036 sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitor users found a hazard ratio of 1.25, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.83 to 1.88, for suicide death; 0.83, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.70 to 0.97, for suicide death or nonfatal self-harm; and 1.01, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.97 to 1.06, for incident depression or anxiety-related disorders. A separate 2026 Danish study found a hazard ratio of 0.93, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.57 to 1.52, versus sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors and 0.58, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.37 to 0.91, versus dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors for suicide or suicide attempts. These observational estimates do not establish protection, but they do not show a consistent excess. ([Ueda et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2024.4369); [Stanislaus et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41380-026-03619-y))

A Swedish national study addressed a different population: 95,490 people who already had depression or anxiety and were receiving glucose-lowering medication. In within-person analyses, semaglutide use was associated with a lower rate of a composite measure of worsening mental illness, with an adjusted hazard ratio of 0.58. Liraglutide was associated with a smaller reduction, with an adjusted hazard ratio of 0.82, while exenatide and dulaglutide were not clearly associated with the outcome. The composite included psychiatric hospitalization, extended psychiatric sick leave, hospitalization for self-harm, and suicide. Self-harm was also less frequent during GLP-1 RA exposure. Because this was observational, it cannot establish an antidepressant effect, but it provides relevant evidence in a psychiatric population frequently excluded from weight-management trials. ([Taipale et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2215-0366%2826%2900014-3))

The largest apparent harmful association came from a 2024 retrospective study comparing GLP-1 RA users with people with obesity who were not recorded as using a GLP-1 RA. After propensity matching, the reported hazard ratios were 2.95 for major depression, 2.08 for anxiety, and 2.06 for suicidal ideation or attempts. ([Kornelius et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-75965-2))

That result is an outlier relative to the randomized evidence and better-controlled active-comparator cohorts. The treated group's index date was the first recorded GLP-1 RA prescription, whereas the comparison group's index date was the first recorded obesity diagnosis, so time zero and health-care contact were not equivalent. The study excluded prior psychiatric diagnoses using only a one-year lookback period and lacked detailed information on weight trajectory, treatment adherence, prescribing reasons, and psychiatric assessments. One plausible explanation is residual treatment-selection and health-care-contact bias: patients prescribed GLP-1 RAs may differ from non-users in clinically important ways that diagnosis codes and propensity matching cannot fully capture. This is an inference from the study's design limitations, not proof that its result is entirely artifactual. ([Kornelius et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-75965-2); [Shapiro et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2024-080679))

## Weight loss, body image, and pre-existing mental health

Weight change could plausibly act as a benefit, a stressor, or a confounder. Randomized and qualitative evidence documents average improvements in mental-health-related quality of life, eating restraint, emotional eating, and reduced food-related preoccupation for some patients, while qualitative interviews also describe adverse effects, treatment-access problems, stigma, and a need for behavioral and medical support. A plausible inference is that pre-existing psychiatric illness could also affect treatment selection, monitoring, and outcome documentation. These pathways are possible explanations for some observed associations, not mechanisms established by the current database studies. ([Pierret et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2025.0679); [de Vere Hunt et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.16951); [Chang et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.70175); [Kornelius et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-75965-2))

Most available database studies do not directly measure these pathways. Consequently, attributing an association to weight loss or body-image change remains a hypothesis rather than a demonstrated explanation in those datasets. ([Chang et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.70175); [Kornelius et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-75965-2))

## Patient-reported benefits and "food noise"

The 2025 randomized-trial meta-analysis found small improvements in mental-health-related quality of life and moderate improvements in eating-related outcomes. The pooled standardized effect was 0.15 for mental-health-related quality of life, 0.35 for eating restraint, and 0.32 for emotional eating. The eating restraint and emotional eating estimates came from only three studies with 341 participants, so they should be interpreted cautiously. These outcomes support a possible improvement in food-related cognition and well-being, but they are not equivalent to remission of major depression. ([Pierret et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2025.0679))

In a 2026 qualitative study of 30 participants across 15 U.S. states, many participants described reduced "food noise," less psychological hunger, and greater perceived control over eating. Participants also described adverse effects, treatment access problems, stigma, and the need for behavioral and medical support. Because this was a small interview study without an untreated comparison group, it documents lived experience rather than proving that GLP-1 RAs improve depression. ([de Vere Hunt et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.16951))

Patient reports of improved self-image or relief from persistent thoughts about food are therefore credible experiences and may represent meaningful benefits for some people. They should not be generalized into a claim that GLP-1 RAs are antidepressants, because trial evidence has not established a direct antidepressant effect and qualitative evidence also documents trade-offs, including adverse effects, stigma, and access problems. ([Pierret et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2025.0679); [de Vere Hunt et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.16951))

## Regulatory interpretation

The regulatory position has become more reassuring as additional trial-level evidence has accumulated. The [European Medicines Agency's Pharmacovigilance Risk Assessment Committee](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/news/meeting-highlights-pharmacovigilance-risk-assessment-committee-prac-8-11-april-2024) concluded in April 2024 that the available evidence did not support a causal association between GLP-1 RAs and suicidal or self-injurious thoughts or actions. FDA-affiliated investigators later reported no population-level increase in the subject-level trial analysis or in a Sentinel active-comparator analysis involving more than 2.2 million new users. Current 2026 prescribing information for [Wegovy](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2026/215256s033lbl.pdf) and [Zepbound](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2026/217806s042lbl.pdf) does not list suicidal behavior or ideation among warnings and precautions. ([Tran et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40267-026-01266-5); [Kuzucan et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjdrc-2025-005840))

This does not mean that an idiosyncratic psychiatric reaction is impossible or that individual symptoms should be ignored. It means that accumulated randomized, observational, and regulatory evidence has not confirmed a population-level causal increase. Suicidal behavior remains rare, which limits precision for uncommon risks, and new or worsening psychiatric symptoms still require clinical evaluation regardless of whether a medication is suspected. ([Tran et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40267-026-01266-5); [Ebrahimi et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2025.0091); [Kuzucan et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjdrc-2025-005840))

## Limits and evidence still needed

Trials should prospectively record baseline and follow-up depression, anxiety, eating-related symptoms, and suicidal ideation using validated instruments. The Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale is one established option for prospective assessment of suicidal ideation and behavior; depression, eating-related outcomes, and other psychiatric outcomes should likewise be prespecified and measured systematically rather than reconstructed after a signal appears. ([Tran et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40267-026-01266-5); [Pierret et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2025.0679); [Ebrahimi et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2025.0091))

Future studies need to include adequately supported participants with stable depression, anxiety, previous self-harm, or other psychiatric conditions. Their frequent exclusion from trials limits the applicability of current findings to precisely the patients in whom concern is greatest. ([Tran et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40267-026-01266-5); [Pierret et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2025.0679); [Ebrahimi et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2025.0091))

Observational studies should use active comparators and new-user designs, with repeated psychiatric measurements, adjudicated suicide outcomes, and detailed information on prior mental illness, weight change, adverse effects, discontinuation, and access barriers. Analyses should distinguish treatment initiation, dose escalation, long-term treatment, and discontinuation rather than averaging all exposure periods together. These priorities would help address limitations identified in current electronic-record and registry studies. ([Chang et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.70175); [Shapiro et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2024-080679); [Ueda et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2024.4369); [Tran et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40267-026-01266-5))

Drug-specific effects also require study. Molecule-specific randomized estimates remain sparse and imprecise, and one observational psychiatric cohort reported different associations for semaglutide, liraglutide, exenatide, and dulaglutide. Current evidence is not strong enough to conclude that one GLP-1 RA reliably improves or worsens mood relative to another. ([Ebrahimi et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2025.0091); [Taipale et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2215-0366%2826%2900014-3); [Carmellini et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/life15091422))

## Conflict of interest and funding context

The 2026 subject-level trial analysis reported no study funding and was conducted by FDA-affiliated investigators. However, manufacturers supplied the proprietary participant-level trial data, which are not fully available for independent reproduction. This is not the same as manufacturer sponsorship of the analysis, but the data-access limitation should be kept in mind. ([Tran et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40267-026-01266-5))

The broad 2025 randomized-trial meta-analysis was supported by public and nonprofit research organizations, including the National Institute for Health and Care Research, Medical Research Council, and Wellcome. Several authors disclosed research funding, consulting, or speaking relationships with pharmaceutical companies, including Novo Nordisk, outside the submitted work. The meta-analysis itself was not funded by a GLP-1 manufacturer, although several underlying drug-development trials were commercial clinical programs. ([Pierret et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2025.0679))

In the suicide and self-harm RCT meta-analysis, one author disclosed consulting for Novo Nordisk outside the submitted work; no other conflicts were reported. The findings are not being treated as sufficient on their own; they are considered alongside the FDA-affiliated subject-level analysis and active-comparator cohorts that reached broadly similar conclusions on suicidality. ([Ebrahimi et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2025.0091); [Tran et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40267-026-01266-5); [Shapiro et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2024-080679); [Ueda et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2024.4369); [Kuzucan et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjdrc-2025-005840))

The 2026 depression meta-analysis was supported by Chinese public and academic grants, and its authors declared no competing interests. Those disclosures do not resolve the report's internal methodological inconsistencies. The active-comparator depression study reported no funding and no conflicts of interest. ([Bi et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-026-08016-x); [Chang et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.70175))

The United Kingdom suicidality cohort received external support from the Canadian Institutes of Health Research rather than a GLP-1 manufacturer. The Swedish psychiatric cohort received foundation and public funding; some authors disclosed relationships with pharmaceutical companies, but these were not presented as funding for the study. ([Shapiro et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2024-080679); [Taipale et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2215-0366%2826%2900014-3))

The 2024 non-user cohort was funded by Chung Shan Medical University Hospital, and the authors declared no competing interests. Its concerning result therefore cannot simply be dismissed as industry bias; its lower evidentiary weight comes from its observational design, non-user comparator, outcome ascertainment, and residual-confounding risk. ([Kornelius et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-75965-2))

The qualitative patient-experience study was funded by the Stanford Center for Digital Health, with recruitment infrastructure supported by the National Institutes of Health. One author disclosed personal fees from Novo Nordisk and several other companies outside the submitted work; no other disclosures were reported. ([de Vere Hunt et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.16951))

The pharmacovigilance paper disclosed personal fees from several companies and a regulatory-committee role, but no GLP-1 manufacturer sponsorship was declared. The mechanistic psychiatric review declared no conflicts of interest. ([Schoretsanitis et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.23385); [Carmellini et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/life15091422))

The principal conclusion is supported across randomized, regulatory, and observational evidence from several independently conducted analyses. However, many source trials originated in commercial drug-development programs, and some participant-level data are unavailable for independent reproduction. ([Tran et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40267-026-01266-5); [Pierret et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2025.0679); [Ebrahimi et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2025.0091); [Shapiro et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2024-080679); [Ueda et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2024.4369); [Kuzucan et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjdrc-2025-005840))

## Practical interpretation

- Current evidence does not confirm that GLP-1 RAs increase suicidal ideation, self-harm, or suicide at the population level. Randomized-trial estimates are centered near no effect, and better-controlled active-comparator cohorts are also broadly reassuring. ([Tran et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40267-026-01266-5); [Shapiro et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2024-080679); [Ueda et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2024.4369); [Stanislaus et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41380-026-03619-y); [Kuzucan et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjdrc-2025-005840))

- Depression is neither an established class adverse effect nor an established treatment indication. Randomized trials show no average worsening, but conflicting observational findings leave uncertainty for particular drugs, exposure periods, and high-risk patients. ([Pierret et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2025.0679); [Chang et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.70175); [Taipale et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2215-0366%2826%2900014-3))

- Reduced food noise, improved eating control, weight-related quality of life, or self-image may improve well-being for some patients. These experiences should not be presented as proof of a direct antidepressant effect. ([Pierret et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2025.0679); [de Vere Hunt et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.16951))

- Baseline psychiatric history and changes in mood, anxiety, sleep, eating behavior, or suicidal thinking should be documented, particularly in patients with previous self-harm or active mental illness, because severe psychiatric illness and suicidality were exclusion criteria in many trials. Monitoring is a prudent response to limited evidence in these groups, not evidence that treatment is known to cause suicidality. ([Tran et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40267-026-01266-5); [Pierret et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2025.0679); [Ebrahimi et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2025.0091))

- New or worsening depression, suicidal thoughts, or unusual changes in mood or behavior require prompt clinical attention regardless of whether a medication is suspected. This does not require assuming that the GLP-1 RA caused the symptoms; current population-level evidence has not found an increased risk. ([Tran et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40267-026-01266-5); [Kuzucan et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjdrc-2025-005840))
