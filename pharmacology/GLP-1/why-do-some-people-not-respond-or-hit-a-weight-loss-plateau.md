# Why Do Some People Not Respond or Hit a Weight-Loss Plateau?

Average weight-loss results are group estimates, not individual forecasts. A person who loses little weight during dose escalation, someone who stops losing after a substantial reduction, and someone who regains weight after interrupted treatment may all be described as a “nonresponder,” but these are different trajectories with different explanations. ([Fujioka et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.21629); [Ard et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.16554); [Horn et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/cob.12734); [Aronne et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2023.24945))

The largest randomized trials discussed here concern semaglutide and tirzepatide. Evidence about predicting individual response is still developing, while direct evidence on switching, combination treatment, or bariatric surgery specifically in medication nonresponders remains limited. ([Wilding et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2032183); [Jastreboff et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2206038); [Alexander et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2025.8222); [Su et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10330-z); [Barenbaum et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.70179); [Tronieri et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.metabol.2019.03.005); [Barrett et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamasurg.2025.3590))

## Average results conceal large individual differences

In STEP 1, semaglutide 2.4 mg plus lifestyle intervention produced a mean weight reduction of 14.9 percent at 68 weeks, compared with 2.4 percent with placebo. However, 86.4 percent of participants receiving semaglutide achieved at least 5 percent weight loss, meaning that the average result did not apply to everyone. The treatment-policy analysis counted outcomes regardless of treatment discontinuation or rescue interventions, so failure to reach 5 percent cannot be interpreted as purely biological nonresponse. ([Wilding et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2032183))

In SURMOUNT-1, mean weight loss at 72 weeks was 15.0, 19.5, and 20.9 percent with tirzepatide 5, 10, and 15 mg, respectively, compared with 3.1 percent with placebo. The proportions reaching at least 5 percent weight loss were 85, 89, and 91 percent. These high averages still left a minority below the conventional 5-percent threshold, and the response distribution differed by dose. ([Jastreboff et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2206038))

**Funding note:** STEP 1 was funded by Novo Nordisk, the manufacturer of semaglutide, and SURMOUNT-1 was supported by Eli Lilly, the manufacturer of tirzepatide. Both trials establish strong average efficacy, but they do not independently establish why a particular person responds poorly. ([Wilding et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2032183); [Jastreboff et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2206038))

Funding concentration is a broader limitation of this literature. A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis found that 50 of 52 trials with characterizable funding, or 96.2 percent, were industry-funded. This does not invalidate the trials, but it makes independent replication of post-hoc response and plateau analyses especially important. ([Alexander et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2025.8222); [Ard et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.16554); [Horn et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/cob.12734))

## Nonresponse, slow response, plateau, and regain are different

Across the studies discussed here, there is no single definition of medication nonresponse or weight plateau. Common research definitions include failing to reach a specified percentage of weight loss by a fixed week, but the threshold and timing vary by drug and study design. ([Fujioka et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.21629); [Ard et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.16554); [Horn et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/cob.12734))

A pooled analysis of liraglutide trials found that achieving at least 4 percent weight loss by week 16 best predicted reaching at least 5 percent at one year. This provided a useful drug-specific early-response rule, but it was derived from the SCALE liraglutide program and cannot automatically be transferred to medications with different dose-escalation schedules or weight-loss trajectories. ([Fujioka et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.21629))

That limitation is illustrated by a 2025 post-hoc analysis of SURMOUNT-1. Participants with less than 5 percent weight loss at week 12 were classified as late responders. Among these participants, 70 percent reached at least 5 percent by week 24 and 90 percent reached it by week 72; the mean time to reach 5 percent was 24.8 weeks. Tirzepatide was still being escalated during much of this early period, making week 12 especially vulnerable to misclassifying slow responders as failures. ([Ard et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.16554))

However, the analysis included participants who received at least 75 percent of assigned doses and had weight measurements at weeks 0, 12, 24, and 72. It was therefore a selected trial cohort rather than a guarantee that 90 percent of apparent early nonresponders in routine care will eventually respond. The analysis was funded by Eli Lilly and included company employees, so the findings should be interpreted with that in mind. ([Ard et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.16554))

A tirzepatide plateau analysis used yet another definition: less than 5 percent weight change over a 12-week interval and every subsequent 12-week interval. Under this rule, even continued slow loss could qualify as a plateau. The analysis also excluded people who were poorly adherent or had not achieved at least 5 percent loss by the study endpoint. It is therefore an operational research definition, not a universal clinical definition. ([Horn et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/cob.12734))

**Funding note:** The plateau analysis was funded by Eli Lilly and several authors were company employees. Independent replication using broader populations and alternative plateau definitions is needed. ([Horn et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/cob.12734))

## How much of response is biological?

Biological differences clearly contribute to treatment response, but current predictors are much better at describing average differences between groups than forecasting what will happen to an individual. ([Alexander et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2025.8222); [Su et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10330-z))

A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis of 64 randomized trials found greater average GLP-1 receptor agonist weight loss among women than men: 10.9 versus 6.8 percent in the six trials that could be quantitatively combined. It did not detect statistically significant treatment-effect heterogeneity by age, race, ethnicity, baseline BMI, or baseline HbA1c. These null interaction findings do not establish equivalence, because subgroup reporting and statistical power were limited. They do argue against treating common demographic or baseline clinical characteristics as reliable individual response tests. ([Alexander et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2025.8222))

Candidate demographic, metabolic, and genetic correlates of response have been reported, but the evidence is heterogeneous and has not produced a validated individual prediction tool. Prognostic associations observed within treated cohorts should be distinguished from treatment-effect modifiers demonstrated through randomized treatment-by-subgroup interactions. Baseline BMI or HbA1c may correlate with outcomes in some datasets without being sufficiently consistent or causal to guide treatment selection. ([Alexander et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2025.8222); [Su et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10330-z))

Genetics also appears to contribute, but the currently identified effects are modest. In a survey cohort of 27,885 GLP-1 receptor agonist users, the primary European-ancestry efficacy genome-wide association study included 15,237 participants. A GLP1R variant was associated with approximately 0.76 kg of additional weight loss per effect allele. A broader model incorporating treatment identity, dose, treatment duration, clinical characteristics, demographics, disease variables, and genetics explained approximately 25 percent of variation in self-reported response, with most of the explained variation coming from non-genetic factors. ([Su et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10330-z))

The study relied mainly on self-reported treatment and weight data, its participants were predominantly female and of European ancestry, and all authors were current or former 23andMe employees. Because the model included treatment-exposure information rather than only pretreatment variables, it should not be presented as a validated pretreatment prediction tool. The results support a biological contribution to response but do not yet provide a clinical genetic test that can reliably identify medication nonresponders before treatment. ([Su et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10330-z))

## Dose, time, adherence, and treatment interruptions matter

A person assessed during dose escalation has not had the same treatment exposure as someone who has spent months at a stable maintenance dose. This is particularly relevant for medications whose trial protocols use gradual dose escalation before the maintenance dose. ([Ard et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.16554); [Aronne et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2023.24945); [Wharton et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2213-8587%2825%2900226-8))

The tirzepatide late-responder analysis found that higher assigned doses were associated with a greater probability of reaching weight-loss thresholds at weeks 24 and 72. Among late responders, mean week-72 weight loss was 8.3, 11.4, and 13.6 percent with the 5, 10, and 15 mg doses. Because late response was defined after randomization, these dose-specific subgroup differences are descriptive rather than a randomized test of whether dose escalation rescues an identified individual nonresponder. ([Ard et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.16554))

Treatment interruption is also common outside trials. In a cohort of 125,474 US adults initiating liraglutide, semaglutide, or tirzepatide, an estimated 46.5 percent of those with type 2 diabetes and 64.8 percent of those without diabetes met the study's discontinuation definition within one year. Discontinuation was operationalized as a dispensing gap of at least 60 days without any study GLP-1 medication on hand, rather than direct confirmation that a participant intentionally stopped taking the medication. Greater weight loss was associated with lower discontinuation, while diagnosed gastrointestinal events classified by the study as moderate or severe were associated with higher discontinuation. The adverse-event variable did not include common nausea or vomiting. ([Rodriguez et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.57349))

Because this was observational, the direction of causation is uncertain. Poorer weight loss may encourage discontinuation, while interruption may itself reduce weight loss; both can occur simultaneously. The study used Truveta data and several authors were Truveta employees. The article did not list external drug-manufacturer funding. ([Rodriguez et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.57349))

These findings show that dose exposure, adverse effects, access, and persistence can materially affect observed results. They do **not** establish that most apparent nonresponse is caused by poor adherence. Labeling a person as nonadherent without first examining tolerability, cost and access, treatment exposure, dose escalation, and interruptions risks confusing structural or treatment-related barriers with individual behavior. ([Ard et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.16554); [Rodriguez et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.57349))

## Why weight-loss plateaus can occur

Weight loss changes the conditions that produced it. As body weight decreases, total energy expenditure generally falls and physiological feedback tends to increase appetite. The intervention initially creates an energy deficit, but the deficit narrows until energy intake and expenditure approach a new equilibrium. At that point, continued treatment may maintain a lower weight without producing substantial additional loss. ([Hall, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.24027))

A previously validated energy-balance model comparing dietary restriction, semaglutide, tirzepatide, and Roux-en-Y gastric bypass generated preliminary estimates suggesting that GLP-1-based treatment and surgery attenuate the appetite-feedback response that opposes weight loss, allowing weight loss to continue longer than it usually does with dietary restriction alone. This was a model-derived mechanistic inference, not a direct participant-level measurement of why any one person plateaued. ([Hall, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.24027))

In the post-hoc tirzepatide analysis, median time to the study-defined plateau ranged from approximately 24 to 36 weeks across baseline BMI categories. Higher doses, younger age, and female sex were associated with later plateaus. However, the study selected adherent participants who ultimately lost at least 5 percent, so it describes plateau timing among trial responders rather than the full treated population. ([Horn et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/cob.12734))

## A plateau does not necessarily mean the medication has stopped working

The clearest evidence comes from randomized withdrawal trials. In SURMOUNT-4, participants lost an average of 20.9 percent during a 36-week open-label tirzepatide period. They were then randomized either to continue tirzepatide or switch to placebo. Over the following 52 weeks, those continuing treatment lost another 5.5 percent on average, while those switched to placebo regained 14.0 percent. Participants entered randomization only after completing the lead-in and tolerating maximum tolerated tirzepatide doses of 10 or 15 mg, and the group had already achieved substantial average weight loss. ([Aronne et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2023.24945))

This shows that, among selected treatment completers and tolerators after substantial average loss, slowing or stopping further weight loss does not necessarily mean that a drug is providing no benefit. Continued treatment may be actively maintaining the lower weight, and removing it may expose the physiological pressure toward regain. The trial does not establish that treatment remains beneficial for someone who never meaningfully responded, or that every plateau represents successful pharmacological maintenance. ([Aronne et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2023.24945))

**Funding note:** SURMOUNT-4 was sponsored by Eli Lilly. The company was involved in study design and conduct, data collection and analysis, interpretation, manuscript preparation, and the decision to submit the manuscript for publication; several authors were employees and shareholders. The randomized withdrawal result is strong evidence, but the extensive sponsor involvement should be kept explicit. ([Aronne et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2023.24945))

## Does increasing the dose overcome a plateau?

Higher doses can improve average weight loss, but this does not prove that dose escalation will rescue a person already identified as a nonresponder. ([Wharton et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2213-8587%2825%2900226-8))

In the STEP UP randomized trial, semaglutide 7.2 mg produced mean weight loss of 18.7 percent at 72 weeks, compared with 15.6 percent with 2.4 mg and 3.9 percent with placebo. The average advantage over 2.4 mg was 3.1 percentage points. However, participants were randomized at baseline to semaglutide strategies targeting 7.2 mg or 2.4 mg, or to placebo, and underwent titration. The trial did not enroll people who had failed or plateaued on 2.4 mg and then randomize them to remain at 2.4 mg or escalate. ([Wharton et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2213-8587%2825%2900226-8))

**Funding note:** STEP UP was funded by Novo Nordisk and included company authors. It supports a higher-dose average effect, not a validated rescue algorithm for individual nonresponders. ([Wharton et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2213-8587%2825%2900226-8))

## What is known about switching medications?

The direct switching evidence discussed here is observational. In a 2026 single-center retrospective cohort, 61 patients switched from therapeutic-dose semaglutide to tirzepatide because of nonresponse or a plateau. Their mean additional loss over six months was 5.3 percent. Those classified as having plateaued lost approximately 8.1 percent, while initial nonresponders lost approximately 2.9 percent. ([Barenbaum et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.70179))

This suggests that switching can produce additional loss, especially after a previous response followed by a plateau, but it does not prove that switching is superior to continued treatment or dose adjustment. The study was small, retrospective, lacked a contemporaneous randomized comparison, and could not fully remove treatment-selection and adherence differences. Regression to the mean and continued weight loss over time could also have contributed. One author reported research funding from Novo Nordisk, which should also be considered when interpreting the result. ([Barenbaum et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.70179))

## What is known about combination treatment?

The randomized evidence discussed here for adding another anti-obesity medication after a plateau comes from a small pilot trial. ([Tronieri et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.metabol.2019.03.005))

In a 45-person pilot trial, participants who had already lost approximately 12.6 percent after one year of liraglutide and behavioral treatment were randomized to add phentermine or placebo for 12 weeks. Additional weight loss was 1.6 percent with phentermine and 0.1 percent with placebo, a difference that was not statistically significant. ([Tronieri et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.metabol.2019.03.005))

The randomized extension was supported in part by an investigator-sponsored study award from Novo Nordisk to the University of Pennsylvania. The authors reported that Novo Nordisk had no role in study design, data collection or analysis, or manuscript preparation. The small sample and short duration leave substantial uncertainty and do not establish either no effect or a clinically meaningful added benefit from combination treatment. ([Tronieri et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.metabol.2019.03.005))

## When bariatric surgery enters the discussion

In the observational study discussed below, metabolic bariatric surgery was associated with greater average weight loss than GLP-1 medication in a selected population with severe obesity, but the study did not define when an early medication nonresponder should transition to surgery. ([Barrett et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamasurg.2025.3590))

A 2025 claims and electronic-health-record cohort compared GLP-1 receptor agonist treatment with metabolic bariatric surgery in people with class II or III obesity. For the longitudinal weight comparison, investigators used a selected electronic-health-record subset with baseline BMI of at least 40 and available baseline and follow-up weights. Mean total loss at the last recorded follow-up weight was 10.3 percent with GLP-1 medications and 28.3 percent after surgery. However, weight data were available for only 257 medication-treated patients and 1,291 surgical patients, representing 1.6 and 9.2 percent of the respective full cohorts. Follow-up timing varied, and the medication group combined different agents and formulations. ([Barrett et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamasurg.2025.3590))

The comparison was observational rather than randomized and was not restricted to standardized medication nonresponders. The limited and unequal availability of longitudinal weights creates potential selection and measurement bias. The study therefore supports a larger observed loss after surgery in this selected population but cannot establish that surgery is the correct next step after an arbitrary early medication threshold. ([Barrett et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamasurg.2025.3590))

**Funding note:** The study received funding from Medtronic for consulting and medical-writing services. One author reported fees from Medtronic, Novo Nordisk, and Eli Lilly, while another was employed by the medical-writing company. The paper reported that Medtronic had no role in study design, analysis, manuscript preparation, or the decision to submit the manuscript for publication. ([Barrett et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamasurg.2025.3590))

## What the competing claims get right

1. **“Response is primarily biological and will eventually be predictable.”** Biology and genetics contribute, but current predictors explain only part of the variation and are not sufficiently validated for individual treatment selection. Group-level sex differences and modest genetic associations should not be mistaken for deterministic tests. ([Alexander et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2025.8222); [Su et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10330-z))

2. **“Most apparent nonresponse reflects inadequate dose, adherence, interruption, or insufficient time.”** These factors often matter and are common outside trials, but the evidence does not establish that they explain most nonresponse. Early assessment during titration can be misleading, while observational discontinuation data cannot cleanly separate cause from consequence. ([Ard et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.16554); [Rodriguez et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.57349))

3. **“Weight-loss plateaus are normal and should not automatically be treated as medication failure.”** This is the best-supported of the four claims. Plateaus are an expected consequence of energy-balance and appetite feedback. Randomized withdrawal evidence in selected treatment completers after substantial average loss shows that a plateau can coexist with an ongoing maintenance effect, but it does not establish that the same is true for persistent low response from treatment initiation. ([Hall, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.24027); [Aronne et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2023.24945))

4. **“Early nonresponders should switch, escalate, combine treatments, or proceed to surgery.”** Any of these may be reasonable in selected cases, but none is supported as a universal early-response rule. Dose-escalation trials were not designed as rescue trials, switching evidence is observational, combination evidence is small, and surgery comparisons have not randomized standardized medication nonresponders. ([Wharton et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2213-8587%2825%2900226-8); [Barenbaum et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.70179); [Tronieri et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.metabol.2019.03.005); [Barrett et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamasurg.2025.3590))


## Evidence gaps

The differing definitions used in current studies support the need for consensus definitions that distinguish early slow response, inadequate exposure, persistent low response, plateau after meaningful loss, regain during treatment, and regain after interruption. ([Fujioka et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.21629); [Ard et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.16554); [Horn et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/cob.12734))

Before predictive models are used to guide treatment, they need external validation in diverse populations and should show that using the prediction improves treatment decisions, not merely that a variable is statistically associated with weight change. ([Alexander et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2025.8222); [Su et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10330-z))

A particularly important practical gap in the evidence reviewed here is the absence of randomized adaptive trials that identify participants with a standardized low response after adequate titration and then compare continued treatment, higher dosing, switching, combination therapy, and surgical referral. ([Wharton et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2213-8587%2825%2900226-8); [Barenbaum et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.70179); [Tronieri et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.metabol.2019.03.005); [Barrett et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamasurg.2025.3590))

Independent replication is especially important. Among the 52 randomized trials with characterizable funding in a 2026 systematic review, 50 were industry-funded. The post-hoc tirzepatide response and plateau analyses discussed above were also funded by Eli Lilly. ([Alexander et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2025.8222); [Ard et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.16554); [Horn et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/cob.12734))

## Practical interpretation

- A small early loss should not automatically be called treatment failure, particularly while a medication is still being escalated. Early-response rules need to be specific to the drug and dosing schedule. ([Fujioka et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.21629); [Ard et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.16554))

- Before concluding that someone is biologically resistant, the evaluation should distinguish the prescribed target dose from the dose actually tolerated and received, and account for interruptions, adverse effects, access, and actual time at a stable dose. These factors should be assessed without assuming that limited response reflects a lack of effort. ([Aronne et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2023.24945); [Rodriguez et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.57349))

- A plateau after meaningful loss may represent a successful maintenance phase rather than absence of drug effect. In selected treatment completers and tolerators after substantial average loss, withdrawal data show that removing treatment can lead to substantial regain. ([Hall, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.24027); [Aronne et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2023.24945))

- Early response contains useful prognostic information, but it is not destiny. Tirzepatide data show that many people below 5 percent at week 12 continue to lose weight over subsequent months. ([Ard et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.16554))

- Dose escalation, switching, combination treatment, and surgery should be judged by total achieved benefit, ongoing trajectory, tolerability, health needs, risks, and treatment burden, not by a single universal percentage or week number. The evidence reviewed here does not provide a validated sequence that applies to everyone. ([Wharton et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2213-8587%2825%2900226-8); [Barenbaum et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.70179); [Tronieri et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.metabol.2019.03.005); [Barrett et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamasurg.2025.3590))
