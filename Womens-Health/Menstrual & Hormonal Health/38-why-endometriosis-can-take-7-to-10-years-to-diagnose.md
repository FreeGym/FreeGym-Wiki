# Why Endometriosis Can Take 7-10 Years to Diagnose

Endometriosis is often described as affecting about 1 in 10 women of reproductive age, although its exact prevalence is uncertain and estimates in the general female population have ranged from approximately 2% to 10%, depending on the population, case definition, and diagnostic method. The familiar "7-10 years" diagnostic-delay figure is best understood as shorthand for heterogeneous published estimates rather than a stable biological or global average. [World Health Organization](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/endometriosis) [ESHRE guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8951218/) [De Corte et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11625652/)

The evidence does not support one isolated explanation for the delay. A more defensible synthesis is a series of interacting bottlenecks: symptoms are normalized, initial clinical encounters do not always trigger escalation, referral may be slow, imaging performance depends on lesion type and operator expertise, and surgery remains important in selected, but no longer all, cases. Disease heterogeneity can act across this entire pathway rather than functioning as a completely separate cause. [Davenport et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37441792/) [De Corte et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11625652/) [ESHRE guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8951218/) [Nisenblat et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26919512/)

Among the competing claims, delayed clinical recognition and referral are among the barriers most consistently reported in qualitative and observational research. Imaging limitations are also important, particularly for superficial peritoneal disease, while disease heterogeneity can make both recognition and testing more difficult. Symptom-based risk scores and biomarkers now exist, but the published studies have not yet shown that routine use in ordinary clinical pathways shortens diagnosis or improves patient outcomes. [Davenport et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37441792/) [De Corte et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11625652/) [ESHRE guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8951218/) [Fauconnier et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34538656/) [Mishra et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2026.103806) [Bendifallah et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41147827/) [NICE development project](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/indevelopment/gid-hte10082/documents)

## What the "7-10 years" figure actually measures

A recent systematic review identified 17 observational studies published since 2018. Reported diagnostic intervals ranged from 0.3 to 12 years, depending on the population, data source, and definition of delay. The review separated the pathway into the following intervals. [De Corte et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11625652/)

- **Overall diagnostic time:** first symptom to physician diagnosis. [De Corte et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11625652/)
- **Primary interval:** first symptom to the first related medical consultation. [De Corte et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11625652/)
- **Clinical interval:** first consultation to diagnosis. [De Corte et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11625652/)

In predominantly cross-sectional studies, mean overall times ranged from 5.4 to 11.4 years and median times from 5 to 12 years. The primary interval was generally 1-4 years, while the clinical interval ranged from 2.3 to 8.6 years. Retrospective medical-record studies reported shorter clinical intervals: means of 2.1-3.7 years and medians of 0.3-1.5 years. The review concluded that both patient and clinician intervals contribute, but that longer delays generally occur after medical contact. [De Corte et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11625652/) [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39373298/)

These estimates have important limitations. All included studies were observational and came from Western high-income countries. Many were online or cross-sectional surveys, and in 13 of 17 studies the method used to confirm the diagnosis was reported by the participant rather than independently verified by researchers. Study quality ranged from poor to good, definitions were inconsistent, and some datasets included older diagnostic experiences. Survey-based estimates are vulnerable to recall and participation bias, while medical records may omit symptoms or consultations that occurred before the available record. Consequently, "7-10 years" is a reasonable description of many historical and survey-based cohorts, but not a universal present-day average, and "confirmed diagnosis" does not always mean surgical or histologic confirmation. [De Corte et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11625652/) [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39373298/)

## Claim 1: Delayed clinical recognition is consistently documented

A 2023 qualitative systematic review included 13 studies examining the experiences of patients and health professionals. It identified four interacting categories of barriers: individual factors, interpersonal influences, health-system factors, and factors specific to endometriosis. [Davenport et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37441792/)

The barriers included difficulty distinguishing pathologic pain from "normal" menstruation, self-management rather than help-seeking, menstrual stigma, normalization of period pain by families and clinicians, limited professional training, dismissive attitudes, delayed specialist referrals, variable symptoms, overlap with other conditions, and the absence of a universally reliable non-invasive test. [Davenport et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37441792/)

These findings show how normalization can occur at more than one point in the pathway. [Davenport et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37441792/)

1. A young person may be taught that severe menstrual pain is normal. [Davenport et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37441792/)
2. Family members or peers may reinforce that interpretation. [Davenport et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37441792/)
3. A clinician may treat the symptoms as uncomplicated dysmenorrhea without documenting their functional impact. [Davenport et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37441792/)
4. Repeated visits may result in changing analgesics or contraceptives without a defined threshold for imaging, referral, or reconsideration of the diagnosis. [Davenport et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37441792/)

Observational evidence is consistent with these qualitative findings. One study included in the diagnostic-delay review found that people who believed their general practitioner had not taken their pain seriously experienced approximately twice the median time to diagnosis. However, the systematic review cautioned that such subgroup findings usually came from single studies and cannot establish a precise causal effect. [De Corte et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11625652/)

Being advised to use ibuprofen or another nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug is not, by itself, evidence of dismissal. Analgesics and hormonal treatment can be appropriate first-line symptom management while endometriosis remains suspected. The risk of delay increases when treatment continues without planned reassessment or escalation despite persistent, disabling symptoms. A practical safety net can include reassessment after treatment failure, attention to cyclical bowel or urinary symptoms, documentation of missed school or work, and referral when symptoms remain disabling. Current guidance permits empirical treatment and recommends further evaluation when imaging is negative and empirical treatment is unsuccessful or inappropriate. [ESHRE guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8951218/) [Davenport et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37441792/) [ACOG guideline](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/clinical-practice-guideline/articles/2026/03/diagnosis-of-endometriosis)

**Evidence verdict:** Clinical recognition and referral are likely important contributors and are among the most consistently documented barriers. The available evidence does not determine what percentage of the total delay they cause, compare their independent causal contribution with every other bottleneck, or prove that they are the sole primary cause. [Davenport et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37441792/) [De Corte et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11625652/)

## Claim 2: Diagnostic tools contribute, but imaging is not uniformly ineffective

The statement that ultrasound and MRI "miss endometriosis" is correct for some disease phenotypes but too broad when applied to all endometriosis. [ESHRE guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8951218/) [Nisenblat et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26919512/)

A Cochrane diagnostic-accuracy review found that neither ultrasound nor MRI was accurate enough to replace surgery for **all pelvic endometriosis**. Performance was much better for specific, visible phenotypes. For ovarian endometriomas, transvaginal ultrasound had an estimated sensitivity of 0.93 and specificity of 0.96, while MRI had a sensitivity of 0.95 and specificity of 0.91, although the MRI evidence came from relatively few participants. [Nisenblat et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26919512/)

A 2025 head-to-head meta-analysis of rectosigmoid deep endometriosis likewise found similar pooled performance for transvaginal ultrasound and MRI. Pooled sensitivity was 0.85 for ultrasound and 0.83 for MRI; specificity was 0.92 and 0.95, respectively. Neither difference was statistically significant. These estimates apply specifically to rectosigmoid deep disease in predominantly selected specialist or surgical cohorts, not to all endometriosis phenotypes or routine imaging settings. Between-study heterogeneity was very high, and ultrasound performance was particularly dependent on operator expertise and the examination protocol. [Xu et al.](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medicine/articles/10.3389/fmed.2025.1552185/full)

A major blind spot is **superficial peritoneal endometriosis**. Current guidance explicitly warns that a negative ultrasound or MRI does not exclude endometriosis, particularly superficial disease. A normal scan should therefore not be interpreted as "no endometriosis"; a more precise interpretation is that no endometrioma or readily detectable deep disease was seen. [ESHRE guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8951218/)

The second part of the claim, that laparoscopy is the only definitive diagnostic route, is increasingly too absolute. Surgery may be considered in circumstances such as the following. [ESHRE guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8951218/) [ACOG guideline](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/clinical-practice-guideline/articles/2026/03/diagnosis-of-endometriosis)

- Imaging is negative but symptoms remain strongly suggestive.
- Empirical treatment fails, is contraindicated, or is unacceptable.
- Infertility or anatomic distortion requires clarification.
- Operative treatment is being considered.
- Histology is needed to evaluate an uncertain lesion or alternative diagnosis.

The 2022 ESHRE guideline concludes that neither immediate diagnostic laparoscopy nor imaging combined with empirical hormonal treatment has proved superior for symptom management. Both pathways may be considered through shared decision-making. Even histology is imperfect: negative histology does not entirely exclude endometriosis. A 2026 ACOG guideline also provides recommendations on the clinical, imaging, and surgical evaluation and diagnosis of endometriosis. [ESHRE guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8951218/) [ACOG guideline](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/clinical-practice-guideline/articles/2026/03/diagnosis-of-endometriosis)

Delayed specialist referral and other health-system barriers appear repeatedly in qualitative research. The diagnostic-delay review, however, does not isolate surgical waiting time as a distinct share of the overall interval. Surgical access may therefore contribute in some settings, but its independent contribution is less precisely quantified than the broader recognition-and-referral problem. [Davenport et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37441792/) [De Corte et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11625652/)

**Evidence verdict:** Diagnostic-tool limitations can be an important contributor, especially when non-expert imaging or a negative scan is used to rule out superficial disease. Imaging is substantially more effective for endometriomas and some deep lesions, and surgery is no longer considered mandatory before every patient can receive a working diagnosis and treatment. [ESHRE guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8951218/) [Nisenblat et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26919512/) [Xu et al.](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medicine/articles/10.3389/fmed.2025.1552185/full)

## Claim 3: Disease heterogeneity can amplify the other bottlenecks

Endometriosis is commonly divided into superficial peritoneal disease, deep endometriosis, and ovarian endometrioma. Its presentation can include dysmenorrhea, non-menstrual pelvic pain, deep dyspareunia, dyschezia, dysuria, cyclical rectal or urinary bleeding, fatigue, infertility, and less common thoracic or scar symptoms. Some patients have several of these manifestations; others present mainly with infertility or apparently unrelated gastrointestinal symptoms. [ESHRE guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8951218/)

This heterogeneity affects diagnosis in two ways.

First, it affects **recognition**. A person with severe cyclical dysmenorrhea can follow a different diagnostic route from someone presenting primarily with infertility, painful bowel movements, urinary symptoms, or non-cyclical pain. Symptoms can also overlap with primary dysmenorrhea, irritable bowel syndrome, bladder disorders, pelvic-floor dysfunction, adenomyosis, and other causes of chronic pelvic pain. [ESHRE guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8951218/) [Davenport et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37441792/)

Second, it affects **test detectability**. Endometriomas and many deep lesions produce recognizable anatomic findings, while superficial peritoneal disease is more difficult to identify non-invasively and may remain undetected despite negative imaging. [ESHRE guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8951218/) [Nisenblat et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26919512/)

Evidence that heterogeneity independently accounts for a particular number of years is limited. The diagnostic-delay review found occasional subtype and symptom-related differences, but most came from individual studies and were not sufficiently consistent to establish a separate causal contribution. [De Corte et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11625652/)

**Evidence verdict:** Heterogeneity is real and clinically important. A useful conceptual interpretation, rather than a quantified causal estimate, is that it acts as an amplifier: it can make symptoms harder to recognize, reduce the sensitivity of any single imaging pathway, and complicate the construction of one universal risk-stratification rule. Its independent causal contribution to diagnostic delay has not been quantified. [ESHRE guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8951218/) [De Corte et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11625652/)

## Claim 4: Risk-stratification tools and biomarkers exist, but clinical utility remains uncertain

### Symptom-based risk stratification

A 2021 prospective case-control diagnostic study evaluated a 21-item patient-completed questionnaire in three French referral centres. It included 105 women with histologically confirmed endometriosis and 197 controls. After internal validation, the full model had an area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve of 0.92. Its high-risk rule had 98% specificity, while its low-risk rule had 98.1% sensitivity. These figures came from two separate thresholds, not one operating point that simultaneously achieved both values. [Fauconnier et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34538656/)

Those results show that symptom combinations can be informative. They do not establish a ready-made population-screening program. The study used a case-control design with selected cases from specialist centres, was internally rather than independently validated, and did not test whether deploying the questionnaire in primary care shortened diagnosis, prevented unnecessary surgery, or improved symptoms. Cases and controls were also highly selected and were not all verified with the same reference standard, creating potential spectrum and differential-verification concerns. [Fauconnier et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34538656/) [STARD 2015](https://www.bmj.com/content/351/bmj.h5527)

A newer example is the 2026 Simplified Adolescent Factors for Endometriosis, or SAFE, score, developed using the Australian Longitudinal Study on Women's Health. It uses six factors related to pelvic-pain severity, menstrual disorders, and family history. The simplified score had an AUC of 0.79 in the training and internal test samples but 0.71 in the external age-cohort test. At a threshold of at least 2, sensitivity in that age-cohort test was 46.7% and specificity was 85.7%. At that threshold, it identified fewer than half of participants subsequently recorded as having an endometriosis diagnosis in that cohort. The validation used a different age cohort within the same longitudinal-study infrastructure, rather than a fully independent geographic or health-system population. The score may assist referral decisions, but the authors described it as warranting clinical evaluation rather than as a diagnostic test. The research was publicly funded. [Mishra et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2026.103806)

For interpretation, it is useful to distinguish between:

- **Prediction performance:** whether a score separates higher-risk from lower-risk groups.
- **Clinical utility:** whether using it in practice improves decisions and patient outcomes.

The 2022 ESHRE guideline found no evidence at that time that symptom diaries, questionnaires, or apps reduced time to diagnosis, although they might improve history-taking and help patients communicate the pattern and severity of their symptoms. [ESHRE guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8951218/)

### Non-invasive biomarkers

Older blood, urine, menstrual-fluid, and endometrial-tissue biomarkers generally lacked sufficient accuracy or validation, leading the 2022 ESHRE guideline to recommend against using them diagnostically. That recommendation was based on literature searched through December 2020 and therefore predates important newer research. [ESHRE guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8951218/)

In 2025, a prospective multicentre external-validation study evaluated a saliva microRNA signature in 971 symptomatic patients aged 18-43. Endometriosis prevalence in the study cohort was 77%. The reported sensitivity was 97.3%, specificity 94.1%, and overall accuracy 96.6%. Patients classified as controls all underwent laparoscopy, although cases could be classified using imaging, laparoscopy, or both. [Bendifallah et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41147827/)

These are unusually strong diagnostic-accuracy results, but several cautions remain:

- The cohort was already symptomatic and had a very high disease prevalence. Positive and negative predictive values would differ in a lower-prevalence primary-care population, and test performance can also vary with disease spectrum and case mix. [Bendifallah et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41147827/) [STARD 2015](https://www.bmj.com/content/351/bmj.h5527)
- The mixed reference standard creates a risk of differential-verification bias and may affect which disease phenotypes are represented among cases and controls. [Bendifallah et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41147827/) [STARD 2015](https://www.bmj.com/content/351/bmj.h5527)
- The validation study did not test whether routine use shortens the diagnostic pathway, changes treatment decisions, improves quality of life, or is cost-effective. [Bendifallah et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41147827/)
- Independent, non-commercial replication and diagnostic-impact trials remain important.

Most importantly, the study was funded by **Ziwig, the manufacturer of the test**, and an author listed an affiliation with Ziwig. The findings should therefore not be dismissed, but they should be interpreted alongside the financial conflict and should not be treated as sufficient, on their own, for universal adoption. [Bendifallah et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41147827/)

NICE's current early-use assessment project evaluates Endosure, Dot Endo, and Endotest for people with suspected endometriosis after a normal examination and negative imaging, or when imaging is unacceptable. Its stated outcomes include diagnostic accuracy, time to referral, time to diagnosis, and time to treatment. The project remains an in-development assessment rather than evidence of unrestricted routine adoption. [NICE development project](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/indevelopment/gid-hte10082/documents)

**Evidence verdict:** It is no longer accurate to say that no promising risk-stratification tools or biomarkers exist. The central gap is not simply slow adoption; it is the absence of sufficiently independent, pathway-level evidence showing that these tools produce better decisions and outcomes in the populations where they would actually be used. [ESHRE guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8951218/) [Mishra et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2026.103806) [Bendifallah et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41147827/) [NICE development project](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/indevelopment/gid-hte10082/documents)

## Why the causes interact

The diagnostic pathway can be represented as a sequence:

**Symptoms begin -> symptoms are normalized or self-managed -> first clinical contact -> symptomatic treatment without a defined escalation rule -> delayed referral -> routine or non-expert imaging -> possible false reassurance after negative imaging -> specialist imaging or empirical treatment -> selective laparoscopy or continued diagnostic uncertainty.** [Davenport et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37441792/) [De Corte et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11625652/) [ESHRE guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8951218/)

This sequence is an evidence-informed conceptual model rather than a demonstrated universal causal pathway. Patients may enter at different points, skip stages, or repeat them. Heterogeneity can operate at every stage by changing which symptoms are reported, which alternative diagnoses are considered, which clinician receives the referral, and whether imaging can identify the lesions. [Davenport et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37441792/) [ESHRE guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8951218/) [Nisenblat et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26919512/)

This proposed serial-bottleneck model may also help explain why individual patients have very different experiences. A person with a conspicuous endometrioma may be diagnosed after one scan, while someone with superficial disease, bowel symptoms, or adolescent-onset dysmenorrhea may cycle through multiple consultations despite equally severe symptoms. [Davenport et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37441792/) [ESHRE guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8951218/) [Nisenblat et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26919512/)

## Quick assessment of the competing claims

- **Claim 1 - clinical recognition:** Supported as a recurrent barrier. Normalization, inadequate training, repeated symptom management, and delayed referral are among the most consistently reported problems. The evidence does not prove that they account for most delay in every health system. [Davenport et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37441792/) [De Corte et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11625652/)
- **Claim 2 - diagnostic tools and surgical access:** Partly supported. Negative imaging cannot exclude superficial disease, but expert ultrasound and MRI perform well for several deep and ovarian phenotypes. Laparoscopy remains important but is not required before all empirical treatment or working diagnoses. [ESHRE guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8951218/) [Nisenblat et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26919512/) [Xu et al.](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medicine/articles/10.3389/fmed.2025.1552185/full)
- **Claim 3 - heterogeneity:** Supported as a clinically useful conceptual amplifier, but not quantified as an independent dominant cause. No single symptom or scan captures every phenotype. [ESHRE guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8951218/) [De Corte et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11625652/)
- **Claim 4 - tools exist but adoption is slow:** Partly supported but incomplete. Risk scores and high-performing biomarker candidates exist; the main evidence gap is limited external, independent, and implementation evidence, not merely professional reluctance to adopt innovation. [Mishra et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2026.103806) [Bendifallah et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41147827/) [NICE development project](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/indevelopment/gid-hte10082/documents)

## Evidence still needed

The strongest next studies would be prospective **inception cohorts** that enroll patients at their first presentation rather than surveying them years after diagnosis. They should use standardized definitions of symptom-to-consultation, consultation-to-referral, referral-to-imaging, and imaging-to-diagnosis intervals, with analyses stratified by lesion phenotype, age at symptom onset, race and ethnicity, socioeconomic position, geography, and healthcare system.

Symptom-based pathways need pragmatic or cluster-randomized trials comparing usual care with a defined escalation algorithm, for example, referral or expert imaging after persistent function-limiting cyclical symptoms or failure of first-line treatment. Outcomes should include time to diagnosis, symptom control, false referrals, missed disease, healthcare use, and patient experience.

Imaging research should compare standardized expert ultrasound, routine ultrasound, and MRI using the same reference standards. Results should be reported separately for superficial, ovarian, rectosigmoid, bladder, uterosacral, and other deep disease rather than pooled into "any endometriosis."

Biomarker studies need to move from diagnostic accuracy to diagnostic impact. A useful trial would test the biomarker prospectively in primary and secondary care and measure whether it reduces time to an appropriate management decision, avoids unnecessary surgery, detects imaging-negative disease, changes quality of life, and remains cost-effective across populations with different pretest probabilities.

Qualitative research remains necessary, but the next step is to connect patient-clinician interaction findings to tested interventions: clinician education, structured symptom documentation, explicit follow-up thresholds, electronic referral prompts, and feedback when an earlier diagnosis was missed.

## Limits and open debates

Direct evidence that earlier formal diagnosis, as distinct from earlier symptom recognition and treatment, improves long-term quality of life is limited. The ESHRE guideline found no adequate studies comparing early with late diagnosis for quality-of-life outcomes. This evidentiary gap is not evidence that earlier recognition and appropriate management provide no benefit. The guideline nevertheless emphasized that symptom relief should not be withheld while diagnostic certainty is pursued. [ESHRE guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8951218/)

This matters because reducing delay should not be defined solely as performing more laparoscopies sooner. A better target is reducing the interval to **appropriate recognition, informed discussion, symptom treatment, specialist assessment when needed, and a diagnosis sufficiently reliable to guide decisions**. [ESHRE guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8951218/) [ACOG guideline](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/clinical-practice-guideline/articles/2026/03/diagnosis-of-endometriosis)

## Practical interpretation

- The "7-10 years" figure describes many survey-based experiences but is not a universal contemporary average. [De Corte et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11625652/)
- Recognition and referral failures are among the most consistently documented contributors to prolonged delay. [Davenport et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37441792/) [De Corte et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11625652/)
- A normal ultrasound or MRI does not exclude endometriosis, particularly superficial peritoneal disease. [ESHRE guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8951218/)
- Expert imaging is nevertheless highly useful for ovarian and several deep-disease phenotypes. [Nisenblat et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26919512/) [Xu et al.](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medicine/articles/10.3389/fmed.2025.1552185/full)
- Laparoscopy is an important selective diagnostic and treatment tool, not a mandatory prerequisite for every working diagnosis. [ESHRE guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8951218/) [ACOG guideline](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/clinical-practice-guideline/articles/2026/03/diagnosis-of-endometriosis)
- Disease heterogeneity makes recognition and testing harder but has not been independently quantified as the main cause. [ESHRE guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8951218/) [De Corte et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11625652/)
- Symptom scores and saliva microRNA testing are promising; neither yet replaces the need for clinical judgment, appropriate imaging, pathway-level validation, and independent evidence. [Mishra et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2026.103806) [Bendifallah et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41147827/) [NICE development project](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/indevelopment/gid-hte10082/documents)

The most defensible conclusion is therefore not that endometriosis takes years to diagnose because clinicians dismiss symptoms, because scans fail, or because surgery is inaccessible. Available evidence is instead consistent with interacting bottlenecks that may occur in sequence: symptoms are normalized, clinical escalation is delayed, heterogeneous disease produces ambiguous or negative tests, and specialist confirmation remains difficult to obtain. The order and contribution of these bottlenecks vary among patients and health systems, and current evidence does not quantify the independent causal share of each. [Davenport et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37441792/) [De Corte et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11625652/) [ESHRE guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8951218/) [Nisenblat et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26919512/)

## Conflict-of-interest note

The recent diagnostic-delay systematic review reported no author conflicts, as did the qualitative systematic review. The 2025 imaging meta-analysis reported no relevant commercial or financial relationships. [De Corte et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11625652/) [Davenport et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37441792/) [Xu et al.](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medicine/articles/10.3389/fmed.2025.1552185/full)

The ESHRE guideline was funded by ESHRE and its members were not paid for guideline work, but several panel members disclosed pharmaceutical, device-company, advisory, or other industry relationships; one reported a pending serum endometriosis-biomarker patent. Its recommendations should therefore be interpreted alongside both its structured methodology and these disclosures. [ESHRE guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8951218/)

The saliva microRNA validation study had the most direct manufacturer involvement among the evidence reviewed: it was funded by the test manufacturer, Ziwig, with an author affiliated with that company. [Bendifallah et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41147827/)
