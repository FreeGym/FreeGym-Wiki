# Is Insulin Resistance the Actual Engine of PMOS, Not the Ovaries?

Insulin resistance is not the universal cause of polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome, formerly called polycystic ovary syndrome, but it is one of its most powerful amplifiers. The compensatory hyperinsulinemia that often accompanies it can directly increase androgen exposure. In many people, particularly those with the classic hyperandrogenic, anovulatory phenotype, this metabolic component may be a major process sustaining androgen excess and disrupted ovulation. [JCI](https://www.jci.org/articles/view/202824) [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23476073/)

The evidence does not support replacing an "ovary-only" model with an "insulin-only" model. It supports a feedback-network model: hyperinsulinemia can stimulate ovarian androgen production and lower sex hormone-binding globulin, while androgen excess, adiposity, altered gonadotropin signaling, adrenal androgen production, and intrinsic ovarian abnormalities can feed back into insulin resistance and anovulation. [JCI](https://www.jci.org/articles/view/202824)

The naming debate has also changed materially. On May 12, 2026, a global consensus process adopted **polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS)** as the new name for the condition. The new name explicitly recognizes that this is a multisystem endocrine and metabolic syndrome, not simply an ovarian-cyst disorder. It does not, however, declare insulin resistance to be the single initiating cause, and implementation across clinical practice, coding, research, and education remains a transition. [The Lancet](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736%2826%2900717-8/fulltext)

_Evidence approach: This article prioritizes systematic reviews and meta-analyses over individual trials, with observational and mechanistic studies used where they directly address causation or phenotype differences. Funding and financial conflicts are reported for the principal evidence syntheses._

## The strongest model is a feedback loop, not a single engine

Insulin can directly affect the ovary. In cultured theca cells obtained from women with the syndrome, insulin stimulated testosterone production through the insulin receptor and inositolglycan signaling. Insulin can also potentiate luteinizing hormone-driven steroid production, effectively acting as a metabolic "co-gonadotropin." These experiments establish biological plausibility for a direct insulin-to-ovary pathway, although isolated-cell studies cannot determine how often that pathway initiates the syndrome in people. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9626131/) [JCI](https://www.jci.org/articles/view/202824)

Hyperinsulinemia can increase androgen exposure through at least two routes:

- It can stimulate androgen production by ovarian theca cells. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9626131/)
- It can reduce hepatic production of sex hormone-binding globulin, increasing the biologically available fraction of circulating testosterone. [JCI](https://www.jci.org/articles/view/202824)

Androgen excess can then impair normal follicular development and ovulation, contributing to the accumulation of small arrested follicles that produced the former "polycystic" description. [JCI](https://www.jci.org/articles/view/202824)

This does not mean the ovary is a passive victim of insulin. In a small ex vivo study, isolated theca cells from nine polycystic ovaries showed excessive androgen production compared with cells from five normal ovaries even outside the original metabolic environment, supporting an intrinsic ovarian steroidogenic abnormality in at least some patients. Luteinizing hormone signaling, ovarian enzyme regulation, genetic susceptibility, and altered follicular regulation may also help determine how strongly a given ovary responds to insulin. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7962289/) [JCI](https://www.jci.org/articles/view/202824)

The causal arrow also points in the opposite direction. Androgen excess can worsen insulin sensitivity, alter adipose biology, and contribute to compensatory hyperinsulinemia. These findings support a model in which a person may enter the cycle through different points, including metabolic dysfunction, ovarian androgen excess, neuroendocrine signaling, adrenal androgen production, or some combination, and then develop a similar clinical phenotype. This is a mechanistic synthesis rather than proof of the initiating event in an individual. [JCI](https://www.jci.org/articles/view/202824)

This bidirectionality is why "root cause" is difficult to assign. Once the feedback loop is established, insulin and androgens can both become upstream and downstream of one another. [JCI](https://www.jci.org/articles/view/202824)

## How common is insulin resistance?

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 28 published reports using euglycemic-hyperinsulinemic clamps, a reference-standard method for measuring whole-body insulin sensitivity, estimated that insulin sensitivity was, on average, 27 percent lower in women with PCOS than in controls, independent of body mass index. Higher body mass index was associated with an additional reduction of approximately 15 percent relative to controls. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27907900/)

That is evidence that reduced insulin sensitivity is a major feature of the syndrome and is not simply an indirect consequence of higher body weight. It is not evidence that every patient is insulin resistant: a group-average model estimate cannot be converted into a universal prevalence figure, and the review was limited by small studies, missing data, heterogeneous clamp protocols, and inadequate reporting of individual Rotterdam phenotypes. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27907900/)

A clamp study of 137 White women illustrates the heterogeneity. Using the investigators' insulin-resistance threshold, 71.4 percent of the total sample was classified as insulin resistant. The proportions were 80.4 percent in the classic hyperandrogenic-anovulatory phenotype, 65 percent in the ovulatory hyperandrogenic phenotype, and 38.1 percent in the normoandrogenic phenotype. These figures support insulin resistance as particularly prominent in classic PMOS, but they come from one relatively small, ethnically restricted cohort and should not be treated as universal prevalence estimates. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23476073/)

"Lean PMOS" is therefore not synonymous with "non-insulin-resistant PMOS." Some people with lower body mass index still have substantial insulin resistance, while others have insulin sensitivity within the expected range. Body size modifies risk but does not reliably identify the mechanism in an individual. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27907900/) [JCI](https://www.jci.org/articles/view/202824)

Importantly, insulin resistance is not currently a diagnostic requirement. Adult diagnosis uses two of three features after excluding alternative causes: clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, and polycystic ovarian morphology on ultrasound or, in adults, an appropriately interpreted anti-Müllerian hormone measure. Anti-Müllerian hormone should not be used as a standalone diagnostic test, and population- and assay-specific cutoffs are required. When hyperandrogenism and irregular cycles are already present, neither ultrasound nor anti-Müllerian hormone is required. Thus, despite the former name, abnormal ovarian morphology is not obligatory, and neither is documented insulin resistance. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10505534/)

## Adrenal, lean, and "post-pill" presentations

### Adrenal androgen excess

Functional adrenal hyperandrogenism is observed in an estimated 20 to 30 percent of affected women. It may involve increased adrenal responsiveness to adrenocorticotropic hormone and can be present across much of the life course. [OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/reproduction/article/169/6/e250091/8373275)

This does not create a clean "adrenal versus insulin" division. Adrenal and ovarian androgen excess can coexist, and insulin-sensitizing interventions have sometimes affected adrenal androgen measures. The relationship between adrenal hyperandrogenism and metabolic dysfunction remains incompletely characterized. Current evidence supports it as an important axis of heterogeneity, not as a consistently insulin-independent subtype. [OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/reproduction/article/169/6/e250091/8373275)

Marked or rapidly progressive androgen excess also requires evaluation for conditions other than PMOS, including nonclassic congenital adrenal hyperplasia, Cushing syndrome, adrenal or ovarian tumors, and severe insulin-resistance syndromes. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10505534/)

### Lean phenotypes

Lean phenotypes demonstrate why appearance and body mass index are poor proxies for pathophysiology. Some lean patients have insulin resistance or compensatory hyperinsulinemia; others do not. Some may have predominantly hyperandrogenic or neuroendocrine abnormalities with relatively preserved metabolic measurements. The available evidence supports degrees of insulin involvement rather than two mutually exclusive categories of "insulin PCOS" and "non-insulin PCOS." [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27907900/) [JCI](https://www.jci.org/articles/view/202824)

### "Post-pill PCOS"

"Post-pill PCOS" is not a phenotype defined in the international diagnostic criteria. Combined hormonal contraception changes the measurements used to evaluate the syndrome: it raises sex hormone-binding globulin, reduces gonadotropin-dependent androgen production, and can suppress anti-Müllerian hormone. Reliable biochemical androgen evaluation while taking a combined pill is therefore difficult. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10505534/)

If biochemical androgen assessment is essential, the guideline advises withdrawing the combined pill for at least three months while providing alternative contraception. This recommendation addresses measurement validity; it does not establish a separate "post-pill" etiology or show that hormonal contraception created PMOS. Symptoms appearing after discontinuation should still be evaluated under the standard diagnostic framework, including exclusion of other causes. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10505534/)

## What treatment trials actually show

Treatment response can show that a pathway is clinically modifiable, but by itself it cannot identify the initiating cause. Blood-pressure reduction with a medication, for example, does not establish that the drug's target caused every case of hypertension. The same caution applies to insulin-sensitizing treatment in PMOS.

### Metformin

A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized trials found that metformin produced statistically significant but generally modest average improvements compared with placebo:

- Body mass index decreased by 0.53 kg/m² (mean difference, -0.53; 95 percent CI, -0.95 to -0.12).
- HOMA-IR decreased by 0.50 units (mean difference, -0.50; 95 percent CI, -0.91 to -0.09).
- Fasting glucose decreased by 0.13 mmol/L (mean difference, -0.13; 95 percent CI, -0.19 to -0.07).
- Smaller, lower-certainty improvements were found in waist-to-hip ratio and several lipid measures.

[Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/ejendo/article/189/2/S38/7236786)

Mild gastrointestinal adverse effects were substantially more common with metformin (odds ratio, 7.67; 95 percent CI, 2.74 to 21.46). [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37536294/)

These findings support metformin for selected metabolic outcomes, particularly in patients with higher metabolic risk. They do not make metformin a universal cure. Average effects were modest, several outcomes had low or very low certainty, and the trials estimated treatment effects rather than the temporal origin of PMOS. Improvement after treatment therefore does not establish that insulin resistance initiated the syndrome. [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/ejendo/article/189/2/S38/7236786)

International guidance consequently recommends metformin primarily for anthropometric and metabolic indications, especially in adults with a body mass index of at least 25 kg/m². It may also be considered below that threshold, but the evidence is more limited. The certainty supporting these recommendations is low to very low. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10505534/)

### Combined hormonal contraception

Calling combined oral contraceptives mere symptom suppression is also incomplete. They do not directly correct insulin resistance, but they treat clinically important components of the syndrome: irregular cycles, androgen-related symptoms, and prolonged exposure of the endometrium to unopposed estrogen. Cycle regulation or regular progestogen exposure can help reduce the risk associated with long-standing amenorrhea. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10505534/)

A meta-analysis of 36 randomized trials comparing metformin, combined oral contraceptives, or their combination found no clear difference between metformin and combined pills for clinical hirsutism. Combined pills produced greater improvements in free androgen index, sex hormone-binding globulin, and testosterone, whereas metformin produced greater reductions in insulin and triglycerides. Combination treatment improved some biochemical outcomes, although its additional clinical benefit was not consistent across lower-risk groups. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37554096/)

This is not a contest in which one treatment addresses the "real disease" and the other addresses only appearances. They target different parts of the feedback system:

- **Metformin** is better aligned with metabolic indications. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10505534/)
- **Combined hormonal contraception** is better aligned with cycle control and androgen-related symptoms. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10505534/)
- **Combination therapy** may be reasonable when reproductive and metabolic indications coexist, particularly in higher-risk patients. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37554096/)

Using contraception alone while never assessing glycemia, lipids, blood pressure, sleep apnea risk, or lifestyle would be incomplete care. But withholding effective cycle and androgen treatment because it is "downstream" would also be incomplete care. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10505534/)

### Inositol

Inositol has a plausible connection to insulin signaling, but its clinical evidence remains limited and inconclusive. A systematic review prepared for the international guideline found some signals of benefit in selected metabolic outcomes and fewer gastrointestinal effects than metformin, but concluded that the overall evidence was limited and inconclusive. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38163998/)

The guideline allows inositol to be considered according to patient preferences, while emphasizing limited clinical benefit for ovulation, hirsutism, or weight. For infertility specifically, it remains experimental because benefits and risks are too uncertain to recommend it as a fertility therapy. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10505534/)

It should therefore not be presented as an established insulin-targeting treatment that corrects the root cause of PMOS. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38163998/)

### Diet

The international guideline found no evidence that any single dietary composition is superior for anthropometric, metabolic, hormonal, reproductive, or psychological outcomes in PMOS. This absence of evidence does not establish that all dietary patterns are equivalent; it means that comparative evidence is insufficient. Sustainable healthy eating tailored to the individual is better supported than a claim that one named diet uniquely treats the syndrome's cause. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10505534/)

Lifestyle intervention is recommended for all affected patients, including those who do not need or want weight loss. Benefits can occur through physical activity, dietary quality, fitness, sleep, and prevention of excess weight gain, not only through a lower number on the scale. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10505534/)

### Resistance training and other exercise

A 2026 network meta-analysis synthesized 29 publications representing 19 unique randomized trials and 884 participants. Combined aerobic and resistance exercise ranked highest for reducing fasting insulin, while aerobic exercise ranked highest for body mass index. However, the certainty of evidence was low or very low across outcomes, many estimates relied partly on indirect comparisons, and superiority was not demonstrated for most reproductive or psychological outcomes. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12958-026-01563-8)

Resistance training is therefore a sound component of general health and metabolic management, but current trials do not establish it as a uniquely effective correction of an insulin-defined root cause. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12958-026-01563-8)

## Treatment-response stratification remains a major gap

The field has much better evidence for average treatment effects - metformin on selected metabolic outcomes and combined pills on androgen-related and cycle outcomes - than it has evidence identifying which individual patient will respond best to which treatment. [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/ejendo/article/189/2/S38/7236786) [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37554096/)

The major treatment reviews report group-average effects; they do not validate a method for assigning individual patients to "insulin-driven" or "not insulin-driven" treatment groups. HOMA-IR and fasting insulin are surrogate measures, whereas the euglycemic-hyperinsulinemic clamp is the reference-standard method for measuring insulin sensitivity. The 2023 guideline notes that clinically available insulin assays have limited clinical relevance and should not be used routinely in care. [JCI](https://www.jci.org/articles/view/202824) [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10505534/)

This creates an important paradox: insulin resistance is a major pathophysiological feature, but no standardized routine insulin test cleanly divides patients into "insulin-driven" and "not insulin-driven" treatment groups. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10505534/)

## Did the ovary-centered name distort care?

There is good evidence that many surveyed patients and health professionals considered the old name confusing and too narrow to communicate the syndrome's breadth. In an international online survey and workshop program involving 7,708 respondents, gaps remained in recognition of cardiometabolic and other nonreproductive associations. In the 2023 survey, 85.6 percent of patients and 76.1 percent of health professionals supported changing the name; "endocrine" and "metabolic" were among the most strongly supported terms for a replacement. Because participation was voluntary, these percentages should not be treated as population-representative estimates. [Erasmus University Rotterdam](https://pure.eur.nl/en/publications/polycystic-ovary-syndrome-perspectives-from-patients-and-health-p/)

That supports the argument that terminology matters. "Polycystic ovary syndrome" implied that ovarian cysts were defining, even though the structures are follicles rather than pathological cysts, ovarian morphology is not required in many patients, and the syndrome has important metabolic, endocrine, psychological, dermatological, and reproductive dimensions. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10505534/) [The Lancet](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736%2826%2900717-8/fulltext)

The survey does not test whether the old name itself changed prescribing behavior or metabolic screening. It measures perceptions and knowledge, not causal effects on clinical decisions. Clinical guidelines recommend both metabolic assessment and symptom-directed treatment, so a contraceptive prescription alone does not establish that the name displaced metabolic care. The terminology hypothesis is plausible, but it remains untested in outcome studies. [Erasmus University Rotterdam](https://pure.eur.nl/en/publications/polycystic-ovary-syndrome-perspectives-from-patients-and-health-p/) [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10505534/)

The fourth competing claim, that renaming remained blocked by inertia, insurance coding, and recognition concerns, is now out of date at the consensus level. The global consensus adopted **polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome** in May 2026. Coding, records, education, and clinical language may take time to transition, but these concerns did not ultimately prevent consensus adoption of the new name. [The Lancet](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736%2826%2900717-8/fulltext)

Because the change was adopted in May 2026, evidence about its effects on diagnostic accuracy, metabolic screening, treatment choices, patient understanding, stigma, and insurance continuity will require post-implementation study. The consensus paper presents these as intended benefits and implementation questions, not as outcomes already demonstrated. [The Lancet](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736%2826%2900717-8/fulltext)

## Verdict on the four claims

1. **"Insulin resistance is the primary mechanism for the majority of phenotypes." - Not established.**

    Insulin resistance is common and can be present independently of body mass index. In the Moghetti cohort, it was most prevalent in the classic hyperandrogenic-anovulatory phenotype. Available evidence does not establish that it is temporally or mechanistically primary in most individuals, and it does not provide a universal prevalence figure based on standardized testing. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27907900/) [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23476073/)

2. **"Not all PMOS is insulin-driven." - Consistent with the evidence, but not established as a clean binary.**

    Intrinsic ovarian abnormalities, luteinizing hormone signaling, adrenal androgen production, genetics, and neuroendocrine factors can be important. Lean and adrenal presentations demonstrate heterogeneity, and the international criteria do not define a separate "post-pill" phenotype. Current evidence does not justify assuming that all PMOS is predominantly insulin-driven, but routine testing also cannot reliably divide patients into insulin-driven and non-insulin-driven groups. [JCI](https://www.jci.org/articles/view/202824) [OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/reproduction/article/169/6/e250091/8373275) [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10505534/)

3. **"The ovary framing causes symptom-only treatment." - Plausible but not causally established.**

    The old name was widely perceived by surveyed patients and health professionals as confusing and as underrepresenting metabolic features. The survey did not test whether the name caused pill-first prescribing or inadequate metabolic care. Combined pills are also guideline-supported treatment for cycles and androgen-related symptoms, not merely cosmetic suppression. [Erasmus University Rotterdam](https://pure.eur.nl/en/publications/polycystic-ovary-syndrome-perspectives-from-patients-and-health-p/) [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10505534/)

4. **"Renaming has been blocked." - No longer accurate at the consensus level.**

    PMOS was adopted as the new name through a global consensus process in May 2026. Implementation across coding, records, research, education, and clinical practice remains ongoing, and the remaining question is whether the new name improves care. [The Lancet](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736%2826%2900717-8/fulltext)


## What evidence would settle the question?

### Longitudinal causal studies

Studies beginning before clinical onset, ideally around puberty or in high-risk families, are needed to determine whether insulin resistance, hyperinsulinemia, androgen excess, or altered gonadotropin signaling appears first in different subgroups. Cross-sectional associations cannot establish the initiating event. [JCI](https://www.jci.org/articles/view/202824)

### Mechanistically stratified trials

Trials should stratify participants using more rigorous measures of insulin sensitivity, androgen source, body composition, and reproductive phenotype. They should then test whether those characteristics predict response to metformin, lifestyle intervention, hormonal contraception, anti-androgens, or combination therapy. [JCI](https://www.jci.org/articles/view/202824)

### Head-to-head first-line strategies

Pragmatic randomized trials could compare:

- Metabolic-first treatment.
- Reproductive-symptom-first treatment.
- Integrated treatment from diagnosis.

Outcomes should include ovulation, cycle regularity, androgen symptoms, glycemia, lipids, body composition, endometrial protection, fertility, quality of life, adverse effects, and patient priorities, not only fasting insulin or testosterone.

### Terminology-impact studies

The PMOS transition creates a natural experiment. Studies should measure whether the new name changes metabolic screening rates, referral patterns, diagnostic delay, prescribing, patient comprehension, stigma, research classification, and insurance continuity. [The Lancet](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736%2826%2900717-8/fulltext)

## Practical interpretation

- PMOS is best understood as a **multiorgan feedback syndrome**, not an ovarian disorder with an incidental metabolic association and not a purely metabolic disorder with passive ovaries. [JCI](https://www.jci.org/articles/view/202824)
- Insulin resistance should be taken seriously at every body size, but it should not be inferred solely from weight, symptoms, or a single fasting-insulin result. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27907900/) [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10505534/)
- Glycemic status should be assessed at diagnosis regardless of body mass index. A 75-g oral glucose tolerance test is the most accurate recommended test; fasting glucose and HbA1c are less accurate when the oral glucose tolerance test cannot be performed. Routine insulin assays are not recommended for clinical classification. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10505534/)
- Metformin, combined hormonal contraception, nutrition, exercise, endometrial protection, fertility treatment, and androgen-directed therapy address different clinically important parts of the syndrome. Treatment should follow the patient's risks and priorities rather than a single "root-cause" hierarchy. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10505534/)
- Inositol may be acceptable to some patients after discussing uncertainty, but it should not be represented as equivalent to established treatment or as a proven cure for insulin-driven PMOS. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38163998/)

The most defensible conclusion is therefore: **insulin resistance and associated compensatory hyperinsulinemia are often major sustaining contributors to PMOS, but they are not universal single causes and may not be the initiating processes in some patients.** The ovary is an active participant within a wider endocrine-metabolic network. The new PMOS name is closer to that biology than PCOS was. [JCI](https://www.jci.org/articles/view/202824) [The Lancet](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736%2826%2900717-8/fulltext)
