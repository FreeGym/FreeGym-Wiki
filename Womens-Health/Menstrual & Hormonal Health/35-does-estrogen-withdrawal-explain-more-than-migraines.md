# Does Estrogen Withdrawal Explain More Than Migraines - Mood Crashes, Joint Pain, Brain Fog, Sleep Disruption?

A fall in estrogen after sustained exposure is a real physiological event and, in some settings, a clinically meaningful trigger. Taken together, current domain-specific studies do not demonstrate a single "estrogen withdrawal syndrome" that directly explains migraine, mood symptoms, joint pain, sleep disruption, and brain fog across the menstrual cycle, postpartum period, perimenopause, and hormone-therapy cessation. ([Raffaelli et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1186/s10194-023-01664-4); [Bloch et al., 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10831472/); [Schmidt et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26018333/); [Parsons et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2022.101719); [Naysmith et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44294-026-00132-z); [Kruse et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41523660/); [Ockene et al., 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16014592/))

The better-supported model is **susceptibility to reproductive-steroid change**. Menstrual migraine is the clearest estrogen-withdrawal example. Mood experiments show strong subgroup sensitivity, but often to progesterone as well as estradiol and sometimes to a rise rather than a fall. Sleep is partly hormone-responsive but is also influenced by vasomotor symptoms, mood, pain, or infant care. Reported muscle or joint pain is more prevalent during and after the menopause transition, although withdrawal-specific causality is weak. Subjective brain fog is real, but objective cognitive changes are smaller and do not track a simple estrogen-deficiency model. ([Raffaelli et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1186/s10194-023-01664-4); [Wei et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41030005/); [Haufe and Leeners, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1210/jendso/bvad036); [Parsons et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2022.101719); [Kravitz et al., 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2491500/); [Kruse et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41523660/); [Bangle et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41066270/))

## The four windows are not the same endocrine event

In this article, "withdrawal" refers to a fall after prior exposure. It is distinguished from a low steady level, hormone variability, and anovulation. ([Raffaelli et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1186/s10194-023-01664-4); [Schmidt et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28427285/); [Tepper et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22659249/))

|Window|What changes|What that means for the withdrawal claim|
|---|---|---|
|Late luteal phase|Estradiol and progesterone fall after an ovulatory cycle.|Estrogen can be one trigger, especially for migraine, but progesterone and its neuroactive metabolites change at the same time. ([Raffaelli et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1186/s10194-023-01664-4); [Haufe and Leeners, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1210/jendso/bvad036))|
|After delivery|Estradiol and progesterone fall after delivery, as does the progesterone-derived neurosteroid allopregnanolone. ([Bloch et al., 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10831472/); [Grötsch and Ehlert, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psyneuen.2024.107081))|A key human experiment withdrew estradiol and progesterone together, so it cannot identify estrogen as the sole trigger. ([Bloch et al., 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10831472/))|
|Perimenopause|Ovarian hormones become erratic; anovulatory cycles become more common and lack a normal luteal progesterone rise.|This is not a uniform series of sharp estrogen drops. SWAN identified several distinct estradiol trajectories, including flat and initially rising patterns. In Santoro et al., baseline estradiol showed a U-shaped association with anovulatory 50-day collections that did not end in bleeding, rather than a single low-estrogen pattern. ([Tepper et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22659249/); [Santoro et al., 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18285413/))|
|Stopping hormone therapy|The removed exposure depends on the product and schedule; combined therapy withdraws a progestogen as well as estrogen.|Symptom recurrence can reflect loss of treatment effect, a pharmacologic withdrawal response, or both. Combined-regimen studies cannot isolate estrogen. ([Ockene et al., 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16014592/))|

This distinction matters because an acute rate of change can have different effects from sustained low exposure. A treatment response to estrogen also does not, by itself, prove that estrogen withdrawal caused the condition. ([Raffaelli et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1186/s10194-023-01664-4); [Schmidt et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28427285/))

## Migraine: the strongest withdrawal model, but not a sufficient cause

The estrogen-withdrawal hypothesis began with very small, nonrandomized and unblinded experiments. In the foundational 1972 study, estradiol administration delayed predictable menstrual migraine in six women until estradiol later fell; only three had a no-intervention comparison cycle. A 2023 critical review found the direct evidence sparse and methodologically inconsistent and concluded that further validation is needed. Later hormone-kinetic work also cautions against treating a fixed estradiol threshold or the fall alone as a universal trigger. The review had German Research Foundation and Lundbeck Foundation support, and several authors reported fees from migraine-drug companies outside the submitted work. ([Somerville, 1972](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5062827/); [Raffaelli et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1186/s10194-023-01664-4); [Pavlovic et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27251885/))

Newer hormone-kinetic data are consistent with a vulnerability-plus-trigger model. In SWAN, 114 women who reported a clinician diagnosis of migraine had a faster post-peak urinary estrogen decline than 223 controls. However, the decline did not distinguish women with migraine history who developed a moderate or severe headache in that diary cycle from those who did not. The hormonal fall appeared to mark susceptibility, not determine the attack by itself. The work was publicly funded; disclosures included Allergan honoraria, Bayer research support, MenoGeniX options, and other pharmaceutical consulting. ([Pavlovic et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27251885/))

A double-blind stabilization trial illustrates both the strength and the limit of the hypothesis. In a crossover study with paired data from 35 women, perimenstrual estradiol gel reduced migraine days by 22 percent while it was used (RR 0.78, 95 percent CI 0.62-0.99). During the five days after the gel stopped, migraine increased by 40 percent (RR 1.40, 95 percent CI 1.03-1.92). Benefit during dosing was followed by increased migraine after dosing stopped. ([MacGregor et al., 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17190936/))

Menstrual migraine therefore supplies the best evidence that estrogen withdrawal can trigger a symptom. Even here, it is one component in a susceptible nervous system rather than a complete explanation. ([Raffaelli et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1186/s10194-023-01664-4); [Pavlovic et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27251885/); [MacGregor et al., 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17190936/))

## Mood: strong evidence for hormone-change sensitivity, not an estrogen-only syndrome

### PMS and PMDD

Ovarian-suppression experiments show that severe premenstrual symptoms can be hormone-dependent in a susceptible subgroup, but they argue against estrogen withdrawal as the sole mechanism. In a classic NIMH experiment, ovarian suppression eliminated symptoms in 10 leuprolide-responsive women with prospectively confirmed severe PMS; blinded add-back of either estradiol or progesterone provoked recurrence, while the same manipulations did not produce symptoms in healthy controls. ([Schmidt et al., 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9435325/))

A later experiment in 12 leuprolide-responsive women with PMDD found that symptoms rose during the first month of combined estradiol-progesterone add-back, when levels changed from low to high, but not during the next two months at stable levels. That was a rise, not a withdrawal. ([Schmidt et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28427285/))

An expanded 2025 replication and extension included 34 women with PMDD and 76 controls, of whom 10 and 15, respectively, came from the original cohort. Both estradiol and progesterone add-back increased affective symptoms in women with PMDD but not controls; irritability and mood swings were more closely associated with progesterone. One senior author reported research funding from the Baszucki Foundation and Sage Therapeutics, consulting for GH Research-Ireland, and service on Sage and Sensorium scientific advisory boards, with stock options in both companies. The disclosure does not invalidate the result, which extends earlier NIMH work that also implicated both hormones. ([Wei et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41030005/))

### Postpartum depression

A key experimental study is striking but tiny. Eight euthymic women with previous postpartum depression and eight controls underwent ovarian suppression, pregnancy-level estradiol plus progesterone exposure, and then withdrawal of both hormones. Five of eight women with the prior disorder, versus none of eight controls, met the significant-symptom criterion during withdrawal; group ratings had begun increasing near the end of hormone exposure. This supports reproductive-steroid-change sensitivity in a vulnerable subgroup, not an estrogen-specific cause of postpartum depression. ([Bloch et al., 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10831472/))

### Perimenopausal depression and hormone-therapy withdrawal

Here the evidence becomes more estrogen-specific, but only in a deliberately enriched subgroup. In a randomized trial of 26 women with previous hormone-responsive perimenopausal depression and 30 matched controls, switching from transdermal estradiol to placebo increased depression-rating scores in the previous-depression group. Women who continued estradiol and controls assigned to either condition remained asymptomatic; hot-flush severity did not explain the mood change. Mean scores after withdrawal were still generally below syndromal major-depression ranges, so this was symptom recurrence in a selected group, not evidence that withdrawal depresses most women. ([Schmidt et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26018333/))

That specificity is reinforced by the opposite experiment: among 72 psychiatrically healthy women given leuprolide to induce hypogonadism, four reached at least 10 on the Beck Depression Inventory, one reached 18, and only one remained at or above 10 at the next biweekly visit, although hot flashes and sleep disruption increased. Low estradiol was not sufficient to produce depression in most healthy participants. ([Ben Dor et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23545794/))

The mood evidence therefore supports a real, sometimes causal sensitivity to reproductive-steroid transitions. It does not support a universal estrogen-withdrawal response, and PMDD and postpartum findings do not identify estrogen as the sole relevant steroid or pathway. ([Schmidt et al., 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9435325/); [Schmidt et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28427285/); [Wei et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41030005/); [Bloch et al., 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10831472/); [Schmidt et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26018333/))

## Sleep disruption: partly hormonal, often indirect

Late-luteal sleep can worsen, but a 2023 synthesis found no clear association between endogenous estrogen and sleep continuity across the menstrual cycle. Declining progesterone was more consistently associated with wake after sleep onset, arousals, and worse subjective sleep, although the underlying studies were small and heterogeneous. ([Haufe and Leeners, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1210/jendso/bvad036))

Postpartum sleep is a particularly weak test of an estrogen-only model. A meta-analysis of 16 actigraphy studies found lower total sleep time and sleep efficiency and more wake after sleep onset after birth, with the largest disruption in the first four weeks. Total sleep time partially recovered through weeks 8 to 16, sleep efficiency was no longer clearly different during that interval, and recovery in wake after sleep onset was not tested by postpartum interval. The analysis measured the transition to parenthood, not hormone withdrawal, and therefore could not isolate hormonal effects from the other changes that accompany early parenthood. ([Parsons et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2022.101719))

During perimenopause, hormones contribute, but downstream symptoms appear important. In 3,045 SWAN participants followed for up to seven years, annual estradiol decline had small independent associations with difficulty falling and staying asleep. Frequent vasomotor symptoms had much larger associations with all three measured sleep complaints. Because estradiol was sampled annually, the study could not test rapid withdrawal kinetics. ([Kravitz et al., 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2491500/))

Treatment effects are correspondingly modest. A meta-analysis of 15 randomized trials found a small improvement in self-reported sleep with menopausal hormone therapy (SMD -0.13, 95 percent CI -0.18 to -0.08) but no significant improvement on polysomnography. In an indirect, across-trial subgroup analysis, combined estrogen-progestogen regimens showed a statistically significant benefit while estrogen alone did not. Because the formulations and studies differed, this does not establish that adding a progestogen caused the benefit or cleanly isolate an estrogen-only mechanism. ([Pan et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35102100/))

Stopping hormone therapy can worsen sleep in the short term. In a randomized suspension study, 1,704 women were assigned and 1,405 were included in the analysis. One or two months off therapy produced more sleep problems than continuation; the two-month group averaged 0.70 more symptomatic days per week on the study's eight-item sleep index (95 percent CI 0.50-0.91). The trial could not distinguish withdrawal from return of symptoms previously controlled by therapy. ([Tom et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21785373/))

Sleep disruption is thus compatible with a shared hormonal trigger, but the plausible contributors differ by window: late-luteal progesterone change, the broader transition to early parenthood, and perimenopausal vasomotor symptoms all compete with a direct estrogen effect. ([Haufe and Leeners, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1210/jendso/bvad036); [Parsons et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2022.101719); [Kravitz et al., 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2491500/))

## Brain fog: the symptom signal is stronger than the estrogen-kinetic signal

The broad menstrual-cycle literature does not show a general late-luteal cognitive deficit. A 2025 meta-analysis of 102 articles, 3,943 participants, and 730 phase comparisons found no robust phase-related changes across attention, memory, executive function, or other objective domains after accounting for phase-verification quality and multiple testing. ([Jang et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0318576))

Perimenopausal cognitive complaints have stronger support, but not as a direct withdrawal effect. An updated meta-analysis of 26 studies and 9,428 participants found poorer pooled cognitive outcomes, combining subjective and objective indices, in perimenopause than premenopause (Hedges' g -0.51), but heterogeneity was extreme (I2 96.1 percent). The overall result depended on standardized menopause staging; no individual objective domain showed a significant peri-versus-pre difference, and the self-reported memory signal was larger than the objective-memory signal. ([Bangle et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41066270/))

A 2026 cross-sectional study of 14,234 women makes the dissociation clearer. Perimenopausal participants had higher odds of reporting brain fog than premenopausal participants (OR 1.31), yet objective global performance was not worse. Subjective cognitive symptoms correlated weakly with test performance and more strongly with psychological symptoms. The study did not measure hormones, so it cannot link the complaints to estrogen kinetics. ([Naysmith et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44294-026-00132-z))

Finally, a 2024 meta-analysis of 34 randomized trials found no overall cognitive-domain benefit from menopausal hormone therapy. Formulation and surgical-menopause subgroup signals were heterogeneous and exploratory. These trials did not directly test rapid estrogen-withdrawal kinetics or a brain-fog-defined population, but their neutral overall results do not support menopausal hormone therapy as a general cognitive treatment. ([Andy et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38501109/))

Neutral objective tests do not make subjective cognitive complaints clinically irrelevant. The evidence does show that "brain fog" is not a precise proxy for estrogen withdrawal or global cognitive impairment. ([Naysmith et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44294-026-00132-z))

## Joint pain: a reproductive-stage association, a weak withdrawal-specific mechanism

The newest stage-comparison evidence documents a higher reported symptom burden. A 2026 meta-analysis of 37 observational studies and 93,021 women estimated muscle or joint pain in 40 percent before menopause, 57 percent during perimenopause, and 59 percent after menopause. Risk was higher in perimenopause than premenopause (RR 1.35, 95 percent CI 1.25-1.46), but not meaningfully different between perimenopause and postmenopause (RR 0.97, 95 percent CI 0.92-1.01). Heterogeneity was very high, 35 of the 37 studies were cross-sectional, most outcomes were self-reported, and specific musculoskeletal diagnoses were rarely established. The review reported no study funding; one author disclosed pharmaceutical honoraria and advisory roles and a Pfizer grant. ([Kruse et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41523660/))

Randomized evidence is mixed. In a post hoc analysis of 10,739 women in the WHI estrogen-alone trial, joint pain at one year was reported by 76.3 percent assigned oral conjugated equine estrogen and 79.2 percent assigned placebo, a 2.9 percentage-point difference. Reported joint swelling was higher with estrogen, at 42.1 percent versus 39.7 percent. These were secondary, self-reported outcomes from one regimen in hysterectomized postmenopausal women, not a joint-disease trial. The WHI program was publicly funded, and Wyeth-Ayerst supplied the study drugs. Chlebowski reported pharmaceutical consulting, research support, and speaker relationships; Woods reported a Procter & Gamble board role. ([Chlebowski et al., 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3855295/); [LaCroix et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21467283/))

A broader 2026 systematic review of 57 reports, including 13 reports classified as randomized trials and nearly four million participants overall, found no significant reduction in generalized musculoskeletal pain in its randomized-trial sensitivity analysis (RR 0.98, 95 percent CI 0.95-1.02; I2 51 percent). Osteoarthritis and rheumatoid-arthritis findings were too heterogeneous for firm conclusions. The review reported no funding and no conflicts of interest. ([Overton et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/20533691251403087))

Pain or stiffness was reported more often after treatment stopped. In surveys mailed 8 to 12 months after abrupt discontinuation in the WHI combined estrogen-progestin trial, former active-treatment participants had more moderate or severe pain or stiffness than former placebo participants (adjusted OR 2.16, 95 percent CI 1.95-2.40). However, the delayed survey did not establish within-person symptom recurrence or capture the acute hormone fall, the symptom bundle mixed joint pain with other aches and stiffness, and stopping combined therapy cannot isolate estrogen. The result is consistent with loss of symptom suppression, withdrawal, or both. ([Ockene et al., 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16014592/))

Joint pain is therefore associated with the menopause transition and may be modestly hormone-responsive in some women. Current evidence does not establish acute estrogen withdrawal as its common cause. ([Kruse et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41523660/); [Chlebowski et al., 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3855295/); [Overton et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/20533691251403087); [Ockene et al., 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16014592/))

## Does smoothing the drop improve the whole cluster?

The trials summarized below are outcome-specific and do not test estrogen stabilization against a shared migraine-mood-joint-sleep-cognition outcome. Their results also do not show a consistent pattern across domains. ([MacGregor et al., 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17190936/); [Eisenlohr-Moul et al., 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5629109/); [Dennis et al., 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18843619/); [Gregoire et al., 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8598756/); [Wisner et al., 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4485597/); [Gordon et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29322164/); [Pan et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35102100/); [Andy et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38501109/); [Overton et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/20533691251403087); [Haimov-Kochman et al., 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16735933/))

- **Menstrual migraine:** Estradiol gel reduced attacks while in use, but migraine increased during the five days after discontinuation. ([MacGregor et al., 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17190936/))

- **Premenstrual dysphoria:** In an NIH-funded three-arm trial, 75 women were randomized and 55 contributed treatment data. Continuous drospirenone/ethinyl-estradiol combined oral contraception was not superior to intermittent dosing or placebo, and all groups improved substantially. Only 20 of the 55 met symptom criteria consistent with DSM-5 PMDD, and the study could exclude only large between-group effects. The trial does not establish equivalence or rule out smaller or regimen-specific benefits. ([Eisenlohr-Moul et al., 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5629109/))

- **Postpartum depression:** A Cochrane review found only two eligible hormone trials involving 229 women and concluded that estrogen might have modest value for severe depression, while prevention of recurrence had not been rigorously tested. The methodologically limited 61-woman estradiol trial reported greater improvement with estradiol and was sponsored by Ciba Pharmaceuticals. A later 85-woman estradiol-sertraline-placebo pilot found no significant estradiol advantage and failed to achieve the intended estradiol exposure. Novartis initially supplied patches for the later trial; investigators subsequently purchased generic patches. These are treatment trials, not proof that postpartum estrogen withdrawal caused the episodes. ([Dennis et al., 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18843619/); [Gregoire et al., 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8598756/); [Wisner et al., 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4485597/))

- **Perimenopausal symptoms:** An NIH-funded trial of 172 euthymic women found that transdermal estradiol plus intermittent micronized progesterone reduced development of clinically significant depressive symptoms over 12 months from 32.3 percent to 17.3 percent when compared with placebo patches plus placebo pills. Because active progesterone was given only with active estradiol, the randomized contrast tested the combined regimen against double placebo and cannot isolate estradiol's contribution. The trial reported no conflicts of interest; placebo patches were developed by 3M Pharmaceuticals. This was a mood-specific endpoint, not a test of the full cluster. A 2026 meta-analysis of 12 randomized trials found only a small placebo-controlled effect of menopausal hormone therapy on depressive symptoms (SMD -0.23, 95 percent CI -0.43 to -0.03), with limited certainty. Sleep effects are also small, cognition is neutral overall, and generalized musculoskeletal-pain evidence is neutral. This is selective symptom benefit, not evidence for one corrected withdrawal mechanism. ([Gordon et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29322164/); [Li et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42061517/); [Pan et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35102100/); [Andy et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38501109/); [Overton et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/20533691251403087))

- **Stopping hormone therapy:** In a randomized, unblinded study of 91 long-term users, tapering reduced early vasomotor-symptom severity but delayed rather than prevented recurrence; by 9 to 12 months the groups did not differ in vasomotor symptoms, mood deterioration, or sexual dysfunction. This trial does not demonstrate that "bridging" prevents a broad withdrawal cluster. ([Haimov-Kochman et al., 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16735933/))


Lifestyle stabilization is conceptually different from hormone smoothing: it does not directly test or demonstrate stabilization of the estradiol trajectory. It therefore should not be described as validation of an estrogen-withdrawal syndrome.

## Why some women react and others do not

Between-woman variability is not evidence that the symptoms are imaginary. In the experiments summarized below, it is consistent with hormone change interacting with vulnerability rather than acting as a universal trigger. ([Schmidt et al., 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9435325/); [Bloch et al., 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10831472/); [Schmidt et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26018333/); [Ben Dor et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23545794/))

The experimental pattern repeats across disorders: ovarian steroids provoked symptoms in severe PMS or PMDD but not controls; simulated pregnancy and withdrawal destabilized women with prior postpartum depression but not controls; estradiol withdrawal affected women with prior hormone-responsive perimenopausal depression but not matched controls; and induced hypogonadism rarely produced depressive symptoms in psychiatrically healthy women. ([Schmidt et al., 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9435325/); [Wei et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41030005/); [Bloch et al., 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10831472/); [Schmidt et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26018333/); [Ben Dor et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23545794/))

Previous reproductive mood disorder or migraine history has been used to define or enrich susceptible groups in observational and experimental studies. The cited studies were not designed to validate a predictor of who will develop the entire proposed symptom cluster across all four windows. ([Pavlovic et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27251885/); [Bloch et al., 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10831472/); [Schmidt et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26018333/)) Calling every transition an "estrogen withdrawal syndrome" would overattribute common symptoms to one hormone; denying hormone sensitivity because most women adapt would make the opposite error.

## Limits and open debates

- The studies in this evidence base used different populations, designs, and outcome measures, so they do not directly compare the same symptom cluster across PMS or PMDD, postpartum, perimenopause, and hormone-therapy cessation. ([Schmidt et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28427285/); [Bloch et al., 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10831472/); [Raffaelli et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1186/s10194-023-01664-4); [Kravitz et al., 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2491500/); [Kruse et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41523660/); [Ockene et al., 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16014592/))

- Many studies classify women by reproductive stage or take annual hormone samples. Those designs cannot test whether day-to-day estradiol slope predicts within-person symptom onset. ([Tepper et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22659249/); [Kravitz et al., 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2491500/))

- The strongest causal mood experiments are small and deliberately enriched for prior hormone-responsive illness. They establish susceptibility in those groups, not population prevalence. ([Bloch et al., 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10831472/); [Schmidt et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26018333/))

- Estradiol and progesterone often change together, and combined hormonal treatments add or remove both. The cited studies separately identify progesterone-related pathways, vasomotor symptoms, psychological symptoms, sleep, pain, and the transition to parenthood as possible contributors or alternative explanations, but no single study evaluates the entire cluster. ([Wei et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41030005/); [Haufe and Leeners, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1210/jendso/bvad036); [Parsons et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2022.101719); [Naysmith et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44294-026-00132-z); [Kruse et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41523660/))

- Treatment trials evaluate whether an intervention changes an outcome; they do not, by themselves, establish that estrogen withdrawal was the outcome's original cause. ([Schmidt et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26018333/); [Pan et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35102100/); [Overton et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/20533691251403087))


The most decisive next study would use the same protocol across reproductive windows: frequent estradiol, progesterone, and neurosteroid measurement; daily symptom ratings; objective sleep and cognitive measures; clinically characterized pain outcomes; and within-person analysis of hormone level, direction, and rate of change. Stabilization trials would then need prespecified outcomes across multiple symptom domains, while testing prior reproductive disorders and other vulnerability markers as effect modifiers.

## Practical interpretation

- **Use "estrogen withdrawal" narrowly.** It is most defensible for menstrual migraine and for mood recurrence in a small, selected group with prior hormone-responsive perimenopausal depression. ([MacGregor et al., 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17190936/); [Schmidt et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26018333/))

- **Use "reproductive-steroid-change sensitivity" for the broader hypothesis.** It accommodates estradiol, progesterone, neurosteroids, direction and speed of change, and individual susceptibility without claiming that all symptoms share one downstream pathway. ([Schmidt et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28427285/); [Wei et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41030005/))

- **Do not infer a common cause from co-timing alone.** The cited studies separately report sleep, psychological, cognitive, and musculoskeletal findings around the menopause transition, but they do not establish that these symptoms mutually amplify one another or share one proximate mechanism. ([Kravitz et al., 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2491500/); [Naysmith et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44294-026-00132-z); [Kruse et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41523660/))

- **Do not assume that smoothing will treat every domain.** The randomized evidence ranges from temporary benefit with rebound in menstrual migraine, to small subjective sleep and mood effects, to neutral cognitive and generalized musculoskeletal outcomes. ([MacGregor et al., 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17190936/); [Li et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42061517/); [Pan et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35102100/); [Andy et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38501109/); [Overton et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/20533691251403087))

- **Avoid both overmedicalization and dismissal.** A normal physiological transition can trigger clinically important symptoms in a susceptible person. The evidence favors identifying the affected domain, timing, prior history, and competing causes rather than assigning an umbrella syndrome. ([Pavlovic et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27251885/); [Bloch et al., 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10831472/); [Schmidt et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26018333/))


The unifying idea survives in a narrower form: reproductive-hormone change can be a shared trigger. The stronger claim, that estrogen withdrawal is the common mechanism behind migraine, mood crashes, joint pain, brain fog, and sleep disruption across all four states, is not demonstrated by current domain-specific evidence. ([Raffaelli et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1186/s10194-023-01664-4); [Wei et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41030005/); [Haufe and Leeners, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1210/jendso/bvad036); [Naysmith et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44294-026-00132-z); [Kruse et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41523660/))
