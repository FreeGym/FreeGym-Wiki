# Does PCOS Look Different in Lean vs. Overweight Women, and Should Treatment Differ?

PCOS does look different, on average, in lean and higher-weight women. Higher adiposity generally amplifies insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, blood-pressure abnormalities, and diabetes risk. But normal BMI does not exclude PCOS, dysglycemia, anovulation, infertility, or longer-term cardiometabolic risk. ([Zheng et al., 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/endocrinology/articles/10.3389/fendo.2025.1680685/full); [Zhu et al., 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30611404/); [Ollila et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28031324/); [Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786))

The evidence supports **BMI-aware, phenotype-informed, goal-directed care**. It does not yet support treating lean and higher-weight PCOS as separate diseases, nor does it support four fully validated treatment protocols corresponding to Rotterdam phenotypes A through D. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786); [Carmina and Lobo, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36292002/); [Dokras et al., 2025](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pmed.1004662))

A terminology note: in 2026, a global consensus process adopted **polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS)** as the new name for PCOS, with a transition period for clinical and research systems. Because nearly all of the evidence reviewed below, and the clinical question, uses PCOS, that term is retained here. ([Teede et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42119588/))

## Lean PCOS and Rotterdam phenotype are different classifications

In adults, under the Rotterdam framework, PCOS is diagnosed after excluding relevant alternative disorders when at least two of three features are present: ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786))

- Hyperandrogenism, identified clinically or biochemically
- Ovulatory dysfunction
- Polycystic ovarian morphology, with anti-Müllerian hormone now usable as an alternative to ultrasound in appropriately selected adults

Anti-Müllerian hormone should be used only within the adult diagnostic algorithm, not as a stand-alone test, and either anti-Müllerian hormone or ultrasound should be used to define polycystic ovarian morphology rather than both. In adolescents, both hyperandrogenism and ovulatory dysfunction are required, and neither ultrasound nor anti-Müllerian hormone is recommended for diagnosis because of poor specificity. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786))

These criteria create four commonly used adult phenotypes: ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786); [Daan et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25239303/))

- **Phenotype A:** hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, and polycystic ovarian morphology
- **Phenotype B:** hyperandrogenism and ovulatory dysfunction
- **Phenotype C:** hyperandrogenism and polycystic ovarian morphology, with ovulation preserved
- **Phenotype D:** ovulatory dysfunction and polycystic ovarian morphology, without hyperandrogenism

BMI is not part of this classification. A lean woman can have phenotype A, B, C, or D, and the same is true of a woman with overweight or obesity. “Lean PCOS” is therefore a body-size descriptor, not a fifth PCOS phenotype. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786); [Daan et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25239303/); [Carmina and Lobo, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36292002/))

This distinction matters because BMI and androgen phenotype may each contribute information. Collapsing everyone into “lean versus obese” ignores reproductive and androgen differences; collapsing everyone into A through D ignores the substantial metabolic effect of adiposity. ([Zheng et al., 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/endocrinology/articles/10.3389/fendo.2025.1680685/full); [Daan et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25239303/); [Carmina and Lobo, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36292002/))

## What differs between lean and higher-weight PCOS

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis pooled 73 studies comparing lower-BMI and higher-BMI women with PCOS. The review defined the lower-BMI group as BMI no greater than 25 kg/m², or no greater than 23 kg/m² in Asian populations, and the higher-BMI group as BMI of at least 25 kg/m², or at least 23 kg/m² in Asian populations. The comparison was therefore not uniformly lean versus obese. Lower-BMI participants had lower systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, LDL cholesterol, triglycerides, and HOMA-IR. The standardized mean differences were -0.58 for systolic pressure, -0.56 for diastolic pressure, -0.49 for LDL cholesterol, -0.72 for triglycerides, and -0.88 for HOMA-IR, with the negative direction favoring the lower-BMI group. These unitless pooled differences describe surrogate markers, not absolute clinical differences or cardiovascular-event rates. Diagnostic criteria, BMI cutoffs, assays, and study populations varied, and heterogeneity was substantial for several outcomes. ([Zheng et al., 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/endocrinology/articles/10.3389/fendo.2025.1680685/full))

Some endocrine differences ran in the other direction. Lower-BMI women had a modestly higher LH-to-FSH ratio, while anti-Müllerian hormone did not differ significantly between groups. These are pooled biomarker differences; they do not demonstrate that lean PCOS is more reproductively severe or predict how an individual woman will respond to treatment. Some reported p-values appear inconsistent with their confidence intervals, so confidence intervals and forest plots are more informative than those isolated p-values. ([Zheng et al., 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/endocrinology/articles/10.3389/fendo.2025.1680685/full))

The comparison changes when lean or nonobese women with PCOS are compared with nonobese women who do not have PCOS. A 2019 meta-analysis found higher prevalence odds of insulin resistance in nonobese PCOS versus nonobese controls, with an odds ratio of 5.70, although the confidence interval was wide at 1.46 to 22.32. The corresponding prevalence odds ratios were 3.42 for impaired glucose tolerance, 1.47 for type 2 diabetes, and 2.57 for metabolic syndrome. Several estimates were imprecise, study definitions varied, and the review found no studies reporting incident myocardial infarction, stroke, or coronary disease specifically in nonobese PCOS. ([Zhu et al., 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30611404/))

The two findings are not contradictory:

- Lean PCOS is generally metabolically more favorable than PCOS accompanied by obesity. ([Zheng et al., 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/endocrinology/articles/10.3389/fendo.2025.1680685/full))
- Lean or nonobese PCOS may still carry greater metabolic risk than nonobese women without PCOS. ([Zhu et al., 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30611404/))
- A favorable BMI lowers average risk but does not establish normal glucose regulation in an individual patient. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786); [Stovall et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21194310/))

“Lean” and “nonobese” should also not be treated as interchangeable research categories. Some studies define lean as BMI below 25 kg/m², while “nonobese” cohorts can include women with overweight. Ethnicity-specific BMI and waist thresholds further complicate comparisons. ([Zheng et al., 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/endocrinology/articles/10.3389/fendo.2025.1680685/full); [Zhu et al., 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30611404/))

## Do Rotterdam phenotypes have different metabolic profiles?

Cross-sectional studies generally find that hyperandrogenic phenotypes have less favorable metabolic profiles than non-hyperandrogenic phenotype D, but the differences are not entirely consistent after body size is taken into account. ([Daan et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25239303/); [Carmina and Lobo, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36292002/))

In a multicenter study of 2,288 women, the 1,219 women classified as having hyperandrogenic PCOS had more obesity or overweight, insulin resistance, and metabolic syndrome than women with non-hyperandrogenic PCOS. Nevertheless, metabolic abnormalities were also present in the non-hyperandrogenic group, including overweight or obesity in 28.5% and elevated LDL cholesterol in 52.2%. ([Daan et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25239303/))

In a smaller study explicitly comparing lean and obese women within Rotterdam phenotypes, body weight appeared more influential than phenotype for several insulin and lipid measures. That study included only a small number of phenotype-D participants, however, so it cannot establish that phenotype is metabolically irrelevant. ([Carmina and Lobo, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36292002/))

The most informative recent longitudinal evidence comes from a 2026 Swedish multiregister study of 50,969 women with PCOS and 246,246 matched controls followed for up to 20 years. After adjustment for an obesity diagnosis, PCOS was associated with hypertension at an adjusted hazard ratio of 2.25 and dyslipidemia at 3.05. The corresponding estimates were substantially higher in register-classified hyperandrogenic PCOS: 5.51 for hypertension and 7.82 for dyslipidemia. Normoandrogenic PCOS was still associated with both outcomes, at 1.94 and 2.62, respectively. ([Persson et al., 2026](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/41/7/1197/8676742))

That study is important but does not settle the A-through-D question. The register-defined PCOS group included diagnoses of PCOS, androgen excess, or anovulatory infertility, and hyperandrogenic status was inferred from diagnosis and medication records rather than complete prospective Rotterdam phenotyping. Hyperandrogenism was probably under-recorded, BMI was available only for a parous subset, and the population was predominantly Nordic. Adjustment for available BMI or obesity data reduces, but cannot eliminate, residual confounding by adiposity. The results support androgen status as a potential risk marker, but not yet a four-protocol treatment system. ([Persson et al., 2026](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/41/7/1197/8676742))

Longitudinal findings by body size also remain mixed. A 2017 population-based cohort reported increased type 2 diabetes risk in women with overweight or obesity but not in its normal-weight PCOS group. More recent analyses have raised concern about cardiovascular risk in some younger, “nonobese” women, but definitions such as BMI below 28 kg/m² do not necessarily describe lean women, and these analyses have generally lacked A-through-D phenotyping. In the 2025 UK Biobank analysis, PCOS was based partly on self-report and the “young nonobese” findings came from subgroup analyses, so they should be treated as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive lean-PCOS evidence. ([Ollila et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28031324/); [Zhao et al., 2025](https://academic.oup.com/eurjpc/article/32/10/811/7905166))

## Can normal fasting glucose hide metabolic risk?

Yes. Fasting glucose and the two-hour glucose response measure different parts of glucose regulation. A person can have normal fasting glucose but abnormal post-load glucose after a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786); [Stovall et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21194310/))

The 2023 international guideline strongly recommends the **75-g oral glucose tolerance test as the most accurate test for assessing glycemic status in PCOS, regardless of BMI**, although the supporting evidence was graded as very low certainty. When an OGTT cannot be performed, fasting glucose or HbA1c can be used, but with recognition that they have lower diagnostic accuracy in this population. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786))

The lean-specific yield is less firmly established than the broad recommendation might imply. In a small 2011 study of 78 women, only 1 of 31 lean participants, or 3%, had impaired glucose tolerance on OGTT, and none had an abnormal fasting glucose. No lean participant met the study’s fasting-insulin definition of insulin resistance, while estimates varied considerably when different HOMA cutoffs were applied. The study was small, clinic-based, used older diagnostic criteria, and was not sufficient to define a universal testing strategy. ([Stovall et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21194310/))

The later nonobese-PCOS meta-analysis found significantly greater prevalence odds of impaired glucose tolerance than in nonobese controls, but the underlying studies differed in PCOS definitions, BMI thresholds, ethnicity, assays, and ascertainment. Taken together, these data do not establish a precise lean-specific number of additional cases detected by universal OGTT. ([Stovall et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21194310/); [Zhu et al., 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30611404/))

## Fasting insulin is not the recommended solution

The claim that normal-weight women require either fasting insulin **or** OGTT combines two fundamentally different tests. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786))

An OGTT is used to identify dysglycemia, including impaired glucose tolerance or diabetes. Fasting insulin and HOMA-IR are surrogate estimates of insulin resistance rather than diagnostic tests for dysglycemia. Their interpretation varies with assay performance, population characteristics, and the cutoff selected. A result may be useful in research without having a validated threshold that changes clinical management. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786); [Stovall et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21194310/))

The international guideline explicitly states that currently available insulin assays have limited clinical relevance and should not be used routinely in PCOS care. Thus, the evidence-based correction to the second claim is: ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786))

- **OGTT:** supported for glycemic assessment, including in women with normal BMI.
- **Routine fasting insulin or HOMA-IR:** not recommended as a standard diagnostic or screening requirement.
- **Normal fasting glucose:** does not rule out impaired glucose tolerance. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786))

This does not mean insulin resistance is unimportant. It means there is no sufficiently standardized clinical fasting-insulin threshold for diagnosing a hidden “insulin-resistant PCOS phenotype” or automatically deciding who should receive metformin. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786); [Stovall et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21194310/))

## Are lean women systematically diagnosed later?

Diagnostic delay is well documented in PCOS overall. In an international online survey of 1,385 women from 32 countries with self-reported clinician-diagnosed PCOS, 33.6% reported waiting longer than two years for a diagnosis, and 47.1% reported seeing at least three health professionals before receiving it. Only 15.6% were satisfied with the information they received at diagnosis. Participants were recruited through PCOS support-group websites, and the study relied on retrospective self-report, so these figures should not be interpreted as population-prevalence estimates. ([Gibson-Helm et al., 2017](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/102/2/604/2972079))

That survey supports the broader claim that PCOS is frequently diagnosed slowly and communicated poorly. It does **not** establish that lean women routinely go undiagnosed for a decade. It also did not compare diagnostic delay by BMI or Rotterdam phenotype. The survey therefore cannot determine whether diagnostic delay differs by BMI or phenotype; dedicated prospective comparisons would be needed. ([Gibson-Helm et al., 2017](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/102/2/604/2972079))

An obesity-centered mental model is one plausible explanation for missed diagnoses in normal-weight women, but the survey did not test that mechanism. Other potential contributors to examine include inconsistent definitions of irregular cycles, incomplete androgen testing, attributing symptoms to stress or normal variation, contraceptive use masking cycle patterns, and limited access to reproductive endocrinology. ([Gibson-Helm et al., 2017](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/102/2/604/2972079))

## The reproductive risks do not disappear at normal BMI

Ovulatory dysfunction should be managed on its own merits. A lean woman with prolonged amenorrhea does not become low priority because her glucose, lipids, and blood pressure are normal. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786))

PCOS is associated with an increased risk of endometrial hyperplasia and endometrial cancer, although the absolute probability of endometrial cancer remains low. Long-standing untreated amenorrhea adds risk, and guideline-listed preventive strategies include cycle regulation and regular progestogen exposure. These considerations apply across BMI categories. The guideline grades the supporting evidence as very low certainty and does not recommend routine endometrial-cancer screening solely because PCOS is present. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786))

Similarly, infertility treatment is determined principally by whether ovulation is occurring and whether other infertility factors are present. For anovulatory infertility without another identified factor, letrozole is the guideline-recommended first-line pharmacological ovulation-induction treatment. This recommendation is not restricted to women with obesity and is supported by high-certainty evidence. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786))

A metabolic-only framing can therefore be misleading. It may underestimate the clinical significance of infrequent cycles, endometrial protection, time-sensitive fertility goals, hirsutism, acne, and psychological burden in women whose BMI and routine metabolic panel appear reassuring. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786))

## Should treatment differ?

Treatment should differ, but the most defensible distinction is not simply **lean versus overweight**, and it is not yet a rigid A-through-D algorithm. Treatment should follow the problem being treated, the patient’s metabolic risk, reproductive plans, contraindications, and preferences. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786); [Dokras et al., 2025](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pmed.1004662))

### Lifestyle treatment is not synonymous with weight loss

Healthy eating, physical activity, and prevention of excess weight gain are relevant across PCOS phenotypes. Adequate sleep and smoking avoidance are also important health goals, particularly within broader cardiometabolic and preconception care. In women who are not overweight, however, the international guideline recommends focusing on healthy lifestyle and prevention of excess weight gain, not making weight loss the default therapeutic goal. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786))

This is an important correction to uniform PCOS counseling. Advising every woman with PCOS to lose weight can be clinically inappropriate and stigmatizing when she is already lean. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786))

### Cycle irregularity and androgen symptoms

Combined oral contraceptive pills can be used for irregular cycles and hirsutism when pregnancy is not being pursued and no contraindication is present. Progestin-only contraception or periodic progestogen therapy may be considered for endometrial protection when estrogen-containing treatment is unsuitable, although PCOS-specific evidence for progestin-only regimens is limited. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786))

These treatments are selected because of cycle and androgen-related goals, not because of BMI alone. Cardiovascular and thrombotic risk factors still matter when selecting hormonal contraception. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786))

### Metformin

The evidence base and guideline support for metformin are more substantial in PCOS accompanied by higher BMI or identifiable metabolic risk. A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized trials found modest average improvements in BMI, HOMA-IR, fasting glucose, and some lipid outcomes versus placebo, accompanied by substantially more mild gastrointestinal adverse effects. These pooled results do not establish that BMI itself is a proven modifier of relative treatment response. ([Melin et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/ejendo/article/189/2/S38/7236786))

Correspondingly, the international guideline states that metformin should be considered in adults with BMI of at least 25 kg/m² for anthropometric and metabolic outcomes. That recommendation is based on very low-certainty evidence. Metformin may be considered below BMI 25, but the guideline explicitly acknowledges limited evidence in that group. Metformin can still be reasonable in a lean woman with impaired glucose tolerance, substantial diabetes risk, or particular cycle-management circumstances; normal BMI alone is neither a mandate nor an absolute reason to withhold it. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786))

For hirsutism and irregular cycles, combined hormonal contraception is generally favored over metformin. For metabolic indications, metformin is favored over hormonal contraception. Combining the two appears to add little clinical benefit for many women with BMI no greater than 30 kg/m², although combination treatment may be more relevant in groups at high metabolic risk. These comparative recommendations are also based on very low-certainty evidence. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786))

### Fertility

For anovulatory infertility, treatment should not be reduced to “lose weight and take metformin.” Letrozole is first-line pharmacological ovulation induction when PCOS-related anovulation is the principal infertility factor. Body weight may affect pregnancy risks and treatment response, but ovulatory status and the complete infertility evaluation remain central. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786))

## What do phenotype-specific treatment trials show?

Direct phenotype-comparative trial evidence remains limited. The guideline organizes treatment mainly by clinical indication and metabolic risk, while recent trials have generally enrolled pooled or restricted PCOS groups without testing whether treatment effects differ across all four Rotterdam phenotypes. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786); [Dokras et al., 2025](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pmed.1004662))

The 2025 COMET-PCOS randomized trial is an instructive example. It enrolled 240 women with hyperandrogenic PCOS and BMI between 25 and 48 kg/m², assigning them to a low-dose combined oral contraceptive, extended-release metformin, or both for 24 weeks. In the main results, end-study metabolic-syndrome prevalence was 26.2% with metformin, or 17 of 65 participants; 28.6% with combined treatment, or 18 of 63; and 28.8% with contraception, or 17 of 59. The adjusted test for a linear trend across the three groups was not significant, with an odds ratio of 1.32, a 95% confidence interval of 0.82 to 2.15, and p = 0.26. This does not establish equivalence among the treatments. The paper reports that 169 participants completed the full trial, while the primary end-study analysis used each participant’s last qualifying visit and included 187 participants. Diarrhea affected more than 64% of the metformin and combined groups, attrition was higher than anticipated, and the trial was not powered for several secondary outcomes. ([Dokras et al., 2025](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pmed.1004662))

This was a subgroup-targeted trial, not a phenotype-comparative trial. It provides evidence for women with hyperandrogenic PCOS and elevated BMI but cannot determine the best treatment for lean PCOS, phenotype D, or whether BMI or phenotype modifies the treatment effect. ([Dokras et al., 2025](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pmed.1004662))

The cited guideline and subgroup-targeted trial do not provide randomized evidence showing, for example, that phenotype A should receive one drug sequence, phenotype C another, and phenotype D a third. Existing guidelines are already partly individualized, but primarily by **treatment indication and metabolic risk**, rather than by Rotterdam letter. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786); [Dokras et al., 2025](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pmed.1004662))

## How the four competing claims hold up

### 1. PCOS has at least four phenotypes with different profiles, and treatment should differ accordingly

**Partly supported.** Four Rotterdam phenotypes are widely used in adults, and hyperandrogenic presentations often have less favorable average cardiometabolic profiles than non-hyperandrogenic presentations. Body size and study ascertainment materially influence the observed differences. The cited studies did not use complete prospective A-through-D phenotyping to compare hard outcomes, and they do not validate phenotype-specific treatment protocols. ([Daan et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25239303/); [Carmina and Lobo, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36292002/); [Persson et al., 2026](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/41/7/1197/8676742); [Dokras et al., 2025](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pmed.1004662))

### 2. Lean PCOS is harder to diagnose, and hidden insulin resistance requires fasting insulin or OGTT

**Half right.** Lean PCOS exists, and neither normal BMI nor normal fasting glucose excludes dysglycemia. OGTT is the preferred glycemic test regardless of BMI. Routine fasting insulin or HOMA-IR, however, is not recommended because assays and clinically actionable thresholds are insufficiently standardized. Evidence on the exact additional OGTT yield specifically in lean women remains limited and heterogeneous. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786); [Stovall et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21194310/); [Zhu et al., 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30611404/))

### 3. The metabolic-syndrome framing underweights anovulation and infertility in lean women, who may remain undiagnosed for a decade

**The clinical concern is valid; the precise delay claim is not established.** Anovulation, endometrial protection, and infertility require attention regardless of BMI or metabolic-panel results. Diagnostic delay is common overall, but the cited survey did not compare delay by BMI or phenotype and therefore cannot demonstrate a routine ten-year delay specifically among lean women. ([Gibson-Helm et al., 2017](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/102/2/604/2972079); [Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786))

### 4. Treatment is too uniform, and the field needs phenotype-specific protocols

**Directionally reasonable, but overstated.** Current guidance already distinguishes among reproductive, androgen-related, metabolic, and fertility indications and does not recommend weight loss as the default goal for women who are not overweight. Neither the guideline nor the cited subgroup trial establishes treatment-response differences across A-through-D phenotypes after accounting for BMI and other risk factors. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786); [Dokras et al., 2025](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pmed.1004662))

## Evidence gaps

The most useful next studies would be:

- Prospective cohorts assigning complete Rotterdam phenotype at baseline and repeatedly over time, with BMI, waist circumference, ethnicity, medication exposure, pregnancy history, and hard cardiovascular and diabetes outcomes
- Studies reporting the additional diagnostic yield of OGTT over fasting glucose and HbA1c separately in BMI below 25, BMI 25-29.9, and obesity, with results further divided by phenotype and ethnicity
- Diagnostic-delay cohorts comparing time to diagnosis by BMI, presenting symptom, androgen status, age, and healthcare setting
- Randomized trials designed to test treatment-by-phenotype and treatment-by-BMI interactions rather than merely adjusting for those variables
- Trials using outcomes relevant to the treatment target: ovulation, live birth, menstrual regularity, endometrial protection, hirsutism, quality of life, incident diabetes, and cardiovascular events
- Better representation of phenotype D, lean women, adolescents, older reproductive-age women, and populations outside European and specialist-clinic cohorts

Fasting insulin should not be elevated to a required clinical test while these gaps are addressed. A more useful research question is whether standardized metabolic, reproductive, and androgen markers can identify groups in whom a particular treatment produces meaningfully better patient outcomes. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786))

## Quick reference

- Lean PCOS is a valid clinical presentation but not a separate Rotterdam phenotype. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786); [Carmina and Lobo, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36292002/))
- Higher BMI generally worsens average insulin-resistance, lipid, and blood-pressure markers. ([Zheng et al., 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/endocrinology/articles/10.3389/fendo.2025.1680685/full))
- Lean or nonobese women with PCOS can still have greater metabolic risk than nonobese controls. ([Zhu et al., 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30611404/))
- Hyperandrogenic phenotypes often have higher cardiometabolic risk, but adiposity and other factors create substantial overlap. ([Daan et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25239303/); [Carmina and Lobo, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36292002/); [Persson et al., 2026](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/41/7/1197/8676742))
- OGTT is the preferred glycemic assessment in PCOS regardless of BMI. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786))
- Routine fasting-insulin or HOMA-IR testing is not recommended in clinical care. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786))
- Weight loss should not be reflexively prescribed to a woman who is not overweight. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786))
- The evidence base and guideline support for metformin are more substantial in higher-BMI or metabolically high-risk PCOS; evidence below BMI 25 is limited. ([Melin et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/ejendo/article/189/2/S38/7236786); [Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786))
- Cycle regulation, endometrial protection, and fertility treatment should not depend on obesity being present. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786))
- Letrozole is first-line pharmacological treatment for anovulatory PCOS infertility when no other infertility factor is identified. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786))
- The cited guideline and subgroup-targeted trial do not validate an A-through-D treatment algorithm. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786); [Dokras et al., 2025](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pmed.1004662))

## Practical interpretation

- Normal BMI should never be used to rule out PCOS. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786))
- A normal fasting glucose does not exclude abnormal post-load glucose; OGTT assesses this more accurately. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786); [Stovall et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21194310/))
- Fasting insulin is not a standardized substitute for OGTT and should not be treated as a required PCOS test. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786))
- Infrequent cycles deserve assessment and, where appropriate, endometrial protection even when metabolic markers are normal. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786))
- Treatment should be selected according to the immediate goal: androgen symptoms, cycle control, metabolic risk, ovulation, fertility, or weight-related health. ([Teede et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/38/9/1655/7241786))
- Body size and androgen phenotype can refine risk assessment, but neither should replace evaluation of the individual patient. ([Zheng et al., 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/endocrinology/articles/10.3389/fendo.2025.1680685/full); [Daan et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25239303/); [Carmina and Lobo, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36292002/))

## Funding and conflict-of-interest considerations

The 2025 lower-BMI-versus-higher-BMI meta-analysis reported no financial support and no commercial or financial relationships that could be construed as conflicts. The 2026 Swedish cohort was supported by the Family Planning Fund, the Selanders Fund at Uppsala University, and a part-time research position funded by Uppsala University Hospital; the funders reported no role in study design, analysis, or publication. One author reported lecture fees from Merck AB, unrelated to the manuscript. ([Zheng et al., 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/endocrinology/articles/10.3389/fendo.2025.1680685/full); [Persson et al., 2026](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/41/7/1197/8676742))

The 2023 metformin meta-analysis reported a postdoctoral research grant to one author from the Orion Research Foundation and an honorarium to another from Novo Nordisk for a lecture on PCOS and obesity. Another author was a member of the journal’s editorial board but was not involved in review or editorial decisions for the paper; the remaining authors reported no conflicts. These relationships do not invalidate the analysis, but they are relevant disclosures and should be considered alongside study design, risk of bias, heterogeneity, and certainty of evidence. ([Melin et al., 2023](https://academic.oup.com/ejendo/article/189/2/S38/7236786))

COMET-PCOS was funded by the US National Institutes of Health rather than a drug manufacturer, and the funder had no reported role in the study. Two investigators disclosed consulting relationships with pharmaceutical and medical-technology companies, including Eli Lilly, Novo Nordisk, Ferring, Mayhealth, Monsanto, Organon, Celmatix, and Covis Pharma. The trial should therefore be interpreted with both its independent public funding and disclosed author relationships in view. ([Dokras et al., 2025](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pmed.1004662))
