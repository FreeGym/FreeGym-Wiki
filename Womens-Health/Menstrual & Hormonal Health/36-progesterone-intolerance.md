# “Progesterone Intolerance” Is a Real Response Pattern - but Not Yet a Single Diagnosis

“Progesterone intolerance” describes a clinically credible response pattern: some women experience depression, anxiety, irritability, sedation, bloating, headache, or other symptoms during the luteal phase or after starting a progesterone-related medication. Experimental reproducibility is best established in prospectively confirmed PMDD cohorts, whereas reactions to exogenous medications are more heterogeneous and require evaluation of the specific molecule, dose, route, and timing. ([Schmidt et al., 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9435325/), [Wei et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41030005/), [Panay and Studd, 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9286739/), [Wallis et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41791561/))

The evidence does **not**, however, support one universal condition in which progesterone is intrinsically toxic or depressogenic. The strongest causal evidence comes from premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD), where susceptible women show an abnormal central nervous system response to otherwise normal **changes** in ovarian steroids. Allopregnanolone and altered GABA-A receptor adaptation are plausible parts of that response, but they do not explain every adverse reaction to every synthetic progestin. ([Schmidt et al., 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9435325/), [Schmidt et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28427285/), [Wei et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41030005/), [Hantsoo and Epperson, 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32435664/), [Sitruk-Ware, 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15063480/))

The most defensible conclusion is therefore:

> **Progesterone-associated symptom worsening is real, but “progesterone intolerance” is an imprecise umbrella term covering several potentially different phenomena.**

It can include PMDD or severe PMS, an expected pharmacologic effect such as sedation from oral micronized progesterone, a molecule- or dose-specific adverse reaction to a synthetic progestin, or a nonspecific worsening of an existing mood or migraine disorder. The studies and reviews summarized here do not establish a population prevalence for a progesterone-specific paradoxical mood phenotype, a diagnostic biomarker, or a reliable ranking of progestins by psychiatric tolerability. ([Nolan et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33245776/), [Panay and Studd, 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9286739/), [de Wit et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34099098/), [Londero et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39226622/), [Stiernman et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40701967/), [Barone et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41422264/))

## First, “progesterone” is not one exposure

Endogenous luteal-phase progesterone, oral micronized progesterone, levonorgestrel, norethindrone, drospirenone, and medroxyprogesterone acetate are not pharmacologically interchangeable. ([Sitruk-Ware, 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15063480/), [Pluchino et al., 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17052903/))

Micronized progesterone is chemically progesterone and can generate neuroactive metabolites such as allopregnanolone. Synthetic progestins vary in structure, metabolism, progesterone-receptor potency, and activity at androgen, mineralocorticoid, and glucocorticoid receptors. Levonorgestrel and norethindrone are 19-nortestosterone derivatives; drospirenone is spironolactone-derived and has antiandrogenic and antimineralocorticoid activity. Reviews of progestin pharmacology specifically caution against assuming that all effects are class effects. ([Pluchino et al., 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17052903/), [Sitruk-Ware, 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15063480/))

This matters mechanistically. Synthetic progestins differ from progesterone in structure, metabolism, and receptor effects, so a response to one exposure cannot automatically be generalized to another. Feeling markedly worse with a levonorgestrel IUD therefore does not, by itself, demonstrate allopregnanolone sensitivity; conversely, tolerating levonorgestrel does not prove that oral micronized progesterone will be tolerated. The pharmacology establishes non-equivalence, but it does not provide a validated rule for predicting individual cross-tolerance. ([Sitruk-Ware, 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15063480/), [de Wit et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34099098/), [Londero et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39226622/))

Sedation also needs to be separated from paradoxical anxiety or depression. Allopregnanolone usually enhances GABA-A signaling and can be sedating. Randomized studies and a systematic review have found that oral micronized progesterone can improve some sleep outcomes, although the studies are generally small and concentrated in postmenopausal women. Daytime sleepiness may still be an unacceptable adverse effect and is compatible with expected sedative pharmacology. The sleep trials did not test whether mood symptoms reflect paradoxical GABA-A excitation, so they cannot resolve that mechanism. ([Pluchino et al., 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17052903/), [Nolan et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33245776/))

## What ovarian suppression and hormone addback actually prove

The clearest causal evidence for ovarian-steroid sensitivity comes from experiments that temporarily suppress ovarian function and then reintroduce individual hormones or controlled hormone combinations. ([Schmidt et al., 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9435325/), [Schmidt et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28427285/), [Wei et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41030005/))

In a classic blinded study, women whose premenstrual symptoms remitted during ovarian suppression experienced symptom recurrence when estradiol or progesterone was reintroduced. The same hormone exposures did not provoke comparable symptoms in asymptomatic controls. This demonstrated that the disorder reflects an abnormal behavioral response to normal gonadal steroids rather than abnormal circulating hormone concentrations alone. ([Schmidt et al., 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9435325/))

A later experiment refined the result. In 12 women whose PMDD symptoms remitted during ovarian suppression and who continued into the analyzed addback phase, symptoms increased during the first month of continuous combined estradiol/progesterone addback but then remitted during subsequent months despite continued, stable hormone exposure. The trigger therefore appeared to be the **transition from low to higher combined ovarian-steroid concentrations**, not the maintained concentration itself. Because estradiol and progesterone were added together, the design could not identify which hormone was responsible for the initial recurrence. ([Schmidt et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28427285/))

A 2025 replication-extension included participants from the original cohort and an independent second cohort, yielding 34 women with PMDD and 76 healthy comparison participants under ovarian suppression, estradiol addback, and progesterone addback. At the group level, both hormones increased affective symptoms in women with PMDD but not healthy controls; irritability and mood swings were more closely associated with progesterone than estradiol. Ten of the 34 women with PMDD did not meet the study’s symptom-recurrence threshold during either hormone addback, underscoring heterogeneity within PMDD. The magnitude of hormone change did not explain symptom severity, again pointing to differential sensitivity rather than excessive exposure. ([Wei et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41030005/))

Nor does the available metabolomic evidence show that symptomatic women simply produce more allopregnanolone. In a controlled study of 15 hormone-sensitive women with PMDD and 15 controls, progesterone addback produced similar increases in progesterone-derived neurosteroids, including allopregnanolone, in both groups. No between-group difference in the formation of those metabolites was identified. ([Nguyen et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28786978/))

Together, these studies support a **steroid-change sensitivity model**:

- Changes in ovarian steroids act as proximal triggers in experimentally hormone-responsive PMDD. ([Schmidt et al., 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9435325/), [Schmidt et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28427285/), [Wei et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41030005/))

- Circulating levels and neurosteroid production can be normal. ([Schmidt et al., 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9435325/), [Nguyen et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28786978/))

- The abnormality is probably downstream, involving neural response, receptor adaptation, or interacting neurotransmitter systems. ([Hantsoo and Epperson, 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32435664/), [Stiernman et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40701967/), [Barone et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41422264/))

- Progesterone is important, but estradiol transitions can also provoke symptoms. ([Schmidt et al., 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9435325/), [Schmidt et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28427285/), [Wei et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41030005/))


That is stronger evidence than an ordinary association study, but it applies primarily to prospectively confirmed PMDD. It does not establish that every adverse reaction to an IUD, progestin-only pill, or HRT regimen has the same mechanism. ([Schmidt et al., 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9435325/), [Schmidt et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28427285/), [Wei et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41030005/))

## The allopregnanolone-GABA-A hypothesis

Allopregnanolone is a progesterone-derived neuroactive steroid and a positive allosteric modulator of the GABA-A receptor. Under many circumstances, that action is inhibitory, anxiolytic, anticonvulsant, and sedating. The PMDD hypothesis is not simply that allopregnanolone is “bad”; it is that dynamic rises and falls in allopregnanolone may be accompanied by abnormal GABA-A receptor adaptation or altered sensitivity in susceptible individuals. ([Pluchino et al., 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17052903/), [Hantsoo and Epperson, 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32435664/))

### Symptomatic-versus-asymptomatic challenge studies

A 1998 study administered intravenous pregnanolone, a closely related GABA-A-active neurosteroid, to 12 women with PMS and 12 controls during follicular and luteal phases. Controls demonstrated the expected slowing of saccadic eye movements in both phases and greater subjective sedation in the luteal phase. Women with PMS showed a dose response only in the follicular phase and did not show the same luteal sedation. Greater symptom severity was associated with a smaller response. The investigators interpreted this as reduced luteal GABA-A sensitivity. ([Sundström et al., 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9508043/))

A later pilot study administered intravenous allopregnanolone to 10 women with PMDD and 10 controls. The groups showed opposite phase-dependent response patterns: women with PMDD had a greater functional response in the luteal than follicular phase, while controls showed the reverse. ([Timby et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26960697/))

The studies are compatible in showing **altered menstrual-cycle regulation of neurosteroid sensitivity**, but they do not establish one simple direction such as permanent hypersensitivity or permanent resistance. They were also very small and used saccadic eye velocity as a pharmacodynamic marker rather than directly demonstrating progesterone-induced depression or anxiety. ([Sundström et al., 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9508043/), [Timby et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26960697/))

### Blocking allopregnanolone formation

A randomized, double-blind crossover study tested dutasteride, a 5α-reductase inhibitor that prevents the luteal increase in allopregnanolone. Sixteen women with prospectively confirmed PMDD and 16 controls participated across two separate dose experiments, with eight women with PMDD and eight controls at each dose. The lower dose did not adequately suppress allopregnanolone and did not relieve symptoms. The higher dose prevented the luteal increase and significantly reduced irritability, sadness, anxiety, food cravings, and bloating in the PMDD group, without adversely affecting control participants. ([Martinez et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26272051/))

This is important preliminary intervention evidence that the 5α-reduced neurosteroid pathway matters: symptoms changed while progesterone itself remained present. Dutasteride also alters other 5α-reduced steroid pathways, so the experiment did not isolate allopregnanolone as the sole causal mediator. The study was nevertheless small, divided between two dose experiments, and not designed to estimate how frequently this mechanism occurs outside selected PMDD patients. It was primarily supported by NIH programs, although GlaxoSmithKline supplied dutasteride. ([Martinez et al., 2016](https://www.nature.com/articles/npp2015246))

### Antagonizing allopregnanolone’s GABA-A effect

Trials of sepranolone, an endogenous stereoisomer intended to antagonize allopregnanolone’s effects, produced mixed findings. An exploratory 2017 trial reported improvement, but its strongest results came from post hoc analyses of selected participants who adhered to treatment and showed a “pure” PMDD pattern. ([Bixo et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28319848/))

A subsequent 206-participant randomized trial did **not** find a statistically significant advantage over placebo on its prespecified primary total-symptom endpoint. Distress improved, and a post hoc per-protocol analysis using a longer nine-day symptom window favored the 10 mg dose. One author was employed by Asarina Pharma, the drug’s developer. ([Bäckström et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34597899/))

The treatment evidence therefore strengthens the case that allopregnanolone signaling matters in at least some patients, but it is not uniformly positive. It does not justify treating allopregnanolone as a universal cause of negative mood. ([Martinez et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26272051/), [Bixo et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28319848/), [Bäckström et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34597899/))

## What is known about GABA-A receptor variation

The human evidence is currently stronger for altered **receptor regulation and pharmacodynamic response** than for a specific inherited “progesterone intolerance gene.” ([Hantsoo and Epperson, 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32435664/), [Stiernman et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40701967/), [Barone et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41422264/))

A 2025 case-control study measured GABA-A receptor-subunit messenger RNA in peripheral blood mononuclear cells across menstrual phases and related it to functional brain imaging. Among 29 women with PMDD and 27 controls, women with PMDD had lower luteal-phase expression of the δ subunit than during their follicular phase, and lower δ-subunit expression was associated with greater emotion-related amygdala activation. δ-subunit expression was not associated with total premenstrual symptom severity, core mood symptoms, or serum allopregnanolone or isoallopregnanolone levels. The peripheral expression measure was not a direct measurement of receptor composition in the brain. ([Stiernman et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40701967/))

Another 2025 study followed a transdiagnostic sample of 112 participants, including 75 psychiatric outpatients recruited for suicidality and 37 controls, with repeated blood sampling and daily affect ratings. Average GABRG2 expression decreased during the midluteal phase. Individual differences in average expression or perimenstrual change in GABRG2, GABRA5, and GABRD were associated with differing cyclical trajectories of anxiety, irritability, and suicidal ideation. The relationship was not simply “higher is worse” or “lower is worse”; more stable expression was associated with less cyclical symptom change. ([Barone et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41422264/))

These studies are mechanistically interesting but remain exploratory. Messenger RNA in circulating cells is not a direct measurement of receptor composition in the brain, and associations with brain activation or symptoms do not establish causality. Neither study validates a clinical predictive test, and current PMDD guidance does not identify a validated GABA-A genotype, blood test, allopregnanolone concentration, or receptor assay that predicts which individual will become depressed on a particular progestin. ([Stiernman et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40701967/), [Barone et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41422264/), [ACOG, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37973069/))

## Is it simply repackaged PMS or PMDD?

For **endogenous, predictably cyclical symptoms**, the established PMS/PMDD framework is usually more precise than a new diagnosis of progesterone intolerance. ([ACOG, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37973069/), [Ismaili et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27378473/))

A woman whose symptoms reliably appear after ovulation, become most severe before menstruation, and substantially remit afterward may have PMS or PMDD regardless of whether she informally describes the problem as “not tolerating progesterone.” The label PMDD describes the clinical timing and impairment; the steroid-sensitivity model describes a possible mechanism. ([ACOG, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37973069/), [Ismaili et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27378473/), [Schmidt et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28427285/))

Prospective measurement matters because retrospective impressions can greatly overestimate PMDD. In one selected symptomatic sample, a retrospective screening questionnaire classified 34.6% of participants as having PMDD, whereas two cycles of prospective Daily Record of Severity of Problems ratings classified only 3.9% as having PMDD. These figures should not be interpreted as population prevalence estimates. ([Henz et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29132173/), [Ismaili et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27378473/))

But not every exogenous reaction is repackaged PMDD. A postmenopausal woman who develops sustained depression after the progestogen portion of HRT cannot literally have a luteal-phase disorder. Similarly, symptoms that begin after insertion of a levonorgestrel IUD and remain present rather than cycling with ovulation would not fit the timing criteria for PMDD and should be evaluated as a possible medication adverse effect. “Progestogen intolerance” has long been used in HRT literature as a practical tolerability and compliance label, but the 1997 review that used the term did not establish a prospectively validated psychiatric diagnosis. ([Panay and Studd, 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9286739/), [ACOG, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37973069/), [Ismaili et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27378473/))

A useful distinction is therefore:

|Observed pattern|Most defensible interpretation|
|---|---|
|Recurrent luteal symptoms with clear postmenstrual remission|Evaluate prospectively as PMS or PMDD. ([ACOG, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37973069/), [Ismaili et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27378473/))|
|Symptoms throughout the month, with premenstrual worsening|Consider an underlying disorder with premenstrual exacerbation rather than assuming PMDD. ([Ismaili et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27378473/))|
|New, sustained symptoms after starting or increasing an exogenous progestogen|Evaluate as a suspected adverse drug response to the specific exposure. ([Panay and Studd, 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9286739/), [Sitruk-Ware, 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15063480/))|
|Sleepiness after oral micronized progesterone|Often compatible with an expected sedative pharmacologic effect. ([Nolan et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33245776/), [Pluchino et al., 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17052903/))|
|Different reactions to different formulations or doses|Possible molecule-, dose-, route-, or regimen-specific effect; assess the exact exposure. ([Sitruk-Ware, 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15063480/), [Skovlund et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10928294/))|
|Cyclical hives, angioedema, asthma, or anaphylaxis|Consider progestogen hypersensitivity, a separate immunologic condition. ([Foer and Buchheit, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30712576/), [Chiarella et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37579875/))|

Progestogen hypersensitivity should not be conflated with mood intolerance. It is a rare syndrome described principally through case reports and small case series, with manifestations that can include dermatitis, urticaria, angioedema, respiratory symptoms, or anaphylaxis after endogenous or exogenous exposure. ([Foer and Buchheit, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30712576/), [Chiarella et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37579875/))

## Do different progestins have meaningfully different mood profiles?

### Pharmacologic differences: well established

The molecules clearly differ in receptor binding, metabolism, androgenic activity, and mineralocorticoid activity. It is therefore biologically plausible that an individual could tolerate one progestin but not another, but direct clinical evidence that intolerance to one molecule predicts tolerance to another is sparse. ([Sitruk-Ware, 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15063480/), [de Wit et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34099098/), [Londero et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39226622/))

### Reliable clinical ranking: not established

A 2021 systematic review and network meta-analysis included 14 randomized trials, with 12 trials and 5,833 participants contributing to the network. Across ten hormonal contraceptive interventions, none clearly worsened depressive symptoms relative to placebo, and there was no persuasive difference between androgenic and antiandrogenic progestins. However, certainty was very low, follow-up was generally about three cycles, first-time users were uncommon, and many trials excluded participants with current depression. Those design features limit the trials’ ability to characterize uncommon early intolerance or effects in patients with active depression; the review did not directly test a subgroup defined by rapid discontinuation. ([de Wit et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34099098/))

A 2026 meta-analysis of 14 prospective and observational studies involving 2,425,648 participants reached a different average conclusion. Oral contraceptive use was associated with depression diagnosis (RR 1.31), antidepressant initiation (RR 1.25), and a statistically significant but clinically small increase in depressive symptom scores (SMD 0.12). Much of the evidence was observational, heterogeneity was very high for diagnostic and treatment outcomes, and subgroup analysis found no clear difference in symptom effects by progestogen content. This supports monitoring mood but does not establish that oral contraceptives caused the pooled increase or identify a reliably “good” or “bad” progestin. ([Kellock et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42061049/))

The apparent disagreement is understandable. Randomized trials are generally better positioned for causal inference but, in this literature, often enrolled selected users for short periods and frequently excluded current depression. Large observational studies include more real-world users and can detect uncommon outcomes but are more vulnerable to confounding by indication, age, health-care use, treatment selection, and discontinuation. ([de Wit et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34099098/), [Kellock et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42061049/))

### What the head-to-head studies show

A randomized trial comparing combined pills containing drospirenone or levonorgestrel found broadly similar effects. Drospirenone produced a modest advantage for negative affect during the menstrual phase, but the difference in reported emotional-wellbeing improvement - 61% versus 51% - was not statistically significant. Because both products also contained ethinyl estradiol, the study cannot isolate a pure progestin effect. ([Kelly et al., 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20384388/))

In an ancillary randomized study of 605 women from the ECHO trial, depression at three months differed little among depot medroxyprogesterone acetate, a levonorgestrel implant, and a copper IUD. At 12 months, depression was lowest with depot medroxyprogesterone acetate, at 9.6%, and highest with the copper IUD, at 17.7%. The investigators cautioned that disappointment about not receiving the locally preferred injectable method may have affected the result. Because the methods differed in route, reversibility, hormone exposure, and other features, the study did not test a simple systemic progestin-exposure gradient. Its findings do not support a universal mood ranking across these three methods. The study reported public and nonprofit funding and no author conflicts. ([Singata-Madliki et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33448029/))

A Danish cohort of 46,565 first-time levonorgestrel-IUD users provides suggestive dose-level evidence. Standardized rates of first antidepressant initiation were 30.8 per 1,000 person-years with the 52 mg system, 19.8 with the 19.5 mg system, and 17.7 with the 13.5 mg system. Compared with the 52 mg system, adjusted rate ratios were 0.77 and 0.85 for the two lower-dose devices. Lower-dose systems were therefore associated with lower rates than the 52 mg system, but the adjusted estimates were not monotonic across the two lower doses. The study was observational, used antidepressant initiation as a proxy for depression, and could not eliminate differences in which women were offered each device, so it does not establish a causal dose-response. It was funded by the independent health insurer Sygeforsikringen “Danmark.” ([Skovlund et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10928294/))

For menopausal HRT, a 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis of 16 randomized trials found no pooled signal that adding a progestin to estrogen worsened validated depression scores on average. Most studies used medroxyprogesterone acetate, however, and methodological heterogeneity and aggregate symptom scores limit inference about uncommon susceptible subgroups. The authors concluded that evidence was too sparse to determine differential effects among individual progestins. ([Londero et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39226622/))

Small direct HRT comparisons are inconsistent. A 23-participant pilot found no detectable mood effect from either micronized progesterone or medroxyprogesterone acetate. ([Cummings and Brizendine, 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12082361/)) A separate one-month randomized study found lower depression and irritability with conjugated estrogen/medroxyprogesterone than with estradiol/norethindrone acetate, but both the estrogen and progestin changed simultaneously, making attribution to norethindrone impossible. A prior PMS history predicted more adverse mood and physical symptoms in that trial. ([Ödmark et al., 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15259288/))

Thus, the claim that different progestins have meaningfully different mood profiles is only partly established:

- **Yes:** these drugs have meaningfully different pharmacology. ([Sitruk-Ware, 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15063480/))

- **Probably:** dose, route, schedule, and molecule can matter to an individual, although comparative evidence is limited. ([Skovlund et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10928294/), [Londero et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39226622/))

- **Not established:** a universal psychiatric-tolerability hierarchy such as “drospirenone best, levonorgestrel worst” or “micronized progesterone always safer.” ([de Wit et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34099098/), [Londero et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39226622/))


The migraine evidence is weaker still. Headache is often recorded as a nonspecific adverse event, without adequate distinction between migraine, migraine with aura, hormonally triggered migraine, and ordinary headache. A systematic review and consensus statement found that evidence on exogenous estrogens and progestogens and migraine course was low quality and absent for many molecules and non-oral methods. Current evidence therefore does not support a confident molecule-by-molecule migraine ranking. ([Sacco et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30171365/))

## What discontinuation and dropout data show

Real-world discontinuation studies confirm that mood symptoms matter to some users, but they rarely identify the responsible molecule or mechanism. ([Wallis et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41791561/), [Ma and Song, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37365881/))

A 2026 prospective cohort followed 4,425 people initiating a new contraceptive method; 880 discontinued within six months. Among those who discontinued, mood-related symptoms contributed to the decision for 28% of progestin-only users, 21% of combined-method users, and 10% of nonhormonal users. Among progestin-only discontinuers, mood symptoms were cited by approximately 40% of implant users, 33% of injection users, 25% of hormonal-IUD users, and 7.4% of progestin-only-pill users. ([Wallis et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41791561/))

Those percentages are **among discontinuers**, not among everyone who started each method. The comparison is also difficult to interpret mechanistically because an implant, injection, IUD, and pill differ simultaneously in molecule, dose, pharmacokinetics, reversibility, bleeding profile, and user population. The study shows that mood is a meaningful tolerability concern among some discontinuers, not that implants cause mood symptoms in 40% of all users. ([Wallis et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41791561/))

The 2023 Cochrane review of drospirenone/ethinyl estradiol for PMS or PMDD found more withdrawals for adverse events than with placebo: OR 3.41 across four trials, corresponding to an estimated 6%-16% withdrawal risk on active treatment when placebo withdrawal was 3%. Nausea and intermenstrual bleeding may have been more common with active treatment, while effects on nervousness, headache, and asthenia were uncertain. The review reported withdrawals for adverse events as an aggregate outcome and did not establish those individual symptoms as the reasons for withdrawal. No qualifying trial directly compared drospirenone with another active combined pill for PMDD treatment. ([Ma and Song, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37365881/))

Three of the five underlying drospirenone trials were manufacturer-funded, and two included manufacturer employees. That does not invalidate the findings, but it lowers confidence in favorable estimates, particularly because the evidence was already rated low quality and placebo responses were substantial. ([Ma and Song, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10289136/))

The practice-based explanation that limited opportunities to switch formulations promote class-wide over-attribution is therefore plausible. Short trials, exclusion of participants with current depression, enrollment of experienced contraceptive users, and early self-selection out of poorly tolerated methods can make average trial results less informative about a susceptible new user. The reviews and trials summarized here do not provide rigorous structured switching studies in which symptomatic women are prospectively assigned to sequences of micronized progesterone, levonorgestrel, norethindrone, drospirenone, and other options with daily mood ratings. The proposition that much apparent “class intolerance” would disappear after systematic switching remains plausible but unproven. ([de Wit et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34099098/), [Londero et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39226622/), [Wallis et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41791561/))

## Funding and conflict-of-interest considerations

Several important mechanistic studies were publicly funded, particularly the NIH ovarian-suppression, addback, metabolomics, and dutasteride experiments. ([1998 addback study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9435325/), [2017 transition study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28427285/), [metabolomics study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28786978/), [dutasteride study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26272051/)) The 2025 addback study nevertheless disclosed that one investigator had received research support or advisory roles from neuropsychiatric drug companies, while the remaining authors reported no commercial relationships. ([Wei et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41030005/))

The larger sepranolone trial included an employee of Asarina Pharma, and its primary endpoint was negative; favorable findings were confined to a secondary distress outcome and post hoc analysis. This makes the mixed result more appropriate than presenting the trial as straightforward confirmation of the allopregnanolone hypothesis. ([Bäckström et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34597899/))

The 2025 GABA-A receptor-expression study disclosed an Asarina Pharma advisory-board relationship for one author. ([Stiernman et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40701967/)) The later transdiagnostic receptor-expression study disclosed consultancy and patent interests related to neurosteroid therapeutics for another author. ([Barone et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41422264/)) These conflicts do not negate the biological observations, but the findings need independent replication using direct central nervous system measures.

By contrast, the ECHO mood comparison reported public and nonprofit funding and no competing interests. ([Singata-Madliki et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33448029/)) The Danish IUD-dose cohort was independently funded, although one investigator reported grants from pharmaceutical manufacturers for unrelated studies. ([Skovlund et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10928294/))

## Evidence-grade verdict

|Claim|Verdict|What the evidence supports|
|---|---|---|

They do not estimate the proportion of all progesterone or progestin users with this mechanism. ## Practical interpretation

A single bad experience should be documented at the level of the **exact exposure**, rather than recorded simply as “all progesterone intolerant.” Clinically useful details include the molecule, dose, route, continuous versus cyclic schedule, time from each dose to symptom onset, whether symptoms persist or fluctuate, and how quickly they remit after stopping. This exposure-specific approach is consistent with the documented pharmacologic differences among progestins and the practical HRT literature on progestogen tolerability. ([Sitruk-Ware, 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15063480/), [Panay and Studd, 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9286739/))

For endogenous symptoms, two cycles of daily ratings are much more informative than retrospective recall. For an exogenous medication, a baseline symptom period followed by daily ratings after initiation or a dose change is a pragmatic application of the same prospective principle, not a separately validated diagnostic test. It can help reveal whether a symptom shift closely follows the exposure rather than the background variability of depression, anxiety, migraine, sleep, or perimenopausal symptoms. ([Henz et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29132173/), [Ismaili et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27378473/))

Where medically appropriate, changing one variable at a time - dose, route, schedule, or molecule - provides more interpretable information than assuming a reaction applies to the entire class. A deliberate rechallenge should not be undertaken without clinical review when the prior event included severe depression, suicidal thinking, serious neurologic symptoms, or suspected hypersensitivity. When progestogen hypersensitivity is suspected, diagnostic challenge or desensitization belongs under specialist supervision. ([Sitruk-Ware, 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15063480/), [Panay and Studd, 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9286739/), [Chiarella et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37579875/))

## Conclusion

“Progesterone intolerance” is **not merely imaginary or dismissible as ordinary PMS**. Reproductive-steroid sensitivity is experimentally reproducible in selected PMDD cohorts, and altered allopregnanolone/GABA-A signaling is one of the better-supported biological models in PMDD. ([Schmidt et al., 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9435325/), [Schmidt et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28427285/), [Wei et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41030005/), [Hantsoo and Epperson, 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32435664/))

But the phrase currently collapses too many different exposures and outcomes into one label. The evidence is strongest for abnormal sensitivity to **hormonal transitions** in PMDD, moderate for an allopregnanolone/GABA-A mechanism in a subset, weaker for specific molecule-by-molecule mood differences, and very weak for any estimate of how common a progesterone-specific paradoxical response is in the general population. ([Schmidt et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28427285/), [Martinez et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26272051/), [de Wit et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34099098/), [Londero et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39226622/))

A more precise clinical formulation would be:

> **Suspected progestogen-associated adverse mood response, specifying the molecule, route, dose, timing, and whether the pattern is cyclical.**

That wording validates the reaction without prematurely concluding that endogenous progesterone, micronized progesterone, levonorgestrel, drospirenone, norethindrone, and medroxyprogesterone are one biologically uniform exposure. ([Sitruk-Ware, 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15063480/), [Pluchino et al., 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17052903/))
