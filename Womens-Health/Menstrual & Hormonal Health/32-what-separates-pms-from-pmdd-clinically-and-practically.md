# What Separates PMS From PMDD, Clinically and Practically?

The most defensible answer is that PMDD is a recognized and clinically useful diagnosis, but not a disorder with a proven biological fault line separating it from PMS. The diagnosis identifies a tightly specified cyclic syndrome with required affective content and substantial impairment. The cutoff is clinically useful, yet current neurobiological research does not show that people just above and below it have categorically different mechanisms. ([ACOG, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1097/AOG.0000000000005426); [Eisenlohr-Moul et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2016.15121510); [Monteiro et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpsychires.2024.05.024))

Prospective symptom tracking is therefore not a formality. It is the main evidence that symptoms are truly cycle-linked and clear after menstruation. History, clinical assessment, and targeted evaluation are also needed to determine whether symptoms are better explained by premenstrual exacerbation of another psychiatric or medical condition, medication or substance effects, or a noncyclical symptom pattern. ([ACOG, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1097/AOG.0000000000005426); [Eisenlohr-Moul et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2016.15121510); [O'Brien et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00737-010-0201-3))

## First, correct the prevalence claim

The often-repeated statement that 70 to 90 percent of women have PMS is too broad. Up to about 90 percent report at least one premenstrual symptom, but clinically significant PMS is generally estimated at about 20 to 30 percent. Having breast tenderness, bloating, irritability, or another isolated symptom before a period is not the same as having a syndrome that causes meaningful distress or impairment. ([ACOG, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1097/AOG.0000000000005426))

The familiar estimate that PMDD affects 3 to 8 percent also depends heavily on how diagnosis is made. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis of 44 studies and 50,659 participants estimated a pooled prevalence of 3.2 percent for prospectively confirmed PMDD and 7.7 percent for provisional diagnoses. In community samples using prospective confirmation, the estimate was 1.6 percent. Heterogeneity was very high in the overall analyses, so these are not universal constants, but studies using retrospective or provisional classification report substantially higher estimates. ([Reilly et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2024.01.066))

## The formal boundary is more than severity alone

Clinically significant PMS can also impair work, relationships, school, and daily life. Functional impairment is therefore not unique to PMDD. What DSM criteria add is a specified symptom count, affective content, time course, clearance after menstruation, and exclusion of better explanations. ([ACOG, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1097/AOG.0000000000005426); [O'Brien et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00737-010-0201-3))

|Feature|Clinically significant PMS|PMDD|
|---|---|---|
|Timing|Recurrent premenstrual symptoms that remit during or shortly after menstruation, with a symptom-free or nearly symptom-free interval|Symptoms emerge in the final premenstrual week, improve within days after menstruation begins, and become minimal or absent in the postmenstrual week|
|Symptom pattern|Physical and/or psychological symptoms; no single universally accepted fixed count|At least 5 of 11 specified symptoms, including at least 1 core affective symptom: mood lability, irritability or anger, depressed mood, or anxiety and tension|
|Impact|Meaningful distress or interference is required under clinically significant definitions|Clinically significant distress or interference with work, school, social activity, or relationships|
|Confirmation|International expert criteria recommend daily prospective ratings for at least 2 cycles|DSM permits a provisional diagnosis from history, but confirmation requires prospective daily ratings during at least 2 symptomatic cycles|
|Competing explanations|A core premenstrual disorder should not be merely the worsening of another condition|Symptoms must not be better explained by premenstrual exacerbation of another disorder, a substance or medication effect, or a medical condition|

This makes PMDD more than a synonym for "very severe PMS," but it does not make the five-symptom threshold a biological boundary. In secondary analyses used to evaluate the diagnostic criteria, the optimal symptom count was two in a community cohort and seven in a clinical cohort. When the cohorts were weighted equally, four or more symptoms best balanced sensitivity and specificity for predicting impairment. The five-symptom rule is consequently best understood as an operational threshold that improves consistency, not a discovered point at which biology abruptly changes. ([Hartlage et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1001/archgenpsychiatry.2011.1368))

The broader International Society for Premenstrual Disorders framework similarly treats PMS and PMDD as core premenstrual disorders that share cyclicity, postmenstrual clearance, recurrence, impairment, and prospective confirmation. PMDD adds the narrower psychiatric symptom requirements. The consensus meeting received unrestricted funding from Bayer Schering Pharma; the authors stated that the company did not attend the discussions or influence the recommendations. ([O'Brien et al., 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4134928/))

## What prospective tracking actually establishes

A retrospective question such as "Are you worse before your period?" is a screen, not confirmation. Daily ratings across the full cycle must document a premenstrual rise, improvement shortly after menstruation begins, minimal or absent symptoms in the postmenstrual interval, sufficient symptom severity, and recurrence across cycles. Clinically significant distress or interference, and psychiatric, medical, medication, or substance explanations, must also be assessed clinically. The Daily Record of Severity of Problems is a widely used, validated, DSM-aligned instrument, and the C-PASS provides standardized scoring of symptom timing, severity, cyclicity, and postmenstrual clearance across at least two cycles. It is not a standalone diagnostic evaluation. ([ACOG, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1097/AOG.0000000000005426); [Eisenlohr-Moul et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2016.15121510); [O'Brien et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00737-010-0201-3))

In the 200-person C-PASS development sample, the authors reported 94.5 percent raw agreement, with 11 disagreements, between C-PASS and a prior expert classification of any menstrually related mood disorder versus none. After reviewing the discrepant records, they estimated 98 percent correct classification. The comparison used the same underlying symptom-rating data and was neither an independent external validation nor a PMDD-specific accuracy study. It therefore supports standardized scoring of prospective symptom data, but does not validate PMDD against a blood test, brain scan, or other independent biological reference standard. ([Eisenlohr-Moul et al., 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5205545/))

Tracking can materially change the label. In a selected Brazilian sample recruited through local media from women with PMS complaints, a retrospective screening tool classified 34.6 percent as having PMDD, while two-cycle daily ratings classified 3.9 percent. Only 6.8 percent of retrospective PMDD classifications were confirmed prospectively. These comparisons apply to the 127 participants who completed two cycles, out of 282 eligible participants. The study therefore demonstrates the risk of screening overclassification and the burden of tracking, not a population overdiagnosis rate. ([Henz et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-0037-1608672))

## A central diagnostic mimic is premenstrual exacerbation

Premenstrual exacerbation, or PME, means that an underlying condition is present throughout the cycle and becomes worse premenstrually. In PMDD, the characteristic symptoms become minimal or absent after menstruation. Depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder, chronic pain, and other conditions can coexist with PMDD, so their presence does not automatically rule it out. What matters is whether daily full-cycle ratings show a postmenstrual interval in which the putative PMDD symptoms are minimal or absent, a chronic condition with a luteal worsening, or both patterns together. ([Kuehner and Nayman, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11920-021-01286-0); [O'Brien et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00737-010-0201-3))

When another disorder is present, prospective ratings should include that disorder's characteristic symptoms as well as PMDD symptoms. The distinction is clearest when genuinely distinct PMDD symptoms remit postmenstrually. Overlapping symptoms are harder to classify, and DSM-5 and ISPMD rules are not completely identical on this point. ([Kuehner and Nayman, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8502143/); [O'Brien et al., 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4134928/))

Sleep disruption and stress can coincide with premenstrual symptoms, but their presence does not establish or exclude PMDD. The relevant diagnostic evidence is a recurrent cycle-linked rise followed by postmenstrual clearance; symptoms that do not show that pattern do not meet prospective criteria for PMDD. There is no diagnostic blood test or scan for PMS or PMDD; laboratory testing is used only when the history suggests another medical explanation. ([ACOG, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1097/AOG.0000000000005426); [O'Brien et al., 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4134928/))

## Is PMDD biologically distinct from PMS?

PMDD has credible biological correlates and experimentally demonstrated sensitivity to ovarian-steroid change in a substantial subgroup. The stronger claim, that it has a mechanism categorically different from PMS, has not been demonstrated. ([Wei et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.20240596); [Hantsoo and Epperson, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ynstr.2020.100213))

A well-supported model is differential susceptibility to ordinary ovarian-steroid change rather than consistently abnormal hormone concentrations. In a 2025 replication-and-extension study, 49 prospectively characterized women with PMDD entered leuprolide-induced ovarian suppression; 34, or 69 percent, met the prespecified suppression-response criterion and proceeded to hormone addback, alongside 76 healthy comparison participants. Among those 34 selected responders, group-level affective symptoms increased during estradiol and progesterone addback, whereas controls did not become symptomatic. Twenty-four of the 34 responders met the recurrence threshold during at least one addback, while 10 did not. Estradiol concentrations did not differ by group; progesterone was higher in controls during progesterone addback, and the magnitude of hormone change did not correlate with affective-symptom severity. Because the addback group was selected for response during suppression, the addback findings generalize most directly to this responder subgroup rather than to everyone meeting PMDD criteria. ([Wei et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.20240596))

The study was supported by the NIMH Intramural Research Program. Rubinow reported research funding from the Baszucki Foundation and Sage Therapeutics, consulting for GH Research-Ireland, and scientific-advisory-board roles with stock options at Sage Therapeutics and Sensorium Therapeutics; the other authors reported no commercial financial relationships. ([Wei et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.20240596))

Allopregnanolone, a progesterone-derived neurosteroid that is a positive allosteric modulator of GABA-A receptors, is a leading candidate pathway. Human challenge studies and related work are consistent with altered adaptation or sensitivity to changing allopregnanolone levels in PMDD. However, peripheral allopregnanolone concentrations do not reliably distinguish PMDD from controls, the human challenge evidence is sparse, and much of the evidence concerning receptor-subunit plasticity comes from animal studies. A pilot allopregnanolone challenge included only 10 participants with PMDD and 10 controls. That pilot used saccadic eye velocity as an indirect functional readout after intravenous allopregnanolone; it did not measure brain receptor abundance or composition. The responsible formulation is "the findings are consistent with altered neurosteroid-GABAergic sensitivity," not "PMDD is caused by GABA-A receptor changes." Direct changes in GABA-A receptor abundance or subunit composition in the living human brain have not been established. The Hantsoo-Epperson review was supported by NIH grant K23 MH107831. ([Hantsoo and Epperson, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ynstr.2020.100213); [Timby et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-016-4258-1))

The Timby study was supported by Swedish public, regional, and university grants. Torbjörn Bäckström reported shares in Umecrine AB; the other authors declared no conflicts of interest. ([Timby et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-016-4258-1))

Neuroimaging does not settle the issue. A 2024 systematic review covered 24 studies and summed enrollments of 1,026 participants, including 608 with PMDD and 418 controls, and described an absence of consensual results across imaging methods. A newer 2026 review restricted to functional MRI covered 15 articles with summed enrollments of 598 participants, although the number of unique participants was lower because some samples overlapped. It reported recurring patterns involving emotion and control networks, but also noted small samples, heterogeneous tasks and cycle-phase definitions, and sample overlap. Both reviews were built around PMDD-versus-healthy-control comparisons, not direct comparisons with prospectively confirmed PMS. Neither review evaluated or validated individual-level diagnostic performance; group-level imaging differences cannot diagnose PMDD or distinguish it from PMS in an individual. Both reviews reported no financial support or competing commercial interests. ([Monteiro et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpsychires.2024.05.024); [Bartoszewicz and Szurowska, 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2026.1795420))

The mechanistic evidence therefore supports a clinically important, hormone-sensitive phenotype in a substantial subgroup. It does not establish a natural discontinuity at the DSM symptom threshold. Most studies ask whether PMDD differs from asymptomatic controls, when the decisive scientific question is whether prospectively confirmed PMS and PMDD differ from each other after matching for symptom type, timing, comorbidity, and cycle phase. ([Wei et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.20240596); [Monteiro et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpsychires.2024.05.024))

## Is PMDD underdiagnosed or overdiagnosed?

Both errors are plausible, and existing evidence does not establish which is larger. ([Craner et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1080/03630242.2014.883658); [Reilly et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2024.01.066); [Henz et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-0037-1608672))

At least one small survey found that routine practice commonly did not meet prospective-confirmation standards. In a 2014 survey of 87 Maine family-medicine and obstetrics and gynecology physicians, 11.5 percent reported routinely using 60-day monitoring and 18.4 percent regularly used any daily symptom ratings. This small, older, geographically limited survey documents incomplete use of recommended diagnostic procedures; it does not establish how many cases were missed, falsely labeled, or merely left unconfirmed, and it cannot provide a current national estimate. ([Craner et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1080/03630242.2014.883658))

Retrospective screening can substantially overclassify PMDD relative to prospective confirmation. The gap between provisional and prospectively confirmed prevalence in the 2024 meta-analysis, together with the Henz reclassification, supports concern about false-positive labeling if a screen is treated as a diagnosis; neither study directly measures routine-care overdiagnosis. Severe PMS, PME, and noncyclic symptoms can all be mislabeled as PMDD when postmenstrual clearance is not measured. ([Reilly et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2024.01.066); [Henz et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-0037-1608672); [Kuehner and Nayman, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11920-021-01286-0); [O'Brien et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00737-010-0201-3))

What the evidence does not show is that PMDD is currently being overdiagnosed because SSRIs are easy to prescribe. The physician-practice survey, prevalence meta-analysis, and retrospective-versus-prospective comparison did not test whether SSRI access causes inflation of confirmed PMDD diagnoses. The supported conclusion is narrower: skipping prospective tracking leaves PMDD unconfirmed and creates a risk of diagnostic misclassification, while available studies do not quantify net underdiagnosis versus overdiagnosis. ([Craner et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1080/03630242.2014.883658); [Reilly et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2024.01.066); [Henz et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-0037-1608672))

## Treatment response does not reveal the diagnosis

SSRIs reduce premenstrual symptoms across a trial literature that enrolled PMS, PMDD, and the older category of late luteal phase dysphoric disorder. A 2024 Cochrane review included 34 randomized trials and 4,563 participants; the main pooled symptom estimate, based on 12 studies and 1,742 participants, was SMD -0.57. Both continuous and luteal-phase dosing reduced symptoms, but an across-trial subgroup comparison favored continuous treatment, with SMD -0.69 versus -0.39 and a subgroup-difference P value of 0.03, leading the review to judge continuous dosing probably more effective. Those SMD estimates came from an across-trial subgroup comparison rather than a direct randomized comparison. Three small direct-comparison trials involving 269 participants found no clear response-rate difference, and the review warned that its eligibility criteria might have missed some regimen-only comparisons, so the magnitude of any continuous-dosing advantage remains uncertain. SSRIs increased adverse effects including nausea, decreased energy or asthenia, and somnolence. Diagnosis-subgroup analyses found no detectable differential effect between PMDD and PMS, but the smaller PMS evidence base was heterogeneous and underpowered; failure to detect an interaction does not establish equivalent treatment effects. Treatment response therefore cannot distinguish PMDD from severe PMS or confirm the diagnosis. Sixty-eight percent of included trials were industry-funded, and a funnel plot for the response-rate outcome suggested possible publication bias. ([Jespersen et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD001396.pub4))

The oral-contraceptive evidence reviewed here concerns drospirenone plus ethinyl estradiol, mostly in PMDD. A 2023 Cochrane review of five trials and 858 analyzed participants found a small-to-moderate improvement in overall symptoms, with an SMD of -0.41 from two trials involving 514 participants, but rated that evidence low certainty. Withdrawal because of adverse effects was more common with drospirenone plus ethinyl estradiol, with an OR of 3.41, 95 percent CI 2.01 to 5.78, across four trials and 776 participants. It remains unclear whether benefit persists beyond three cycles, applies to less severe PMS, or exceeds that of other combined contraceptives. The review identified pharmaceutical-company funding in three of its five included trials, and two trials had manufacturer employees as coauthors. The small Pearlstein crossover trial was funded by Berlex Laboratories, now part of Bayer. This concentrated manufacturer involvement should be considered when interpreting the small, low-certainty evidence base. ([Ma and Song, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD006586.pub5); [Pearlstein et al., 2005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.contraception.2005.08.021))

Exercise may improve PMS symptoms, but the evidence does not establish a PMDD-specific response. A 2020 review included 15 randomized trials with 717 participants; seven trials with 265 participants contributed to the primary meta-analysis, which found SMD -1.08, 95 percent CI -1.88 to -0.29. Heterogeneity was very high at 87 percent, and 87 percent of included trials were judged at high risk of bias. Exercise can be offered as supportive care, but improvement with it is not diagnostic of either PMS or PMDD. ([Pearce et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.3399/bjgpopen20X101032))

## What evidence would clarify the boundary?

The most informative next studies would not compare PMDD only with healthy controls. They would enroll prospectively confirmed, cycle-tracked groups spanning no symptoms, mild symptoms, clinically significant PMS, and PMDD, then test whether biology or treatment response changes smoothly or discontinuously across severity.

Key needs include:

- Direct neurobiological comparisons of prospectively confirmed PMS and PMDD, with cycle phase, medication use, and psychiatric comorbidity controlled.

- Longitudinal daily-rating cohorts that measure how often individuals move across the PMDD threshold and whether those transitions reflect stable biology or changing stress, sleep, reproductive stage, and baseline mental health.

- Biomarker studies with preregistered thresholds and external validation, rather than group-level differences that cannot classify individuals.

- Randomized trials powered to test a treatment-by-diagnosis interaction, rather than merely showing that each group improves. These trials should randomize treatment within each prospectively confirmed diagnostic group and account for baseline severity both categorically and continuously.

- Comparative trials of SSRIs, hormonal suppression, psychotherapy, and lifestyle interventions using the same prospective diagnostic framework.


Without that design, similar treatment response across PMS and PMDD is compatible with a shared mechanism, but it cannot prove one. Different response rates are also insufficient unless the difference is prospectively specified, replicated, and shown to be larger than differences caused by symptom profile or comorbidity. Even a replicated treatment-by-diagnosis interaction would demonstrate predictive or treatment-selection utility, not by itself prove distinct etiology. ([Wang et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMsr077003); [Kent et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.7326/M18-3667))

## Practical interpretation

- Up to 90 percent reporting any premenstrual symptom is not the prevalence of clinically significant PMS. PMS is closer to 20 to 30 percent, while prospectively confirmed PMDD appears less common than provisional estimates suggest. ([ACOG, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1097/AOG.0000000000005426); [Reilly et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2024.01.066))

- PMDD is not defined by impairment alone. It requires a specific number and type of symptoms, marked cyclicity, postmenstrual clearance, impairment, and exclusion of better explanations. ([ACOG, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1097/AOG.0000000000005426); [Eisenlohr-Moul et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2016.15121510))

- Prospective daily tracking is diagnostic evidence, not paperwork. Together with clinical assessment of competing causes, it can reveal prospectively confirmed PMDD, severe PMS, PME, or a noncyclic condition. ([Henz et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-0037-1608672); [Kuehner and Nayman, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11920-021-01286-0))

- PMDD has credible neurobiology, especially differential sensitivity to physiologic ovarian-steroid changes in a substantial subgroup and a plausible allopregnanolone-GABA-A pathway. None of the cited studies validates a biomarker that distinguishes prospectively confirmed PMS from PMDD. ([Wei et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.20240596); [Hantsoo and Epperson, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ynstr.2020.100213); [Bartoszewicz and Szurowska, 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2026.1795420))

- A small 2014 Maine survey found that prospective confirmation was commonly skipped, and retrospective screens can substantially overclassify PMDD relative to daily ratings. How often routine care misses or falsely labels cases, and the net direction of diagnostic error, remain unknown. The cited evidence does not show that easy SSRI prescribing is causing a current wave of PMDD overdiagnosis. ([Craner et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1080/03630242.2014.883658); [Reilly et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2024.01.066); [Henz et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-0037-1608672))

- Treatment response is not a diagnostic test. SSRIs can help both PMS and PMDD, the cited drospirenone and ethinyl-estradiol evidence is concentrated in participants with PMDD, and exercise trials do not establish a category-specific response. ([Jespersen et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD001396.pub4); [Ma and Song, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD006586.pub5); [Pearce et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.3399/bjgpopen20X101032))


The cleanest synthesis is that PMDD is a recognized and clinically useful phenotype with experimentally demonstrated hormone sensitivity in a substantial subgroup, while its cutoff from PMS remains operational rather than biologically proven. That is not a reason to minimize PMDD. It is a reason to diagnose it with the prospective rigor that gives the category meaning. ([Eisenlohr-Moul et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2016.15121510); [Wei et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.20240596); [Monteiro et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpsychires.2024.05.024))
