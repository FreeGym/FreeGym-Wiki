# Is Intermittent Fasting Different for Women?

Female physiology is relevant, but the human evidence reviewed here does not support a blanket rule that women must always use shorter fasts than men. In selected women with obesity or polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), daily eating windows ranging from about 4 to 10 hours have not produced a consistent pattern of adverse change in the serum reproductive hormones measured. However, lean, regularly cycling, highly active women are poorly represented, so the evidence also cannot establish that restrictive eating windows are safe for “most women.” ([Jirapak et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261467643); [Kalam et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.23562); [Lin et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-024-01461-5); [Talebi et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.15801); [Corapi et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-026-04316-7)) ([Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/02601060261467643 "Impact of time-restricted eating on reproductive hormones and androgen markers in adult females: A systematic review - Aimee Jirapak, Emily Adams, Georgia M Black, Brooke L Devlin, 2026"))

The most direct causal human evidence relevant to reproductive suppression concerns **low energy availability** - too little dietary energy remaining after exercise to support normal physiology. Fasting can contribute to this problem if it reduces total intake or makes adequate intake harder, but a fasting schedule and low energy availability are not the same exposure. ([Angelidi et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1210/endrev/bnae011); [Chang et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.isci.2024.109000); [Loucks and Thuma, 2003](https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2002-020369); [De Souza et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1093/humrep/deab149))

## What “intermittent fasting” means

Intermittent fasting is an umbrella term that includes time-restricted eating, alternate-day fasting, modified alternate-day fasting, and periodic patterns such as 5:2 fasting. Most direct research on female reproductive hormones has examined **time-restricted eating**, in which food is consumed within a consistent daily window. An 8-hour eating window corresponds to roughly 16 hours without caloric intake; a 6-hour window corresponds to roughly 18 hours. ([Garegnani et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD015610.pub2); [Ezpeleta et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmet.2023.12.004); [Jirapak et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261467643))

Daily time-restricted eating, alternate-day fasting, and multi-day fasting are distinct interventions and should not be assumed to have interchangeable effects. A daily eating window that preserves total energy intake differs in duration and energy exposure from severe caloric restriction or prolonged fasting. ([Garegnani et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD015610.pub2); [Ezpeleta et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmet.2023.12.004))

A second distinction is whether an intervention is **energy matched**. In trials of adults with overweight or obesity, time-restricted eating often produces an energy deficit even when calorie counting is not prescribed. When actual intake is not matched, changes in weight or hormones cannot be attributed cleanly to meal timing rather than the energy deficit, weight loss, or a combination. The [2026 Cochrane review](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD015610.pub2) found that intermittent fasting generally produced little to no additional weight-loss benefit over standard dietary advice in adults with overweight or obesity over periods of up to 12 months, suggesting that fasting is primarily an optional way of organizing intake rather than a uniquely effective weight-loss intervention. ([Chang et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.isci.2024.109000); [Garegnani et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD015610.pub2)) ([Cochrane](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD015610_intermittent-fasting-traditional-dietary-advice-or-no-treatment-which-works-better-help-adults "Intermittent fasting for adults with overweight or obesity"))

## What the female-specific hormone evidence shows

A female-specific systematic review published in July 2026 identified only six eligible human studies. Its search covered research through May 2024, so it did not include later 2025 and 2026 PCOS trials. In randomized trials involving women with obesity, time-restricted eating did not show consistent changes in testosterone, estrogen, progesterone, luteinizing hormone, cortisol, dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA), or sex hormone-binding globulin. Nonrandomized PCOS studies sometimes reported lower testosterone or luteinizing hormone, but weight loss and study design limited causal interpretation. The review characterized the overall evidence as inconclusive rather than as proof that fasting is harmless or harmful. ([Jirapak et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261467643)) ([Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/02601060261467643 "Impact of time-restricted eating on reproductive hormones and androgen markers in adult females: A systematic review - Aimee Jirapak, Emily Adams, Georgia M Black, Brooke L Devlin, 2026"))

One frequently cited study was a small eight-week secondary analysis involving 23 women with obesity: 12 premenopausal and 11 postmenopausal. Participants used 4- or 6-hour eating windows. Testosterone, androstenedione, and sex hormone-binding globulin did not change significantly, but DHEA fell by approximately 13 to 14 percent in both groups. The DHEA estimates were highly variable, and their clinical significance is uncertain. Estradiol, estrone, and progesterone were assessed only in the postmenopausal participants. The study therefore did not determine whether restrictive windows altered ovulation, luteal function, cycle length, or menstrual regularity in cycling women. ([Kalam et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.23562)) ([The Ohio State University](https://ohiostate.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/effect-of-time-restricted-eating-on-sex-hormone-levels-in-premeno/ "Effect of time-restricted eating on sex hormone levels in premenopausal and postmenopausal females"))

A longer trial randomized 90 adults with obesity to an 8-hour eating window, daily calorie restriction, or a control condition for 12 months. Testosterone, DHEA, and sex hormone-binding globulin did not change significantly in the female subgroups. However, estradiol, estrone, and progesterone were again measured only in postmenopausal women. This provides useful but limited evidence that the study did not detect large, persistent changes in the measured hormones. It is not a trial of menstrual or ovulatory function in regularly cycling women. ([Lin et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-024-01461-5)) ([Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41430-024-01461-5 "Effect of time restricted eating versus daily calorie restriction on sex hormones in males and females with obesity"))

## Why unchanged hormone levels do not prove menstrual safety

Menstrual and ovulatory function cannot be established from one or two serum hormone measurements alone. Estradiol, progesterone, and luteinizing hormone vary across the menstrual cycle, and a single progesterone measurement can provide evidence of recent ovulation only when appropriately timed; it cannot define normal or fertile luteal function. Bleeding patterns alone also do not directly measure ovulation or luteal function. ([Draper et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-018-32647-0); [Practice Committees of ASRM and SREI, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fertnstert.2021.02.010))

The existing obesity trials rarely used prospectively tracked cycle length, confirmed ovulation, luteal-phase progesterone, amenorrhea incidence, or fertility as primary outcomes. Several of the hormones most relevant to ovarian function were measured only in postmenopausal participants, where cycle disruption is no longer an applicable outcome. ([Kalam et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.23562); [Lin et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-024-01461-5); [Jirapak et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261467643)) ([The Ohio State University](https://ohiostate.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/effect-of-time-restricted-eating-on-sex-hormone-levels-in-premeno/ "Effect of time-restricted eating on sex hormone levels in premenopausal and postmenopausal females"))

This means that “no significant change in sex hormones” is a narrower conclusion than “menstrual and ovulatory safety has been established.” The latter would require trials specifically designed and powered to measure cycle and ovulation outcomes. ([Jirapak et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261467643); [Practice Committees of ASRM and SREI, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fertnstert.2021.02.010))

## PCOS is a different population

In a 2024 randomized trial, 90 women with obesity and PCOS followed either early 14:10 time-restricted eating with a probiotic, the same schedule with placebo, or a conventional three-meal calorie-restricted diet for eight weeks. All groups lost a similar amount of weight - approximately 2.2 to 2.9 kilograms - and the trial did not detect significant between-group differences in menstrual, gonadal, or metabolic outcomes. Time-restricted eating was not superior to ordinary calorie restriction, but the study was not designed to establish equivalence or long-term reproductive safety. ([Talebi et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.15801)) ([Wiley Online Library](https://dom-pubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/dom.15801?af=R "The effects of time-restricted eating alone or in combination with probiotic supplementation in comparison with a calorie-restricted diet on endocrine and metabolic profiles in women with polycystic ovary syndrome: A randomized clinical trial"))

A 2025 randomized trial assigned 93 women with overweight or obesity and PCOS to an 8-hour eating window plus a prescribed 1,200-1,500 kcal/day diet or the same calorie prescription with usual meal timing for 16 weeks. Weight declined by 7.47 kilograms with time-restricted eating and 4.38 kilograms with usual meal timing, a between-group difference of 3.09 kilograms. Because the study prescribed rather than objectively controlled energy intake, it cannot isolate an independent effect of meal timing, and the result requires replication. ([Zheng et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.isci.2025.113654)) ([iScience](https://www.cell.com/iscience/fulltext/S2589-0042%2825%2901915-7 "Time-restricted eating plus calorie-restricted diet versus calorie-restricted diet in women with polycystic ovary syndrome"))

A 2026 randomized trial assigned 76 women with PCOS to a 6-hour eating window from 1 p.m. to 7 p.m., a 25 percent daily calorie restriction, or a control condition for six months. Body weight declined by 4.32 percent with time-restricted eating and 4.66 percent with calorie restriction. The estimated between-group difference was 0.34 percentage points (95 percent confidence interval, -2.15 to 2.83; P = 0.79), so the trial did not detect a difference but did not establish equivalence. No serious adverse events were reported, which is reassuring but does not establish reproductive or long-term safety. Adherence logs or food records were returned by 20 of 26 participants in the time-restricted-eating group and 19 of 26 in the calorie-restriction group. The primary outcome was weight management, however, not fertility or detailed ovulatory function. ([Corapi et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-026-04316-7)) ([Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-026-04316-7 "Time-restricted eating for body weight management in women with polycystic ovary syndrome: A randomized controlled trial"))

A 2026 meta-analysis pooled four randomized trials involving 216 women with PCOS and reported modest improvements in insulin-sensitivity markers with time-restricted eating. The small number of short trials and heterogeneous comparators did not establish long-term or reproductive safety. ([Hamsho et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu18132096)) ([Nutrients](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/18/13/2096 "The effects of time-restricted eating in women with polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome: A systematic review and meta-analysis"))

These trials do not support the claim that a 14- to 18-hour daily fast is inevitably hormonally disruptive for every woman. They also do not establish that an 18-hour fast is appropriate for a lean, highly active woman with otherwise regular cycles. PCOS is a heterogeneous disorder diagnosed from features that include ovulatory dysfunction and hyperandrogenism, so effects associated with weight loss or reduced androgen exposure in PCOS cannot automatically be generalized to women without PCOS. ([Talebi et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.15801); [Zheng et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.isci.2025.113654); [Corapi et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-026-04316-7); [Teede et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1210/clinem/dgad463))

## Do women tolerate fasting differently from men?

Direct evidence is inadequate. The reviews cited here do not provide adequately powered formal sex-by-treatment tests for reproductive outcomes. Statistical significance in women and men when analyzed separately does not establish that treatment effects differ or are identical; that requires a direct interaction test. ([Garegnani et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD015610.pub2); [Jirapak et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261467643); [Altman and Bland, 2003](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.326.7382.219))

The 2026 Cochrane review included 22 randomized trials with 1,995 participants. Only one trial enrolled men exclusively, while eight enrolled women exclusively; the review specifically noted the need for results disaggregated by sex to evaluate differences between women and men. It found little to no difference in weight loss between intermittent fasting and regular dietary advice, while the evidence concerning adverse events was very uncertain. ([Garegnani et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD015610.pub2))

The present evidence therefore supports neither of the strongest claims: it does not prove that women experience disruption at fasting durations that men tolerate, and it does not prove that women and men respond identically. ([Garegnani et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD015610.pub2); [Jirapak et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261467643); [Altman and Bland, 2003](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.326.7382.219))

## Low energy availability has direct causal human evidence

Energy availability is commonly defined as dietary energy intake minus exercise energy expenditure, expressed relative to fat-free mass. A person can develop low energy availability without intentionally dieting, for example when training volume rises but food intake does not. Conversely, someone can follow a daily fasting schedule without low energy availability if sufficient energy is consumed during the eating window. ([Angelidi et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1210/endrev/bnae011))

In controlled experiments, regularly menstruating women underwent several days at different energy-availability levels. Marked reductions disrupted the frequency and amplitude of luteinizing-hormone pulses, an early signal that the brain’s reproductive control system was responding to insufficient energy. The often-quoted level of 30 kcal/kg fat-free mass/day arose largely from short, controlled studies; later reviews caution that it is a preliminary research guide rather than a universal diagnostic threshold. Individual susceptibility, the duration of under-fueling, exercise load, and energy reserves also matter. ([Loucks and Thuma, 2003](https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2002-020369); [Angelidi et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1210/endrev/bnae011)) ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12519869/ "Luteinizing hormone pulsatility is disrupted at a threshold of energy availability in regularly menstruating women"))

The [REFUEL randomized trial](https://doi.org/10.1093/humrep/deab149) provides stronger causal evidence. Seventy-six exercising women with oligomenorrhea or amenorrhea were assigned either to increase energy intake or to maintain their usual habits for 12 months. The intervention group achieved an average increase of about 330 kcal/day, or 18 percent, without reducing exercise expenditure. This modest increase raised the likelihood of experiencing menses (hazard ratio 1.91, 95 percent confidence interval 1.27-2.89) and was accompanied by increases in body weight, body fat, and total triiodothyronine. Thirty-three of 76 participants completed the full 12-month intervention, which limits precision and generalizability. ([De Souza et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1093/humrep/deab149)) ([OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/36/8/2285/6308647 "Randomised controlled trial of the effects of increased energy intake on menstrual recovery in exercising women with menstrual disturbances: The REFUEL study"))

REFUEL was not an intermittent-fasting trial. It nevertheless supports a causal role for inadequate energy intake in menstrual dysfunction among exercising women with existing oligomenorrhea or amenorrhea. Increasing intake raised the likelihood of menses; the trial did not by itself establish restored ovulation, normal luteal function, fertility, or complete reproductive-axis normalization. Taken together with the fasting literature, this makes low energy availability a better-established human concern than a universal rule based on the number of hours since the last meal. ([De Souza et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1093/humrep/deab149); [Angelidi et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1210/endrev/bnae011); [Jirapak et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261467643))

A reasonable mechanistic interpretation is that fasting is most likely to become problematic when it is layered onto an existing deficit - for example, high exercise expenditure combined with dieting - or when a narrow window prevents the person from consuming enough total energy. The reviews cited here did not identify fasting trials stratified by measured baseline energy availability, so that inference still requires direct testing. ([Angelidi et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1210/endrev/bnae011); [Chang et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.isci.2024.109000); [Jirapak et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261467643); [Gordon et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2017-00131))

## Thyroid effects

Low energy availability is associated with changes in circulating thyroid hormones, particularly reductions in triiodothyronine as the body adapts to inadequate energy. These energy-conservation responses are not equivalent to evidence that time-restricted eating directly causes primary thyroid disease. ([Angelidi et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1210/endrev/bnae011); [De Souza et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1093/humrep/deab149))

The female time-restricted-eating trials were not designed to measure the incidence of clinical hypothyroidism, and the 2026 female hormone review concentrated mainly on reproductive and androgen outcomes. The evidence therefore does not support the broad statement that a standard daily fast “damages women’s thyroids.” It does support concern about thyroid-axis adaptation when fasting contributes to persistent under-fueling. ([Jirapak et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261467643); [Angelidi et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1210/endrev/bnae011)) ([Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/02601060261467643 "Impact of time-restricted eating on reproductive hormones and androgen markers in adult females: A systematic review - Aimee Jirapak, Emily Adams, Georgia M Black, Brooke L Devlin, 2026"))

## Sleep effects

A 2024 systematic review identified six randomized trials of time-restricted eating involving daily fasts of at least 14 hours and interventions lasting at least eight weeks. Most did not show a general deterioration in sleep. One trial reported a 0.5-hour reduction in self-reported sleep duration, two reported sleep-efficiency reductions of 2.0 and 2.7 percentage points, and one reported a 7-minute increase in self-reported sleep-onset latency. The review judged objective effects minimal overall, and the sleep-efficiency changes were not large enough to reclassify initially good sleepers as poor sleepers. Only one trial found a significant improvement in subjective sleep quality. ([Bohlman et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2024.1419811)) ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11322763/ "The effects of time-restricted eating on sleep in adults: A systematic review of randomized controlled trials"))

The trials were small, mostly involved adults with overweight or obesity, and only three used objective sleep measurements. One trial with several adverse findings combined time-restricted eating with calorie restriction. The studies were not designed to establish female-specific sleep effects. The claim that women uniquely develop sleep disruption at a fasting duration tolerated by men therefore remains unproven, while individual sleep worsening remains possible. ([Bohlman et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2024.1419811); [Garegnani et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD015610.pub2))

## Are eating windows under 10 hours unsafe for women?

The studies and reviews cited here do not validate a female-specific safety threshold at a 10-hour eating window. Existing studies have used 4-, 6-, 8-, and 10-hour eating windows in selected female populations without detecting a consistent adverse pattern in the endocrine outcomes measured. But these studies were generally small or involved obesity and PCOS, and menstrual or ovulatory outcomes were often incomplete. ([Jirapak et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261467643); [Kalam et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.23562); [Lin et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-024-01461-5); [Talebi et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.15801); [Zheng et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.isci.2025.113654); [Corapi et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-026-04316-7)) ([The Ohio State University](https://ohiostate.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/effect-of-time-restricted-eating-on-sex-hormone-levels-in-premeno/ "Effect of time-restricted eating on sex hormone levels in premenopausal and postmenopausal females"))

The evidence supports saying that **some selected women have completed eating-window protocols shorter than 10 hours without a consistent detected adverse signal in the outcomes measured**. It does not support saying that such windows have been proven safe for most women, nor that progressively shorter windows produce progressively greater benefits. ([Jirapak et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261467643); [Kalam et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.23562); [Corapi et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-026-04316-7))

A longer eating window may be practically preferable for someone who cannot meet energy and nutrient needs within a shorter one. That is a practical inference from the energy-availability evidence, not a rigorously established hormonal cutoff. ([Angelidi et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1210/endrev/bnae011); [Gordon et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2017-00131))

## How the competing claims hold up

- **“Women experience disruption at durations men tolerate.”** Not established. The reviews cited here do not identify adequately powered sex-comparison trials with reproductive outcomes. ([Garegnani et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD015610.pub2); [Jirapak et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261467643); [Altman and Bland, 2003](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.326.7382.219))

- **“Eating windows under 10 hours are well tolerated by most women.”** Too broad. Some selected women with obesity or PCOS have completed such protocols, but the evidence base does not represent most women or establish safety. ([Kalam et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.23562); [Corapi et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-026-04316-7)) ([The Ohio State University](https://ohiostate.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/effect-of-time-restricted-eating-on-sex-hormone-levels-in-premeno/ "Effect of time-restricted eating on sex hormone levels in premenopausal and postmenopausal females"))

- **“Fasting amplifies dysfunction in women who are already under-eating.”** This is a well-supported concern when fasting causes or compounds under-fueling, although the evidence comes primarily from low-energy-availability experiments and treatment trials rather than direct fasting-versus-nonfasting comparisons. ([Loucks and Thuma, 2003](https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2002-020369); [De Souza et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1093/humrep/deab149)) ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12519869/ "Luteinizing hormone pulsatility is disrupted at a threshold of energy availability in regularly menstruating women"))

- **“The harms are based only on rodents and anecdotes.”** Also too dismissive. The female-specific systematic review found the direct human evidence on ordinary time-restricted eating limited and inconclusive, while controlled human evidence shows reproductive and metabolic consequences of inadequate energy availability. That evidence supports concern when fasting causes or compounds under-fueling; it does not establish meal timing as an independent cause. ([Jirapak et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261467643); [Angelidi et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1210/endrev/bnae011); [De Souza et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1093/humrep/deab149))

## Quick reference

- The evidence reviewed here does not support a rule that every woman should fast for fewer hours than a man. ([Garegnani et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD015610.pub2); [Jirapak et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261467643))

- Female-specific reproductive evidence is small and concentrated in women with obesity or PCOS. ([Jirapak et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261467643)) ([Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/02601060261467643 "Impact of time-restricted eating on reproductive hormones and androgen markers in adult females: A systematic review - Aimee Jirapak, Emily Adams, Georgia M Black, Brooke L Devlin, 2026"))

- In one six-month PCOS trial, a 6-hour eating window and calorie restriction produced similar point estimates for weight loss, but the confidence interval did not establish equivalence; no serious adverse events were reported. ([Corapi et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-026-04316-7)) ([Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-026-04316-7 "Time-restricted eating for body weight management in women with polycystic ovary syndrome: A randomized controlled trial"))

- The studies and reviews cited here do not establish a female-specific safety boundary at a 10-hour eating window. ([Jirapak et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261467643); [Kalam et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.23562); [Corapi et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-026-04316-7))

- Low energy availability has direct human evidence for menstrual dysfunction and thyroid-axis adaptation; the female fasting review does not provide comparable evidence isolating fasting duration. ([Angelidi et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1210/endrev/bnae011); [De Souza et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1093/humrep/deab149); [Jirapak et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261467643))

- Intermittent fasting generally does not outperform conventional dietary advice for weight loss in adults with overweight or obesity over periods of up to 12 months. ([Garegnani et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD015610.pub2)) ([Cochrane](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD015610_intermittent-fasting-traditional-dietary-advice-or-no-treatment-which-works-better-help-adults "Intermittent fasting for adults with overweight or obesity"))


## Practical interpretation

- For weight loss in adults with overweight or obesity, intermittent fasting is an optional meal structure rather than a demonstrated necessity. The evidence reviewed here does not establish a benefit to pursuing the shortest tolerable eating window. ([Garegnani et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD015610.pub2); [Jirapak et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261467643)) ([Cochrane](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD015610_intermittent-fasting-traditional-dietary-advice-or-no-treatment-which-works-better-help-adults "Intermittent fasting for adults with overweight or obesity"))

- For women with obesity or PCOS, time-restricted eating may be a workable alternative to ordinary calorie restriction. Reproductive advantages have not been established, and weight-loss findings are mixed: most comparative trials have not detected superiority, while one 2025 trial reported greater loss when an 8-hour window was added to the same prescribed calorie range. Because actual intake was not experimentally matched, that result does not isolate a meal-timing effect. ([Talebi et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.15801); [Zheng et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.isci.2025.113654); [Corapi et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-026-04316-7)) ([Wiley Online Library](https://dom-pubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/dom.15801?af=R "The effects of time-restricted eating alone or in combination with probiotic supplementation in comparison with a calorie-restricted diet on endocrine and metabolic profiles in women with polycystic ovary syndrome: A randomized clinical trial"))

- For a woman with high exercise expenditure, existing menstrual irregularity, or chronically low intake, protecting energy availability is supported by more direct human evidence than meeting a particular fasting target. This conclusion is an inference from low-energy-availability studies; the reviews cited here did not identify fasting trials stratified by measured baseline energy availability. ([Angelidi et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1210/endrev/bnae011); [De Souza et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1093/humrep/deab149); [Jirapak et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261467643))

- New or worsening cycle irregularity, missed periods, persistent sleep deterioration, or declining exercise performance after adopting fasting should prompt reassessment of total intake and, when appropriate, medical evaluation rather than reliance on changing the fasting window alone. Menstrual disturbance has causes unrelated to diet and should not automatically be attributed to fasting. ([Gordon et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2017-00131); [Angelidi et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1210/endrev/bnae011); [Bohlman et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2024.1419811))


## Evidence gaps

The most useful next studies would be adequately powered RCTs in healthy, regularly cycling women, with prospectively tracked cycle length, confirmed ovulation, luteal-phase function, amenorrhea incidence, and symptoms as prespecified outcomes. ([Jirapak et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261467643); [Practice Committees of ASRM and SREI, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fertnstert.2021.02.010))

Trials should match total energy and nutrient intake between time-restricted and conventionally timed groups. Without energy matching, it remains difficult to distinguish an effect of the fasting clock from an effect of eating less. ([Chang et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.isci.2024.109000); [Garegnani et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD015610.pub2))

Subgroup analyses should stratify participants by baseline energy availability, exercise expenditure, body composition, menstrual history, reproductive stage, and the magnitude of weight loss. Trials should also directly test sex-by-treatment interactions rather than comparing whether each sex separately reached statistical significance. ([Garegnani et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD015610.pub2); [Altman and Bland, 2003](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.326.7382.219))

Longer studies are needed to assess thyroid function, bone turnover and density, sleep, fertility-related outcomes, adherence, and whether early versus late eating windows have different effects in women. These gaps are emphasized by the limited female hormone review, the sleep review, the REDs review, and the Cochrane review’s call for sex-disaggregated data. ([Jirapak et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261467643); [Bohlman et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2024.1419811); [Angelidi et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1210/endrev/bnae011); [Garegnani et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD015610.pub2)) ([Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/02601060261467643 "Impact of time-restricted eating on reproductive hormones and androgen markers in adult females: A systematic review"))
