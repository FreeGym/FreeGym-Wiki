# Your Thyroid Test Is Abnormal. But Is It Causing Your Symptoms?

Fatigue, difficulty concentrating, low mood, hair shedding, cold sensitivity, and weight concerns are not a thyroid fingerprint. They can occur alongside thyroid disease, but symptoms alone cannot reliably establish or exclude hypothyroidism. Iron deficiency, acute sleep restriction, low energy availability, perimenopause, and depression can also contribute to overlapping complaints, although the cited studies do not directly compare these conditions head-to-head. ([Canaris et al., 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10695693/); [Stadje et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27765009/); [Houston et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29626044/); [Wüst et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38759474/); [Oxfeldt et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37329147/); [Badawy et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38642901/))

The evidence reviewed here does not support a rule that thyroid should always be checked first or always checked last. It supports separating overt hypothyroidism from mild subclinical hypothyroidism and confirming borderline thyroid results. It does not establish whether common competing causes should be evaluated sequentially or in parallel. One laboratory audit found a thyroid-heavy testing pattern, while observational studies found frequent treatment at modest TSH elevations and, in one cohort, many levothyroxine starts that investigators classified as nonevidence-based. These studies do not directly measure whether clinicians attributed women's symptoms to thyroid disease instead of iron deficiency, inadequate sleep, or under-eating, and they do not show how often another condition was the true cause. ([Handjiev et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40085492/); [Ayala et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37656124/))

## Is there evidence of a thyroid-first testing pattern?

A 2025 report of a 2019 primary-care laboratory audit examined 16,889 requests mentioning fatigue or tiredness from 14,942 individuals. TSH was ordered in 80 percent of requests, while ferritin was ordered in 9.4 percent. Ten percent of TSH results and 26 percent of ferritin results were outside the laboratory reference range. Approximately 73.5 percent of the individuals were female, so the sample was female-predominant but not women-only. This shows a substantial test-ordering imbalance in that health system. It does not establish that TSH testing was inappropriate, that ferritin abnormalities represented iron deficiency or caused the fatigue, or that clinicians pursued thyroid disease before considering other diagnoses. ([Handjiev et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40085492/))

A multicenter study reviewed 977 adults started on levothyroxine at three United States academic medical centers between 2017 and 2020. Sixty-nine percent were women. Using prespecified criteria, the researchers classified 54 percent of the prescriptions as nonevidence-based, 31 percent as appropriate, and 12 percent as indeterminate. The most common reason for a questionable prescription was starting levothyroxine when TSH was below 10 mIU/L without adequate prior TSH or free thyroxine testing. Female sex was associated with modestly higher odds of a prescription classified as nonevidence-based, with an adjusted odds ratio of 1.3 and a 95 percent confidence interval of 1.0 to 1.7. This was a weak, borderline association and does not establish sex-based bias. ([Ayala et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37656124/))

That association does not prove that women were prescribed thyroid medication because their symptoms were dismissed, or that iron deficiency, sleep loss, or under-eating was the real explanation. It shows that prescriptions classified by the investigators as nonevidence-based were common in this cohort, but the reason for the weak association with female sex is unknown.

A separate analysis of 110,842 new levothyroxine users in the United States found that 73.2 percent were women and that the median pretreatment TSH was 5.3 mIU/L. Among the 58,706 people with both TSH and thyroid-hormone measurements available, 8.4 percent had overt hypothyroidism, 61.0 percent had subclinical hypothyroidism, and 30.5 percent had thyroid tests in the normal range. Overall, 59.6 percent started treatment with TSH between 4.5 and 9.9 mIU/L. The study documents how often treatment begins at modest TSH elevations, but it cannot determine whether treatment was appropriate for every individual or whether other causes of symptoms were overlooked. ([Brito et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34152370/))

## Symptoms do not establish that the thyroid is responsible

The Colorado Thyroid Disease Prevalence Study included 25,862 adults. Several symptoms were more common among people with hypothyroidism than among euthyroid participants, but individual symptoms had low sensitivity and limited positive predictive value. In practical terms, hypothyroidism can cause fatigue, cold intolerance, memory complaints, dry skin, and other symptoms, but no individual symptom had sufficient diagnostic accuracy to establish or exclude hypothyroidism. ([Canaris et al., 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10695693/))

A systematic review of primary-care patients presenting with tiredness found pooled prevalences of approximately 18.5 percent for depression, 2.8 percent for anemia, 0.6 percent for malignancy, and 4.3 percent for serious somatic disease in the higher-quality studies. The estimates varied substantially across studies, and the review was not specific to women. It evaluated anemia rather than iron deficiency without anemia, so it could not estimate the diagnostic yield of identifying depleted iron stores in women whose hemoglobin remained normal. ([Stadje et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27765009/))

These findings make symptom-based attribution unreliable in both directions. Symptoms cannot establish hypothyroidism, but they also cannot safely exclude it.

## Overt and subclinical hypothyroidism are not the same condition

Overt primary hypothyroidism generally involves elevated TSH together with free thyroxine below the reference range. Subclinical hypothyroidism is defined biochemically by elevated TSH with free thyroxine still within the reference range. The term does not mean that the result is imaginary or irrelevant. It means that the biochemical abnormality is mild and that its relationship to symptoms and treatment benefit is less certain. ([NICE, 2023](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng145/chapter/recommendations); [Feller et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30285179/); [Ayala et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37656124/))

This distinction matters because evidence against routinely treating mild subclinical hypothyroidism should not be used to dismiss overt hypothyroidism.

A prospective study enrolled 252 women with TSH between 4.5 and 10 mIU/L, 241 of whom completed five years of follow-up. During follow-up, 22.8 percent returned to normal thyroid function without treatment, 58.1 percent remained mildly subclinically hypothyroid, and 19 percent met the study's criteria for levothyroxine treatment. An initial TSH above 8 mIU/L predicted a greater likelihood of meeting treatment criteria, while a TSH of 8 mIU/L or lower without evidence of thyroiditis predicted normalization. ([Rosário et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26342200/))

A single mildly elevated TSH therefore should not automatically become a permanent diagnosis. Some women have persistent or progressive thyroid disease, while others normalize on repeat testing.

## Does levothyroxine relieve symptoms in mild subclinical hypothyroidism?

A 2018 systematic review and meta-analysis included 21 randomized trials with 2,192 nonpregnant adults who had subclinical hypothyroidism. Thyroid-hormone treatment lowered TSH into the reference range, but it did not produce a statistically significant improvement in general quality of life or thyroid-related symptoms. The standardized mean difference was -0.11 for general quality of life and 0.01 for thyroid-related symptoms, values consistent with little or no average benefit. ([Feller et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30285179/))

The evidence is not equally strong for every possible subgroup. Many participants had relatively mild symptoms, and only a minority of trials adequately reported baseline symptom burden. The meta-analysis therefore cannot exclude the possibility that patients with substantial baseline symptoms, who were underrepresented or incompletely characterized, may respond differently. The meta-analysis did not test whether a younger, women-only, or persistently abnormal biochemical subgroup benefited. It does show that a symptom benefit cannot be assumed merely because TSH is mildly elevated.

One small women-only trial randomized 40 women with mild subclinical hypothyroidism to thyroxine or placebo for six months, and 35 completed follow-up. It found no clinically relevant improvement in the measured outcomes, and anxiety scores worsened in the thyroxine group. The trial was small and is not definitive by itself, but its findings are consistent with the larger meta-analysis. ([Kong et al., 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11904108/))

These results illustrate a situation in which thyroid attribution should remain uncertain: a woman has nonspecific symptoms, one modestly elevated TSH, normal free thyroxine, and no evidence that correcting TSH improves the symptoms.

## Is a TSH of 2.5 to 4.5 early disease or normal variation?

There is no biologically universal TSH reference interval. A systematic review and meta-analysis found that estimated TSH reference limits vary with age, sex, iodine intake, geographical region, study size, assay method, and assay manufacturer. A cutoff that is appropriate for one population or laboratory is not automatically appropriate for every other population. ([Xing et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33662155/))

Higher TSH within the reference range can nevertheless predict future hypothyroidism. In the HUNT study, 10,083 women and 5,023 men with baseline TSH between 0.2 and 4.5 mIU/L were followed for approximately 11 years. Among women, the risk of later hypothyroidism increased from 1.1 percent at a baseline TSH of 0.5 to 1.4 mIU/L to 31.5 percent at 4.0 to 4.5 mIU/L. ([Åsvold et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22049180/))

That result makes the upper reference range useful for risk prediction. Evidence of autoimmune thyroiditis may further alter prognosis, but the HUNT study did not test treatment. It does not show that starting levothyroxine at a TSH of 2.5 to 4.5 improves fatigue, mood, weight, cognition, or long-term health. No trial cited here evaluated levothyroxine solely because a nonpregnant adult's TSH was between 2.5 and 4.5 mIU/L. Prognostic association and treatment benefit are different questions. ([Åsvold et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22049180/); [Rosário et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26342200/); [Feller et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30285179/))

This article addresses nonpregnant adults. Pregnancy and attempts to conceive fall outside the scope of this review, and the nonpregnant treatment evidence cited here should not be assumed to apply to those settings. ([Feller et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30285179/); [NICE, 2023](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng145/chapter/recommendations))

## Iron deficiency is a plausible and treatable competing cause

Iron-responsive fatigue can occur before hemoglobin falls into the anemic range. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 18 randomized trials involving 1,170 iron-deficient but nonanemic adults found that iron supplementation produced a modest reduction in self-reported fatigue. Across four trials with 714 participants, the standardized mean difference was -0.38, with no statistical heterogeneity. Iron did not significantly improve objective measures of physical capacity such as maximal oxygen consumption. ([Houston et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29626044/))

In one trial, 198 menstruating women aged 18 to 53 years who reported fatigue, had hemoglobin above 12 g/dL, and had ferritin below 50 micrograms/L were randomized to 80 mg of elemental iron or placebo for 12 weeks. Fatigue scores declined by 47.7 percent with iron and 28.8 percent with placebo, producing an adjusted between-group difference of 18.9 percentage points. Iron did not significantly improve depression, anxiety, or general quality of life. The ferritin value of 50 micrograms/L was an entry criterion for this trial, not a universally validated diagnostic cutoff for iron deficiency. ([Vaucher et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22777991/))

The trial was funded by Pierre Fabre Médicament, the manufacturer of the iron product. The sponsor was involved in study design, generated the randomization sequence, and participated in aspects of data collection and management. The academic investigators reported full access to the data and responsibility for the analysis. This financial relationship should be considered when interpreting the result. The broader meta-analysis included multiple trials, so the evidence for a modest fatigue benefit does not depend on this manufacturer-sponsored study alone. ([Vaucher et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22777991/); [Houston et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29626044/))

The practical implication is limited but important: a normal hemoglobin does not establish that iron status is adequate in a menstruating woman with fatigue. The intervention evidence is strongest for fatigue. It does not establish that iron supplementation reliably reverses hair shedding, weight change, or every other symptom attributed to thyroid disease.

## Acute sleep restriction increases sleepiness and impairs attention

A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated 44 studies in which sleep was restricted to between two and six hours for one night. Acute sleep restriction consistently increased subjective sleepiness and impaired sustained attention. ([Wüst et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38759474/))

These experiments show that acute insufficient sleep can increase sleepiness and impair concentration. Those effects can resemble complaints described as tiredness or brain fog, but the meta-analysis did not directly measure fatigue as a separate construct. It also does not determine how many women diagnosed with subclinical hypothyroidism have chronic insomnia, obstructive sleep apnea, restless legs, caregiving-related sleep disruption, or another sleep disorder. One night of experimental restriction is not equivalent to months of poor sleep.

Sleep history therefore matters, but sleep deprivation should not become another catch-all diagnosis. The cited meta-analysis does not establish how persistent sleepiness, snoring, witnessed breathing pauses, morning headaches, insomnia, restless legs, or chronically restricted sleep should be evaluated, because it studied one night of experimental sleep restriction rather than chronic sleep disorders. ([Wüst et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38759474/))

## Under-eating can alter thyroid physiology without proving thyroid-gland failure

Low energy availability occurs when dietary energy remaining after exercise is insufficient to support normal physiological function. It is defined relative to exercise energy expenditure and fat-free mass and should not be used as a synonym for every form of under-eating. Direct experimental evidence comes from small, short prospective studies in both sedentary and trained populations, not from the full population of women presenting to primary care. ([Areta et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33095376/))

In a controlled trial, 30 trained, eumenorrheic women completed 10 days of low energy availability at approximately 25 kcal/kg of fat-free mass per day or optimal energy availability at approximately 50 kcal/kg of fat-free mass per day. Food intake was provided and exercise was supervised. Low energy availability reduced muscle-protein synthesis, lean mass, nitrogen balance, free androgen index, resting metabolic rate, and circulating thyroid-hormone concentrations. ([Oxfeldt et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37329147/))

This supports an adaptive endocrine and metabolic response to low energy availability. It does not establish that low energy availability commonly causes the classic biochemical pattern of primary subclinical hypothyroidism, which requires elevated TSH with normal free thyroxine. The cited evidence supports a broader energy-conservation response, including lower thyroid-hormone availability, rather than proving that the thyroid gland itself is failing.

A review of the low-energy-availability literature noted that controlled prospective interventions are generally short and that the evidence base remains limited relative to the breadth of claims made about the condition. ([Areta et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33095376/))

Low energy availability may be considered when there is a clear mismatch between dietary energy intake and exercise energy expenditure. The cited studies do not validate intentional weight loss, rapidly increased training, dietary restriction, declining performance, recurrent injury, menstrual disturbance, or persistent preoccupation with limiting food as a primary-care diagnostic checklist, and they do not establish how often low energy availability coexists with diagnosed thyroid disease. ([Oxfeldt et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37329147/); [Areta et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33095376/))

## Perimenopause and depression belong in the same differential

A 2024 systematic review included 17 prospective cohort studies with 16,061 women. In the meta-analysis of seven studies with 9,141 participants, women in perimenopause had 1.40 times the odds of depressive symptoms or a depressive diagnosis compared with premenopausal women. The analysis did not find the same increase when postmenopausal women were compared with premenopausal women. These prospective cohort data show an association and do not prove that menopausal stage caused each episode. ([Badawy et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38642901/))

Cognitive changes may also occur during the menopausal transition. In the Study of Women's Health Across the Nation, 2,362 women were followed for four years. Perimenopause was associated with a temporary reduction in the improvement normally seen with repeated cognitive testing, particularly for learning-related measures, followed by recovery after menopause. The effect was subtle and does not imply that all midlife cognitive complaints are caused by menopause. ([Greendale et al., 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19470968/))

These findings support considering perimenopause and depression alongside thyroid disease rather than treating them as mutually exclusive explanations. The cited menopause studies do not quantify how often sleep disruption, iron loss from abnormal uterine bleeding, and thyroid autoimmunity coexist in the same woman. ([Badawy et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38642901/); [Greendale et al., 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19470968/))

## Is a strict sequential workup supported?

The available evidence does not validate a rigid sequence in which iron, sleep, energy intake, stress, and mood must all be corrected before thyroid testing is allowed. Nor does it establish that a parallel strategy is superior. Requiring every competing cause to be corrected before thyroid testing would, by design, postpone thyroid evaluation, but no comparative trial here measured whether that changes outcomes.

No comparative diagnostic-strategy trial was identified among the studies reviewed here. The major studies examine test-ordering patterns, levothyroxine prescribing, treatment response, or individual competing causes. They do not randomize women with overlapping symptoms to thyroid-first, iron-first, sleep-first, or parallel diagnostic strategies. ([Handjiev et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40085492/); [Ayala et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37656124/))

A practical approach, while not prospectively validated, is targeted and non-rigid:

- In nonpregnant adults without suspected pituitary disease, initial testing can begin with TSH, with free thyroxine measured from the same sample if TSH is above the reference range. Measure TSH and free thyroxine together initially when central thyroid dysfunction is possible or when the applicable local protocol requires it. If TSH is only mildly elevated and free thyroxine is normal, confirm persistence before converting the result into a permanent diagnosis or open-ended treatment. ([NICE, 2023](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng145/chapter/recommendations); [Rosário et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26342200/))

- Assess iron status in context, particularly in menstruating women with fatigue despite normal hemoglobin. The cited treatment studies did not validate heavy or prolonged bleeding, recent pregnancy, blood donation, restricted intake, or gastrointestinal symptoms as a screening checklist. The evidence supports a modest fatigue benefit from correcting documented iron deficiency without anemia, not indiscriminate iron supplementation. ([Houston et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29626044/); [Vaucher et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22777991/))

- Ask directly about sleep duration and sleep quality rather than relying only on a generic question such as "Are you sleeping well?" The cited meta-analysis establishes the effects of acute sleep restriction, not the diagnostic yield of questions about insomnia, snoring, breathing pauses, restless legs, shift work, or caregiving disruption. ([Wüst et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38759474/))

- Recent weight loss, dietary restriction, training volume, menstrual function, and recovery may be reviewed when low energy availability is being considered, but current controlled evidence comes from small, short studies, including one trial in trained women, and does not validate this list as a general primary-care screening algorithm. ([Oxfeldt et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37329147/); [Areta et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33095376/))

- Consider depressive symptoms and menopausal stage without assuming that either is the explanation merely because thyroid tests are normal. ([Stadje et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27765009/); [Badawy et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38642901/))

- When a time-limited levothyroxine trial is chosen for persistent mild subclinical hypothyroidism, define which symptoms are expected to improve and reassess them after TSH has normalized. NICE advises considering discontinuation if symptoms persist once TSH is within the reference range. Continuing or escalating treatment despite no symptom response should prompt reassessment rather than be assumed to address the cause. ([NICE, 2023](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng145/chapter/recommendations); [Feller et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30285179/))


This is an evidence-informed synthesis, not a prospectively validated universal diagnostic algorithm.

## What the evidence still cannot answer

No study among those reviewed here directly measured the prevalence of iron deficiency, chronic sleep disturbance, low energy availability, perimenopause-related symptoms, and depression among women already diagnosed with subclinical hypothyroidism.

No trial was identified in the evidence reviewed here that randomized such women to levothyroxine, iron repletion, sleep treatment, increased energy intake, depression treatment, menopause-directed treatment, or combinations based on a comprehensive initial evaluation.

The cited levothyroxine evidence is poorly suited to identifying small subgroups based on baseline symptom severity because only a minority of trials adequately reported it and the meta-analysis did not conduct subgroup analyses. The evidence reviewed here does not establish how consistently menstrual status, iron stores, sleep disorders, energy availability, thyroid autoantibodies, or menopausal stage were measured across all underlying trials. Consequently, the evidence cannot support the claim that "many" or "most" women taking thyroid medication would have recovered simply by correcting iron, sleep, or food intake. It supports the narrower conclusion that levothyroxine has little average symptomatic benefit in mild subclinical hypothyroidism and that benefit in patients with substantial baseline symptoms remains uncertain. ([Feller et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30285179/); [Kong et al., 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11904108/))

## Quick reference values

- In the 2019 primary-care audit reported in 2025, TSH was ordered in 80 percent of fatigue-related requests and ferritin in 9.4 percent. Results were outside the laboratory range in 10 percent of TSH tests and 26 percent of ferritin tests. ([Handjiev et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40085492/))

- New levothyroxine starts at three academic centers: 54 percent classified by the investigators as nonevidence-based; female sex adjusted odds ratio 1.3. ([Ayala et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37656124/))

- Levothyroxine in subclinical hypothyroidism: 21 randomized trials, 2,192 adults; no meaningful average improvement in quality of life or thyroid-related symptoms. ([Feller et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30285179/))

- Five-year follow-up of women with TSH 4.5 to 10 mIU/L: 22.8 percent normalized, 58.1 percent remained mildly subclinically hypothyroid, and 19 percent met the study's treatment criteria. ([Rosário et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26342200/))

- Women in the HUNT cohort: future hypothyroidism occurred in 1.1 percent with baseline TSH 0.5 to 1.4 mIU/L and 31.5 percent with TSH 4.0 to 4.5 mIU/L over approximately 11 years. ([Åsvold et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22049180/))

- Iron-deficient adults without anemia: iron produced a modest reduction in subjective fatigue, standardized mean difference -0.38, but did not improve objective physical capacity. ([Houston et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29626044/))

- Perimenopause: odds ratio 1.40 for depressive symptoms or diagnosis compared with premenopause in a prospective-cohort meta-analysis. ([Badawy et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38642901/))


## Practical interpretation

- Elevated TSH together with low free thyroxine is not the situation in which thyroid evaluation should be postponed while every other possibility is corrected. ([NICE, 2023](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng145/chapter/recommendations))

- Mildly elevated TSH with normal free thyroxine should usually be interpreted as a result requiring confirmation and context, not immediate proof that the thyroid caused every symptom. ([Rosário et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26342200/); [NICE, 2023](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng145/chapter/recommendations))

- A TSH of 2.5 to 4.5 may predict greater future thyroid risk, particularly near the upper end, but prediction alone does not show that immediate medication improves current symptoms. No treatment trial cited here enrolled nonpregnant adults solely because their TSH was within this range. ([Åsvold et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22049180/); [Feller et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30285179/))

- Iron status, sleep, energy intake, menstrual history, menopausal stage, mood, and thyroid function may all be relevant to the same presentation. No comparative diagnostic-strategy trial identified here establishes that they must be assessed in a rigid order or that a parallel strategy produces better outcomes.

- Normal hemoglobin does not rule out iron-responsive fatigue, but low or borderline ferritin does not prove that every symptom is caused by iron deficiency. ([Houston et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29626044/))

- Persistent symptoms after TSH normalization should reopen the differential diagnosis rather than automatically trigger a higher levothyroxine dose. For mild subclinical hypothyroidism, NICE advises considering discontinuation when symptoms persist despite TSH being within the reference range. ([NICE, 2023](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng145/chapter/recommendations); [Feller et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30285179/))

- Multiple causes may remain possible. Identifying one abnormal result does not establish that it explains the full symptom pattern.
