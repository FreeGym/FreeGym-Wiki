# Do Hormonal Contraceptives Affect Strength and Endurance Training Adaptations?

The best current answer is that hormonal contraceptives have not been shown to blunt training adaptations consistently or by an amount that is clearly important in practice. For resistance training, the pooled effects of oral contraceptive use on strength and muscle growth are close to zero. For endurance training, the evidence is much thinner: one small sprint-interval cohort found smaller improvements in VO₂peak and peak cardiac output, but comparable improvements in peak power and time-to-fatigue. Other longitudinal cohorts have not found worse functional improvement. ([Nolan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01911-3); [Nolan et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1123/wspaj.2025-0137))

This conclusion applies primarily to oral contraceptive pills, predominantly combined pills. There is insufficient method-specific evidence for hormonal IUDs, implants, progestin-only pills, injections, patches, or rings. Therefore, evidence about oral pills should not be presented as evidence about hormonal contraception as one homogeneous category. ([Nolan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01911-3); [Nolan et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1123/wspaj.2025-0137))

## Performance is not the same as training adaptation

An acute performance study asks whether someone runs, lifts, jumps, or reaches a different VO₂max on a particular day or pill-cycle phase. A training-adaptation study asks whether performance or physiology changes differently after several weeks or months of matched training. ([Elliott-Sale et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-020-01317-5); [Elliott-Sale et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01435-8))

A 2020 systematic review and meta-analysis included 42 exercise-performance studies and 590 participants; most comparisons were small and nonrandomized. Oral contraceptive users may have performed slightly worse than naturally menstruating women on average, but the estimated difference was trivial and highly variable. Performance was also essentially unchanged between active-pill and withdrawal phases. Most included studies were rated moderate, low, or very low quality. Because this review concerned performance tests rather than responses to matched multiweek training, it does not establish whether oral contraceptives alter training adaptation. ([Elliott-Sale et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-020-01317-5))

## Resistance training: the average effect is close to zero

The most direct synthesis is a 2024 systematic review and multilevel meta-analysis of eight articles, 54 effects, and 325 participants. The interventions lasted 8-16 weeks, with a weighted average of 11.6 weeks. Every included study examined oral contraceptive pills rather than other hormonal methods. ([Nolan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01911-3))

The standardized difference in improvement between pill users and nonusers was:

- Hypertrophy: 0.01, 95% CI -0.11 to 0.13.

- Strength: 0.10, 95% CI -0.08 to 0.28.

- Power: -0.04, 95% CI -0.93 to 0.84. ([Nolan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01911-3))


The near-zero point estimates suggest little average difference in these data, but they do not prove equivalence or remove the confounding inherent in mostly nonrandomized user-versus-nonuser comparisons. The hypertrophy and strength estimates provide no evidence of an average disadvantage for pill users over approximately 12 weeks. The power estimate is much less informative because only three studies reported power outcomes and the confidence interval is extremely wide. ([Nolan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01911-3))

This pooled result does not mean every individual study was neutral. One 10-week cohort of 72 women reported lean-mass gains of 1.0 kg in oral contraceptive users and 1.6 kg in nonusers. However, contraceptive exposure was not randomized, different pill formulations were combined, and body composition was estimated using hydrostatic weighing rather than direct regional muscle imaging. This study was already included in the neutral meta-analysis. ([Riechman and Lee, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000004059); [Nolan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01911-3))

Two small studies published in 2025 found results in the opposite direction. Engstad and colleagues reported greater arm lean-mass and vastus lateralis growth in 15 pill users than in 17 nonusers, while Holm and colleagues reported greater arm lean-tissue and selected regional muscle growth in 14 users of combined levonorgestrel-containing pills than in 12 nonusers. Neither study found greater strength gains in pill users. The first pill group slept approximately 42 minutes longer per day, while the second completed approximately two more training sessions, leaving plausible nonhormonal explanations for some of the hypertrophy differences. ([Engstad et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.70052); [Holm et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.70189))

A broader 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis included 40 retrospective clinical studies and was not restricted to training adaptation. It detected no statistically significant pooled difference in its examined muscle-size or strength outcomes, but heterogeneity was high and the authors cautioned that conclusions were difficult. ([Gilmer et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00146.2026))

The resistance-training evidence therefore does not support the directional claim that oral contraceptives generally blunt muscle or strength gains. The clearest residual uncertainty concerns small formulation-specific or regional hypertrophy effects and whether the mostly short-term findings generalize to longer interventions or highly trained athletes. ([Nolan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01911-3); [Engstad et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.70052); [Holm et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.70189); [Flood et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2024.2368762))

## Endurance training: the evidence is much thinner

A 2026 systematic review screened 5,513 records but found only three qualifying papers from two independent datasets, totaling 77 women, 47 of whom used hormonal contraception. Only oral pills and depot contraceptive injection were represented. Both training interventions improved aerobic or functional performance, and the improvements were generally comparable between hormonal contraceptive users and naturally menstruating women. The review concluded that the evidence was too sparse and methodologically limited for evidence-based recommendations. ([Nolan et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1123/wspaj.2025-0137))

The main potentially negative finding came from a four-week sprint-interval study. Twenty-five users of low-dose monophasic combined oral contraceptives and 16 naturally menstruating women completed 12 cycling sessions. VO₂peak increased by 8.5 percent in pill users and 13.0 percent in naturally menstruating women. Peak cardiac output increased by 4.0 percent and 16.1 percent, respectively. However, peak power increased almost identically, by 13.1 percent and 13.8 percent. Four weeks after training stopped, the pill group retained more of its VO₂peak improvement. ([Schaumberg et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000001171))

A 2020 paper from a substantially overlapping sprint-interval cohort found comparable improvements in time-to-fatigue. It used the same 25 combined-pill users but included 22 naturally menstruating participants rather than the 16 analyzed in the 2017 paper, because participants with potential luteal-phase deficiency were included. Pulmonary oxygen-uptake kinetics improved within the naturally menstruating group but not within pill users, although the between-group tests were inconsistent across exercise intensities. Pill users showed greater improvement in peripheral muscle oxygenation. These papers report different outcomes from substantially overlapping participants and should not be treated as independent replications. ([Schaumberg et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2020.00629))

The other independent dataset included 36 previously inactive women completing 7-8 weeks of combined heat acclimation and physical training. It included 15 oral contraceptive users, seven depot medroxyprogesterone acetate users, and 14 ovulatory nonusers. VO₂max and 4.6-km running performance improved without significant differences between groups, but the injection subgroup was far too small to establish a method-specific effect. ([Armstrong et al., 2005](https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpendo.00434.2004))

A larger observational study enrolled 450 British Army recruits and measured them in weeks 1 and 13 of a 14-week basic training course that included endurance, strength, circuits, loaded marching, and field training. Running, lifting, power, and body-composition outcomes improved without group-by-training differences among nonusers, combined-pill users, and a pooled progestin-only group. However, only 260 participants completed the final testing, and the progestin-only group combined progestin-only pills, implants, and injections. This is inconsistent with a large functional disadvantage among completers but cannot isolate a particular method or pure endurance-training response. ([O'Leary et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000003588))

A small 2026 study of approximately eight weeks of continuous running and walking found no significant group-by-training interaction for VO₂peak in enrolled groups of 18 eumenorrheic women and eight combined-pill users, although complete phase-specific VO₂peak pairs were available for only 13 or 16 and six or seven participants, respectively. It had no parallel untrained comparison group and was far too small to exclude modest differences. ([Salmi et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.14814/phy2.70857))

The most defensible conclusion is therefore that no endurance-adaptation penalty has been established. One small cohort suggests that oral contraceptives might alter central and peripheral components of aerobic adaptation without meaningfully changing short-term functional improvement. That finding needs independent replication. ([Nolan et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1123/wspaj.2025-0137); [Schaumberg et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000001171); [Schaumberg et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2020.00629))

## Different formulations may matter, but this has not been demonstrated

Hormonal contraceptive methods differ in hormone content, delivery route, regimen, and degree of ovarian suppression. Oral contraceptive pills also differ in estrogen type and dose and in progestin type and activity. Collapsing all methods into one exposure is therefore biologically and methodologically questionable. It does not follow, however, that meaningful formulation-specific training effects have already been demonstrated. ([Elliott-Sale et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.contraception.2012.11.023); [Elliott-Sale et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01435-8); [Nolan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01911-3); [Nolan et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1123/wspaj.2025-0137))

|Method|Direct training-adaptation evidence|Current interpretation|
|---|---|---|
|Oral contraceptive pills, predominantly combined|All resistance studies; combined-pill groups in sprint-interval, heat-acclimation, military-training, and continuous-running cohorts|Average resistance adaptations appear similar, but individual formulations have not been compared adequately.|
|Depot medroxyprogesterone injection|Seven women in one aerobic-training cohort; pooled with other progestin methods in a military cohort|No route-specific conclusion is possible.|
|Progestin-only pills and implants|Pooled together with injections in the military cohort|Their individual effects cannot be isolated.|
|Hormonal IUDs|No qualifying resistance or aerobic intervention in the current adaptation reviews|No adaptation conclusion is possible.|
|Patch and vaginal ring|No qualifying method-specific adaptation intervention|Oral-pill findings should not automatically be transferred to these routes.|

([Nolan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01911-3); [Nolan et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1123/wspaj.2025-0137); [Armstrong et al., 2005](https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpendo.00434.2004); [O'Leary et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000003588); [Salmi et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.14814/phy2.70857))

There are formulation-specific signals, but they are weak. A 2003 study reported smaller strength gains in users of an antiandrogenic cyproterone-containing pill than in levonorgestrel-pill users. The participants were already using their respective formulations before recruitment, exposure was not randomized, there was no nonuser group, and unmeasured characteristics associated with formulation use could have influenced the comparison. ([Ružić et al., 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12590431/))

A 2019 study found a nonsignificant trend toward greater quadriceps growth in users of low-androgenic third-generation pills. A post hoc analysis suggested that the seven women taking 30 micrograms of ethinyl estradiol drove the result, but each dose subgroup contained only seven participants, and the 20-microgram subgroup included both gestodene and desogestrel while the 30-microgram subgroup contained only gestodene. ([Dalgaard et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2019.00824))

The recent combined-pill hypertrophy findings also suggest that formulation could matter, but their nonrandomized designs and absence of a corresponding strength advantage prevent a causal conclusion. Formulation-specific effects are biologically plausible and inadequately studied, not established. ([Engstad et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.70052); [Holm et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.70189))

## Mechanistic studies do not provide a simple answer

Combined pills suppress ovulation and lower endogenous ovarian hormone concentrations while supplying exogenous estrogen and progestin. A meta-analysis of 42 studies found lower total and free testosterone and higher sex hormone-binding globulin in combined-pill users. These endocrine changes provide a plausible mechanism, but they are not evidence that muscle or endurance adaptations are impaired. That meta-analysis was funded by Pantarhei Bioscience, and its author disclosures included company employment, executive status, and equity ownership. Its findings should be interpreted with those financial relationships in mind. ([Zimmerman et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1093/humupd/dmt038))

A small stable-isotope study found lower fed-state myofibrillar protein synthesis in 11 oral contraceptive users than in 12 nonusers, with results that appeared to differ between the two pill formulations. This was an acute surrogate study and cannot predict muscle growth over months. ([Hansen et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0838.2009.01002.x))

A 2025 randomized crossover study in 12 second-generation pill users found no difference in integrated muscle protein synthesis or whole-body myofibrillar proteolysis between active-pill and inactive-pill phases. This study addressed pill phase within users, not pill use versus nonuse. ([Colenso-Semple et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00035.2025))

Similar caution applies to tendon mechanisms. An acute study reported lower tendon collagen synthesis and a reduced muscle-connective-tissue collagen response to exercise in pill users, but a 2025 longitudinal analysis of the same 15 combined-pill users and 17 nonusers studied by Engstad found no significant group-by-training interaction for patellar-tendon cross-sectional area, stiffness, or shear-wave velocity after approximately 12 weeks of resistance training. Acute collagen markers therefore do not establish impaired chronic tendon adaptation. ([Hansen et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.90933.2008); [Vesterhus et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.19581); [Engstad et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.70052))

Overall, the mechanistic evidence is internally mixed. ([Zimmerman et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1093/humupd/dmt038); [Hansen et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0838.2009.01002.x); [Colenso-Semple et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00035.2025); [Hansen et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.90933.2008); [Vesterhus et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.19581)) Direct changes in strength, muscle size, power, endurance performance, and tendon properties should carry more weight than isolated short-term hormone or protein-turnover measurements.

## Hormonal contraceptive users can confound cycle-phase research

This methodological claim is substantially correct. Users of conventional cyclic combined-pill regimens have active-pill days and inactive-pill or pill-free days, not natural follicular, ovulatory, and luteal phases. The bleeding during a conventional pill-free interval is withdrawal bleeding and should not be treated as evidence of a natural early-follicular hormonal state. Other methods, particularly hormonal IUDs, implants, and injections, create different combinations of exogenous exposure, ovarian activity, and bleeding patterns. ([Elliott-Sale et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-020-01317-5); [Elliott-Sale et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01435-8))

A study of 95 oral contraceptive users identified two broad pill types but 11 different brands, with substantial variation in endogenous estradiol and progesterone. The authors cautioned against treating oral contraceptive users as a homogeneous experimental group or assuming that oral-contraceptive users are hormonally equivalent to naturally menstruating participants in the early-follicular phase. ([Elliott-Sale et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.contraception.2012.11.023))

A 2024 audit identified 61 exercise-performance studies containing 68 hormonal-contraceptive exposures. Monophasic combined oral pills accounted for approximately 60 percent of exposures, and no study included world-class athletes. This illustrates both the overrepresentation of one method and the limited applicability of the evidence to elite competition. ([Flood et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2024.2368762))

Hormonal contraceptive use can therefore introduce exposure or cycle-phase misclassification when users are combined with naturally cycling participants, grouped together regardless of method, or assigned natural-cycle phases from bleeding dates. It is not an unavoidable confounder if researchers separate methods, report exact formulations and regimens, and verify the endocrine state of comparison participants. ([Elliott-Sale et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01435-8))

## What switching studies tell us

A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis found no significant average within-person change in VO₂max after oral contraceptive initiation: the pooled mean difference was -0.84 mL/kg/min, with a 95 percent confidence interval from -2.38 to 0.70. The estimate combined six studies totaling 71 women: four nonrandomized pre-post or crossover studies and the intervention arms, but not the placebo comparisons, from two randomized trials. Statistical heterogeneity was low, but the studies varied in design, pill formulation, and duration of use. More importantly, they measured whether starting a pill changed VO₂max, not whether participants subsequently adapted less to the same training. ([Schumpf et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.heliyon.2023.e17049))

Neither the resistance nor aerobic adaptation review identified a trial that randomized contraceptive initiation, discontinuation, or switching during a controlled training intervention. Existing-user comparisons remain vulnerable to self-selection, formulation choice, reasons for contraceptive use, previous menstrual health, symptoms, sleep, diet, and training adherence. ([Nolan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01911-3); [Nolan et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1123/wspaj.2025-0137))

## Claim-by-claim assessment

- **“Hormonal contraceptives blunt strength or muscle gains slightly.”** Not supported as a general claim. Oral-pill meta-analytic estimates are near zero, and newer studies include both neutral and greater regional hypertrophy findings. ([Nolan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01911-3); [Engstad et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.70052); [Holm et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.70189))

- **“Hormonal contraceptives blunt endurance adaptations.”** Unproven. One small sprint cohort reported smaller central cardiovascular adaptations, but functional improvement was preserved and other longitudinal data are neutral. The total evidence remains too sparse for confidence. ([Schaumberg et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000001171); [Nolan et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1123/wspaj.2025-0137))

- **“Any effect is too small to matter outside elite sport.”** Broadly consistent with the resistance evidence, but not directly tested. The literature has not compared the effect size of contraception with sleep, nutrition, progressive overload, or training adherence, and world-class athletes are essentially absent. ([Nolan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01911-3); [Flood et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2024.2368762))

- **“Different formulations produce different effects.”** Plausible but not established. The field lacks adequately powered, randomized head-to-head comparisons by estrogen dose, progestin molecule, pill regimen, or route. ([Nolan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01911-3); [Nolan et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1123/wspaj.2025-0137))

- **“Pooling all hormonal contraceptive users obscures real signals.”** Strongly supported as a methodological criticism. Whether clinically important method-specific signals actually exist remains unanswered. ([Elliott-Sale et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.contraception.2012.11.023); [Flood et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2024.2368762))

- **“Contraceptive use confounds cycle-phase research.”** Correct when exposure is ignored or misclassified. Combined-pill users should not be assigned natural menstrual phases, and nonoral methods require method-specific characterization. ([Elliott-Sale et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01435-8))


## Practical interpretation

- Current evidence provides no performance-based reason to start, stop, or switch an otherwise suitable oral contraceptive pill solely to improve strength or hypertrophy adaptations. ([Nolan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01911-3))

- An endurance penalty has not been established, but the evidence is too limited to exclude a small effect on VO₂peak or central cardiovascular adaptation in some users. ([Nolan et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1123/wspaj.2025-0137))

- Individual monitoring remains reasonable because group averages do not capture changes in symptoms, recovery, sleep, bleeding, training availability, or personal response after starting or switching a method. The current adaptation studies were not designed to distinguish these indirect pathways from direct hormonal effects. ([Elliott-Sale et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-020-01317-5); [Nolan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01911-3))

- Future studies should report the exact estrogen type and dose, progestin, route, regimen, duration of use, active or inactive exposure phase, adherence, and reason for use. They should include objectively verified naturally cycling controls, longer interventions, trained athletes, and sufficiently powered method-by-training interactions. ([Elliott-Sale et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01435-8); [Nolan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01911-3); [Nolan et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1123/wspaj.2025-0137)) A reasonable next step would be prospective new-user or switching studies among participants who were already seeking contraception.


## Funding and conflict considerations

No contraceptive-manufacturer sponsorship was declared in the 2024 resistance meta-analysis or the two 2025 resistance-training cohorts discussed above. The meta-analysis was funded through an Irish Research Council grant; Engstad reported no specific funding, and Holm reported no funding. Nolan reported no direct conflicts of interest, and the two cohorts declared none. ([Nolan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01911-3); [Engstad et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.70052); [Holm et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.70189))

The Armstrong study was supported by the US Army, while the military-training cohort was funded by the UK Ministry of Defence and included Army or Ministry-affiliated authors. These are institutional interests relevant to heat and military performance, not contraceptive-manufacturer interests. ([Armstrong et al., 2005](https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpendo.00434.2004); [O'Leary et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000003588))

The 2026 running study received public and foundation funding plus support from Firstbeat Analytics, a sports-analytics company; the authors declared no conflict of interest. ([Salmi et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.14814/phy2.70857))

The Zimmerman endocrine meta-analysis was funded by Pantarhei Bioscience and included company employment, executive, and equity relationships. It is therefore appropriate to use it as supporting endocrine evidence, not as the sole basis for a training-performance conclusion. ([Zimmerman et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1093/humupd/dmt038))
