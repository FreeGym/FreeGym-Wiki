# Do Hormonal Contraceptives Affect Strength and Endurance Training Adaptations?

Most available resistance-training research concerns oral contraceptive pills. Across those studies, pill use has not consistently reduced strength or muscle growth. Endurance adaptations are less certain: the dedicated systematic review found only two independent datasets, and one suggested smaller improvements in central aerobic physiology, but the evidence was insufficient to establish a general performance penalty. Most importantly, findings about particular oral contraceptives should not be extended automatically to hormonal IUDs, implants, injections, or every pill formulation. ([Nolan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01911-3); [Nolan et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1123/wspaj.2025-0137); [Elliott-Sale et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-026-02442-3)).

## Performance is not the same as training adaptation

Three questions need to be separated: whether contraceptive users perform differently at a particular assessment, whether they improve differently after a training programme, and whether starting or stopping contraception changes performance within the same person. These questions are addressed by different study designs, so evidence answering one does not necessarily answer the others. ([Elliott-Sale et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-020-01317-5); [Nolan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01911-3); [Lebrun et al., 2003](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsm.37.4.315); [Ekenros et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1097/JSM.0b013e3182625a51)).

For example, a widely cited 2020 systematic review included 42 studies and found that oral contraceptive use was most likely associated with a trivial average difference in exercise performance, with considerable variation between studies. However, that review was principally about performance under different hormonal conditions, not the accumulated gains from months of training. It cannot establish that contraceptives reduce muscle growth or endurance adaptations by a particular percentage. ([Elliott-Sale et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-020-01317-5)).

## Strength and muscle growth: no consistent impairment

The most directly relevant resistance-training meta-analysis included eight articles, 54 effect estimates, 325 participants, and interventions lasting approximately 8-16 weeks. Two articles drew on the same participants and intervention and were treated as one study in the analysis. Although its title referred broadly to hormonal contraception, every included article examined oral contraceptive pills. The pooled estimates were close to zero for hypertrophy and strength. ([Nolan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01911-3)).

|Training outcome|Standardized between-group difference|95% confidence interval|
|---|---|---|
|Muscle hypertrophy|0.01|-0.11 to 0.13|
|Strength|0.10|-0.08 to 0.28|
|Power|-0.04|-0.93 to 0.84|

These are standardized effect sizes, not percentage changes. The findings do not support a consistent average impairment of muscle growth or strength in the studied pill users. The much wider interval for power leaves considerably more uncertainty. However, the meta-analysis was not an equivalence or non-inferiority analysis. Nonsignificant differences do not prove that the true effects are exactly zero or establish equivalence across every formulation, population, or training duration. ([Nolan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01911-3)).

Individual studies nevertheless disagree. Riechman and Lee studied 72 women completing ten weeks of resistance training and reported smaller lean-mass gains in oral contraceptive users: approximately 1.0 kg versus 1.6 kg in nonusers. This was a comparison of existing contraceptive users and nonusers, not randomized assignment to contraception; the outcome was whole-body lean mass estimated through hydrostatic weighing, rather than direct measurement of skeletal muscle growth. ([Riechman and Lee, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000004059)).

Newer studies have sometimes found the opposite. Engstad and colleagues compared 15 habitual pill users with 17 nonusers during approximately 12 weeks of training. Vastus lateralis cross-sectional area increased by about 10.0% versus 5.3%, respectively, but strength gains did not differ. Contraceptive use was not randomized. Pill users also slept approximately 42 minutes longer per day, a potential confounder, not proof that sleep explained the result. ([Engstad et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.70052)).

Another 12-week study compared 14 second-generation pill users with 12 nonusers. Pill users showed greater hypertrophy in three of seven measured muscles, but again no greater strength gains. Contraceptive use was not randomized. The three muscle-specific interaction p-values were 0.032-0.036, no family-wise multiplicity correction across the seven muscle tests was reported, and pill users completed two more sessions on average (23 versus 21). These findings are exploratory. They challenge a blanket “the pill blunts gains” claim, but do not establish that taking a pill enhances training responses. ([Holm et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.70189)).

A broader 2026 systematic review of 40 clinical studies likewise detected no pooled effects on the muscle outcomes it examined. However, the authors emphasized substantial heterogeneity and limitations in study quality. These were not 40 randomized training trials, and failure to detect an effect was not treated as definitive proof of no effect. ([Gilmer et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00146.2026)).

## Endurance adaptations: a much thinner evidence base

A 2026 systematic review specifically addressing aerobic training found just three eligible articles representing **two independent datasets and 77 participants**. Both contraceptive users and naturally menstruating women improved, with performance outcomes generally comparable. One dataset showed smaller central cardiovascular adaptations in pill users. With only two independent datasets, this is very low-certainty evidence. The review concluded that the evidence was insufficient for firm recommendations. ([Nolan et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1123/wspaj.2025-0137)).

The clearest concerning finding comes from a four-week sprint-interval training study. Among 25 oral contraceptive users and 16 naturally cycling women, VO₂peak increased by approximately **8.5% versus 13.0%**. However, peak cycling power improved similarly: approximately **13.1% versus 13.8%**. Contraceptive use itself was not randomized. ([Schaumberg et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000001171)).

That distinction matters. A smaller improvement in a physiological measurement is not automatically a smaller improvement in every performance outcome. Nor does the difference between those VO₂peak gains establish that pill users become 4.5% slower in races. The study provides a signal worth investigating, not a universal endurance penalty. ([Schaumberg et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000001171)).

## What larger longitudinal studies add

A prospective military-training study provides evidence beyond small laboratory interventions. It assessed 450 women initially, with 260 available at the 13-week follow-up. Changes in running performance, lifting performance, leg power, and lean mass did not differ significantly between nonusers, combined-pill users, and progestin-only contraceptive users. ([O’Leary et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000003588)).

However, the progestin-only category pooled pills, injections, and implants; hormonal IUD users were excluded. Contraception was not randomized, and the substantial loss to follow-up limits interpretation. The study is reassuring about adaptation during demanding mixed training, but cannot establish that implants, injections, and progestin-only pills have identical effects. ([O’Leary et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000003588)).

## Do formulation and delivery method matter?

**Different hormonal exposures are real; a reliable ranking of their training effects is not.**

Combined pills supply an estrogen and a progestin while altering endogenous ovarian hormone production. Formulation, dose, regimen, and delivery method therefore matter when defining the exposure. A low endogenous estradiol measurement in a pill user does not describe the full hormonal environment, because synthetic hormones are also present. ([Holm et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.70189); [Elliott-Sale et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01435-8)).

Evidence that these differences translate into predictable training advantages is much weaker. In a ten-week study, a post hoc comparison suggested greater muscle growth among users of pills containing 30 μg rather than 20 μg of ethinyl estradiol. Each dose subgroup contained only seven women, dose was not randomized, and the direct 30 μg versus 20 μg comparison did not meet the conventional p < 0.05 threshold (13.1% versus 8.5%; p=0.08). This is hypothesis-generating evidence, not a basis for selecting a higher-estrogen pill for hypertrophy. ([Dalgaard et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2019.00824)).

Route also prevents simple generalization. Some users of levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine systems retain cyclical ovarian hormone profiles, unlike the intended suppression associated with combined pills. Methodological guidance therefore recommends characterizing the actual contraceptive method and hormonal context rather than assuming that every hormonal contraceptive user is physiologically equivalent. ([Elliott-Sale et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-026-02442-3)).

The defensible conclusion is that pooling formulations may obscure differences, but it does not prove that important differences exist. Current findings do not establish a “best pill for strength,” a superior progestin for endurance, or equivalent adaptation effects across pills, implants, and IUDs. ([Nolan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01911-3); [Nolan et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1123/wspaj.2025-0137); [O’Leary et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000003588); [Elliott-Sale et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-026-02442-3)).

## Starting, stopping, and switching contraception

A small older trial more directly tested contraceptive initiation. In a double-blind, placebo-controlled study with 14 women included in the analysis, approximately two months of a triphasic combined pill reduced relative VO₂max by about 4.7%, compared with a 1.5% increase with placebo. Strength and other performance outcomes did not show corresponding differences. ([Lebrun et al., 2003](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsm.37.4.315)).

This is relevant evidence that starting a particular formulation can affect aerobic capacity. However, it was not a standardized training-adaptation trial, and one small study of one triphasic formulation cannot be generalized to every modern contraceptive. ([Lebrun et al., 2003](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsm.37.4.315)).

Conversely, a within-person study of 17 recreationally active women found no significant differences in knee-extension strength, handgrip strength, or hop performance when the same women were assessed during pill use and nonuse. It was a nonrandomized crossover study, not a long-term training trial. ([Ekenros et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1097/JSM.0b013e3182625a51)).

Together, these studies do not establish that stopping contraception improves training gains, or predict what will happen when someone switches from a pill to an IUD or implant. **Evidence about initiating a pill is not interchangeable with evidence about switching formulations or methods.** ([Lebrun et al., 2003](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsm.37.4.315); [Ekenros et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1097/JSM.0b013e3182625a51)).

## What muscle, recovery, and tendon studies show

### Muscle protein turnover

A 2025 randomized-order crossover study assessed 12 women taking the same second-generation monophasic pill. Muscle protein synthesis and myofibrillar protein breakdown were measured during active-pill and inactive-pill phases, including responses to resistance exercise. Exercise increased synthesis, but the pill phases did not differ significantly. ([Colenso-Semple et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00035.2025)).

This argues against a large phase-specific difference in the measured anabolic response under those conditions. But the randomized element was **phase order**, not assignment to contraceptive use versus nonuse. It therefore cannot establish that pills and natural cycles produce identical long-term hypertrophy. ([Colenso-Semple et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00035.2025)).

### Recovery after repeated training

A study of 40 trained women compared second-generation pill users with nonusers after three strenuous resistance-exercise sessions. Pill users showed greater reductions in one measure of knee-extension strength, whereas several other strength, power, and muscle-damage outcomes did not differ between groups. ([Oxfeldt et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000003316)).

The appropriate interpretation is a possible outcome-specific recovery difference, not proof of universally impaired recovery and not proof of identical recovery. Whether such differences accumulate into different training adaptations requires longitudinal testing. ([Oxfeldt et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000003316)).

### Tendon and connective tissue

A tracer study comparing 11 habitual pill users with 12 nonusers found lower tendon collagen synthesis in pill users, alongside differences in IGF-related measures. That provides a plausible biological signal, but contraceptive use was not randomized and the outcome was collagen turnover, not injury incidence or long-term tendon function. ([Hansen et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.90933.2008)).

In contrast, the ten-week resistance-training study by Dalgaard and colleagues found increases in patellar tendon cross-sectional area without a significant difference between contraceptive groups. A short-term difference in collagen synthesis therefore should not be presented as established evidence of poorer tendon adaptation or greater injury risk. ([Dalgaard et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2019.00824)).

## How contraception affects menstrual-cycle research

The methodological concern is legitimate, but saying the field has not addressed it is too strong. Detailed guidance was published in 2021 and expanded for applied research in elite athletes in 2026. These are methodological recommendations, not experimental proof of a performance effect. ([Elliott-Sale et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01435-8); [Elliott-Sale et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-026-02442-3)).

A withdrawal bleed during a pill-free interval is not equivalent to menstruation following an ovulatory natural cycle. Similarly, the inactive-pill phase should not simply be relabelled the natural early follicular phase. Research needs to identify formulation, dose, regimen, duration of use, and relevant testing phase, while characterizing ovarian hormone status according to the research question. ([Elliott-Sale et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01435-8)).

Contraceptive use is therefore not automatically an uncontrolled confounder. It can be the exposure being studied, a prespecified subgroup, or a reason to define separate analytical groups. Problems arise when researchers pool unlike exposures, misclassify hormonal states, or interpret results from one reproductive group as applying to another. ([Elliott-Sale et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01435-8)).

## How the four competing claims hold up

**1. “Hormonal contraceptives slightly blunt training adaptations.”**

This is not supported as a general statement. Strength and hypertrophy studies point in different directions, with no consistent pooled impairment. Endurance evidence contains a concerning physiological signal, but does not establish a universal reduction in performance gains. ([Nolan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01911-3); [Gilmer et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00146.2026); [Nolan et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1123/wspaj.2025-0137); [Schaumberg et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000001171)).

**2. “Any effect is too small to matter outside elite sport.”**

This is a reasonable practical position, but stronger than the direct evidence permits. Current systematic reviews do not establish a dependable effect size across contraceptive methods, and the reviewed trials were not designed to quantify contraceptive effects relative to sleep, nutrition, and training quality. Prioritizing those fundamentals over speculative contraceptive optimization is a practical inference, not a comparison these trials directly tested. ([Nolan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01911-3); [Nolan et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1123/wspaj.2025-0137); [Elliott-Sale et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-026-02442-3)).

**3. “Different formulations produce different adaptation effects.”**

Biologically plausible and supported by some exploratory findings, but not established well enough to rank formulations. Small, nonrandomized subgroups cannot reliably separate dose or progestin effects from differences between the women using them. ([Dalgaard et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2019.00824)).

**4. “Contraception confounds cycle-phase research, and the field has not addressed it.”**

Correct about the need to distinguish hormonal exposures; overstated about neglect. Published guidance explicitly addresses the problem. Whether an individual study followed that guidance remains a separate question. ([Elliott-Sale et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01435-8)).

## Practical interpretation

Current evidence does not provide an established training-adaptation rationale for routinely starting, stopping, or switching an otherwise suitable oral contraceptive solely to improve muscle growth or strength. ([Nolan et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01911-3); [Gilmer et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00146.2026)). The performance literature instead supports an individualized approach that considers the reason for use and the person’s experience. Contraceptive decisions belong in a broader clinical discussion, not a training-only calculation. ([Elliott-Sale et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-020-01317-5)).

A useful distinction is between **a reproducible personal problem** and a theoretical hormonal disadvantage. Tracking symptoms, training completion, and objective performance around a clinician-guided change may help assess the former. Current research does not justify assuming the latter merely because a contraceptive changes blood hormone concentrations. This is a practical application of individualized assessment, rather than a validated switching protocol. ([Elliott-Sale et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-026-02442-3)).

## Funding and conflict-of-interest considerations

The [2024 resistance-training meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01911-3) reported Irish Research Council support, while the [military cohort](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000003588) reported UK Ministry of Defence funding. The [Engstad](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.70052) and [Holm](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.70189) studies reported no specific funding. The available disclosure statements did not identify contraceptive-manufacturer funding for these studies. That does not eliminate design limitations or prove the absence of undisclosed interests.

The [2025 protein-turnover study](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00035.2025) received Canadian public research funding. However, Stuart Phillips disclosed nutrition-industry financial relationships, including with Roquette and Nestlé Health Sciences, and Exerkine-related patents. These are relevant transparency disclosures, although they are not evidence that a contraceptive manufacturer funded the experiment. [Newcastle University ePrints](https://eprints.ncl.ac.uk/304829)

The [2026 methodological guidance](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-026-02442-3) disclosed Richard Burden’s former paid consultancy with Mint Diagnostics. It reported no funding for the article. Funding and conflict statements were not verified for every paper discussed here; an unverified disclosure should not be interpreted as an absence of conflicts.
